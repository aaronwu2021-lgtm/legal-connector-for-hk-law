### I.3 Legal framework applied

The governing-law framework is taken from a structured element library for the arbitration law of the HK/SG/EN Model Law family. Two modules are engaged:

- **Module NYC — Enforcement, New York Convention Article V.** Structural type: **disjunctive gateway**. The grounds for refusing recognition and enforcement form a **closed list**, and **any single ground, standing alone, is sufficient** to permit refusal. Stage NY-1 (burden on the party resisting enforcement): (i) incapacity / invalid agreement; (ii) improper notice / unable to present case; (iii) beyond the scope of the submission; (iv) composition of the tribunal or arbitral procedure not in accordance; (v) award not yet binding, set aside or suspended. Stage NY-2 (the court may raise these of its own motion): (vi) non-arbitrability; (vii) contrary to public policy. Stage NY-3 is a **threshold-discretion** stage: even where a ground is made out, the enforcing court **retains a discretion to enforce nonetheless**. Authorities in the library: New York Convention 1958, Art. V; *Hebei Import & Export v Polytek Engineering* (1999) 2 HKCFAR 111 (CFA) on the public-policy standard.
- **Module ARBCH — Arbitrator challenge.** Structural type: **balancing**, not conjunctive. The test (UNCITRAL Model Law art. 12) is whether an objective, informed observer would consider there to be a real possibility of doubt as to impartiality. No single factor is dispositive; the library weights them: *failure to disclose* and *financial interest* are **heavy**; *overlapping appointments* and *relationship with a party or counsel* are **substantial**; *previously expressed views* is **moderate**; *custom of the particular field* is **light** and typically operates to **weaken**, not strengthen, a challenge. Authorities: Model Law art. 12; *Halliburton v Chubb* [2020] UKSC 48 (multiple appointments and the duty of disclosure).

**Two cautions on the library.** First, every authority in it is flagged `unverified`; none should be cited in a filing before it is pulled and checked. Second, the library is tagged HK/SG/EN. The seat here is **Singapore**, and the procedural law is the Singapore International Arbitration Act (Cap. 143A) [Park Memo § 2.2]. *Hebei* and the Arbitration Ordinance (Cap. 609) are **Hong Kong** materials and are comparative and persuasive only; the Model Law standard and the Convention text are instruments of general application and travel directly.

### I.4 Why the enforcement architecture drives the severity ratings

Because Article V is a **closed disjunctive list**, the deviations below do not need to be aggregated to matter. A single deviation that maps onto a single enumerated ground is enough to expose the award. That structure raises the severity of several items that would otherwise read as procedural housekeeping — most importantly the 90-day award deadline (Issue 3) and the appointment mechanics (Issue 6), each of which is a self-contained Article V(1)(d) exposure. The residual discretion at stage NY-3 is the counterweight that keeps these ratings honest: a made-out ground permits, but does not compel, refusal, and Greenleaf should not assume any single defect is fatal.

The practical significance is not symmetrical. **Greenleaf is the anticipated award creditor.** Its claim is $11.9m against Kuiper's $1.4m counterclaim [Park Memo § 3]. Kuiper is a German GmbH with its registered office in Munich [MSA, Recitals], so on the record an award will most likely need to be enforced against assets in Germany. Every Article V ground catalogued below is therefore a weapon that will principally be available **to Kuiper, against Greenleaf's own award**. This inverts the usual instinct: Greenleaf's interest lies in a *conservative, consented, well-documented* procedure, not in extracting every procedural advantage the Clause's literal terms might yield.

## II. How the Clause and the Rules Interact

Before the issue-by-issue analysis, three structural provisions govern everything that follows.

**II.1 Party agreement prevails, but subject to three exceptions.** By agreeing to arbitrate under the Rules the parties agree to be bound by them "subject to any modifications that the parties may agree upon, provided such modifications are not inconsistent with any mandatory provision of these Rules or any mandatory provision of the law of the seat" [SAI Rules art. 1.2]. Article 1.4 states the conflict rule: where the Rules and the parties' agreement conflict, "the agreement of the parties shall prevail, except where: (a) such agreement would contravene a mandatory provision of these Rules; (b) such agreement would contravene the law of the seat of the arbitration; or (c) the SAI Court determines that giving effect to such agreement would render the arbitration impracticable."

**II.2 The Rules never identify which of their provisions are mandatory.** This is a structural gap in the Rules themselves, and it is the doctrinal hinge for Issues 3, 5, 6, 8, 10 and 11. Every one of the Clause's departures from a Rules default is exposed to an argument that the departed-from provision was mandatory, and neither party can resolve that argument from the text. Article 1.4(c) compounds it by giving the SAI Court a free-standing impracticability override that requires no finding of mandatoriness at all. The consequence for strategy: **a modification the parties have re-confirmed in writing at the outset is worth far more than a modification that rests only on the 2023 clause text**, because a re-confirmed modification takes the dispute out of Article 1.4 altogether.

**II.3 Objections are waived if not raised promptly.** A party that knows of a departure from the Rules or the arbitration agreement and proceeds without promptly objecting "shall be deemed to have waived its right to object" [SAI Rules art. 30.1]. An objection is prompt only if raised "at the earliest opportunity and in any event no later than the next written submission or hearing" following awareness [art. 30.2]. This cuts both ways and is operationally the single most important provision in the Rules for Greenleaf's team. It is addressed as a cross-cutting matter at Section VII below.

### ISSUE 1 — The escalation procedure is a condition precedent and is not yet satisfied; the triggering notice may also be defective

**Severity: CRITICAL** — Risk direction: adverse to Greenleaf. Action required *before* filing.

**Clause wording.** Section 14.2 opens: "**Subject to Section 14.3,** any dispute arising under or in connection with this Agreement shall be resolved exclusively by binding arbitration as follows" [MSA § 14.2]. Section 14.3 then provides: "(a) Before commencing arbitration under Section 14.2, a Party asserting a claim shall first deliver written notice of the dispute to the other Party **in accordance with Section 15.3** (the 'Dispute Notice'). The Dispute Notice shall describe in reasonable detail the nature of the dispute **and the relief sought**. (b) Within thirty (30) calendar days following delivery of the Dispute Notice, senior representatives of each Party (**at the level of Vice President or above**) shall meet (in person or by videoconference) and attempt in good faith to resolve the dispute through direct negotiation. (c) If the dispute is not resolved **within sixty (60) calendar days following delivery of the Dispute Notice**, either Party may commence arbitration pursuant to Section 14.2" [MSA § 14.3].

Section 15.3 sets the delivery standard: notices relating to dispute resolution under Section 14 "shall be sent to the Parties at their respective addresses set forth in Section 15.2 and to their respective General Counsel (or Head of Legal, as applicable) **by both email and internationally recognized courier service**. A Notice relating to dispute resolution under Section 14 **shall be effective only if delivered in accordance with this Section 15.3**" [MSA § 15.3].

**Rule engaged.** The Rules do not override contractual preconditions to arbitration. Article 1.4 gives the parties' agreement priority, and Article 4.1 fixes commencement at the Secretariat's receipt of the Request — the Rules supply no grace for an unsatisfied precondition. Article 30 then makes any failure to object promptly a waiver, which is how Kuiper's objection will be preserved or lost.

**Analysis.**

*The 60-day clock.* Greenleaf issued a formal notice of breach on **12 September 2024**; Kuiper responded on **3 October 2024** denying breach and asserting the $1.4m counterclaim [Park Memo § 2.3; Vogel Email, opening paragraph]. Sixty calendar days from 12 September 2024 expires on **11 November 2024**. As at the reference date of 4 November 2024, the escalation period has **seven days left to run**. A Request for Arbitration filed before 11 November 2024 would be filed in the teeth of an express contractual precondition.

*Whether the 12 September notice even started the clock.* Two defects are possible on this record and neither can be resolved from the documents provided:

1. **Content.** Section 14.3(a) requires the Dispute Notice to state "the relief sought." The record describes the 12 September document only as a "formal notice of breach" [Park Memo § 2.3]. Whether it stated the relief sought is not established.
2. **Delivery.** Section 15.3 requires delivery **by both email and courier** to the addresses in Section 15.2, and makes compliance a condition of effectiveness. Whether the 12 September notice went by courier as well as email is not established on this record.

If the notice was email-only, then on the plain words of Section 15.3 it was never "effective," the 60-day clock has **never started**, and the earliest permissible commencement date is 60 days after a compliant notice is served.

*A related address point.* Section 15.2 records Kuiper's Head of Legal at `k.vogel@kuiperdata.de` and Greenleaf's General Counsel at `myuen@greenleafbio.com` [MSA § 15.2]. The 28 October email is sent from `k.vogel@kuiper-data.de` and addressed to `m.yuen@greenleafbio.com` [Vogel Email, header]. Both differ by one character from the contractual addresses. This may be a transcription artefact in the file, but it must be checked: Section 15.2's final sentence permits an address change only "by giving written notice to the other Party," and there is no evidence in the record of any such change. Any Dispute Notice should be served at the **Section 15.2 addresses** and, belt and braces, at the addresses used in the 28 October email.

*The senior-representative meeting.* There is no evidence in the record that VP-or-above representatives of the two parties met within 30 days of 12 September 2024, as Section 14.3(b) requires. Kuiper's 3 October letter was a written denial, not a meeting.

**Legal consequence.** Two distinct exposures.

*Immediate:* Kuiper obtains a clean, cheap procedural objection to the Request. On the record it will take it — its 28 October email closes by asking for "reasonable advance notice so that the parties may first explore whether a more efficient resolution is possible" [Vogel Email, final paragraph], which reads as the groundwork for exactly this argument. A satellite dispute on admissibility consumes weeks and, given the Clause's 90-day award deadline (Issue 3), those are weeks the timetable cannot spare.

*Downstream:* under the connector's closed list, non-compliance with an agreed pre-arbitration procedure is capable of engaging **Article V(1)(d)** — "composition of the arbitral authority or the arbitral procedure was not in accordance with the agreement of the parties." I should be candid about the strength of this. The better and more modern view is that compliance with a multi-tier escalation clause goes to **admissibility** (a matter for the tribunal, and not reviewable at the enforcement stage) rather than to **jurisdiction**. On that view the Article V(1)(d) exposure is thin. But the point does not need to be strong to be decisive here, because **waiting costs Greenleaf almost nothing**: Section 14.3(d) expressly preserves emergency and interim relief notwithstanding the escalation procedure (see Issue 2), so the one genuinely urgent item on the file is not held up by waiting.

**Severity rationale.** Rated Critical not because the legal risk is the largest on this list, but because it is the **first** thing capable of derailing the filing, it is entirely within Greenleaf's control, and the cost of curing it is a few days.

**Recommended fix.**

1. **Do not file before 11 November 2024.** There is no upside to filing on 10 November that survives contact with an admissibility objection.
2. **Confirm from Greenleaf's files** (a) whether the 12 September notice stated the relief sought and (b) whether it was couriered as well as emailed, with the courier confirmation of receipt. Obtain the tracking record.
3. **If either limb fails, re-serve now.** Serve a document expressly captioned "Dispute Notice under Section 14.3(a)" describing the dispute and the relief sought, by email **and** international courier, to Dr. Vogel as Head of Legal at the Section 15.2 address, copied to Feldhammer Grau as Kuiper requested [Vogel Email, opening paragraph]. Accept that this restarts the 60-day clock; the certainty is worth it.
4. **Offer the Section 14.3(b) meeting in writing**, at VP-or-above level, with proposed dates. Whether or not Kuiper attends, the written offer answers the good-faith limb and converts a Greenleaf vulnerability into a Kuiper one.
5. **Recite compliance in the Request for Arbitration**, attaching the Dispute Notice, the courier receipt, the meeting invitation and any response. Article 4.2(c) requires a description of the circumstances giving rise to the claims; that is the natural place for it.

### ISSUE 2 — Emergency arbitrator: the Clause's silence is a gap that resolves in Greenleaf's favour, but the preservation window is closing or closed

**Severity: CRITICAL** — Risk direction: legal analysis favourable; timing critical.

**Clause wording.** The Clause is **silent** on the emergency arbitrator procedure. It contains no opt-out, and no reference to Article 15bis or Appendix II [MSA § 14.2]. Section 14.3(d) preserves the point expressly: "nothing in this Section 14.3 shall prevent a Party from seeking emergency or interim relief pursuant to the arbitration rules referenced in Section 14.2 **or from a court of competent jurisdiction** where such relief is necessary to prevent irreparable harm" [MSA § 14.3(d)].

**Rule engaged.** Article 15bis.4: "The emergency arbitrator procedure shall apply to all arbitrations commenced under these Rules **unless**: (a) the parties have agreed to opt out of the emergency arbitrator procedure; **or** (b) the arbitration agreement was concluded **before the effective date of the edition of the Rules first introducing the emergency arbitrator procedure**." Appendix II, EA-7: "These Emergency Arbitrator Rules apply to all arbitrations under the SAI Rules (2022 Edition) unless the parties have expressly opted out in their arbitration agreement." Article 15.4 separately preserves the right to seek interim measures from a competent judicial authority and provides that doing so is not a waiver of the agreement to arbitrate.

**Analysis — availability is confirmed, and on firmer ground than the assignment memorandum suggests.**

The Park Memo concludes that "since the clause is silent, the emergency arbitrator mechanism should remain available" and asks for confirmation [Park Memo § 5]. That conclusion is correct, but the reason matters, because Article 15bis.4(b) is a trap the memorandum does not address and which a respondent's counsel would be expected to find.

Limb (a) is not engaged: the Clause contains no opt-out, express or implied.

Limb (b) **cannot be engaged on these facts, on any hypothesis**:

- The 2022 Edition took effect on **1 January 2022** [SAI Rules, cover page and art. 35.1] and **contains** the emergency arbitrator procedure at Article 15bis and Appendix II.
- Therefore the edition that first introduced the emergency arbitrator procedure is, **at the latest**, the 2022 Edition, with an effective date of 1 January 2022. If some earlier edition introduced it, that edition's effective date is earlier still.
- The MSA was concluded on **15 March 2023** [MSA, Recitals].
- 15 March 2023 postdates 1 January 2022, and postdates any earlier date *a fortiori*. Limb (b) requires the arbitration agreement to have been concluded **before** the relevant effective date. It was not.

Limb (b) could only bite if the emergency arbitrator procedure had first appeared in an edition effective **after** 15 March 2023 — which is excluded by the fact that the 2022 Edition already contains it. **The emergency arbitrator procedure is available to Greenleaf.** EA-7 reinforces this, since its own carve-out is limited to express opt-out and contains no vintage exception at all. (The internal mismatch between Article 15bis.4(b) and EA-7 is worth noting for completeness, but it does not affect the outcome: both routes lead to availability.)

One residual dependency: this analysis is under the 2022 Edition. If the SAI were to administer under the 2024 Edition (Issue 12), the emergency arbitrator provisions of that edition are not in the record and must be re-checked.

**Analysis — the timing is worse than the assignment memorandum states.**

Palladian Forensic Technologies advises that Kuiper's server logs documenting the corruption events and outages are subject to retention policies producing automated deletion "within 90 to 120 days from the date of the events unless a litigation hold is implemented by Kuiper," and the relevant events ran from **June through August 2024** [Park Memo §§ 2.3, 5]. Applying Palladian's own estimate to the record dates:

| Event period | 90 days from | 120 days from | Status at 4 Nov 2024 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early June 2024 | ~early Sept 2024 | ~early Oct 2024 | **Window already passed** |
| Late June 2024 | ~late Sept 2024 | ~late Oct 2024 | **Window already passed** |
| July 2024 | ~Oct 2024 | ~Nov 2024 | **Closing now** |
| August 2024 | ~late Nov 2024 | ~late Dec 2024 | Open, weeks remaining |

The memorandum's characterisation that "the deletion window may be imminent" [Park Memo § 5] understates the position. On Palladian's own estimate, the logs for the **earliest** events are already outside the stated window, and the July logs are inside it now. Only the August tranche has meaningful runway.

**Legal consequence.** Loss of Kuiper's server logs would remove the primary contemporaneous evidence that the corruption originated on Kuiper's servers — the proposition Palladian's preliminary findings support and on which Greenleaf's $8.7m direct claim depends [Park Memo §§ 2.3, 3]. Under Article 14.2 of the Rules "[e]ach party shall have the burden of proving the facts relied on to support its claim," so the evidential consequence falls squarely on Greenleaf. Note also that loss of the logs is not automatically fatal: if logs were destroyed after Kuiper was on notice of the dispute (Greenleaf's 12 September notice at the latest), a spoliation and adverse-inference position becomes available before a tribunal with broad evidentiary discretion under Articles 18.2 and 18.4.

**Recommended fix — sequenced, and to be started today.**

1. **Serve a written preservation demand on Kuiper immediately**, before and independent of any tribunal or court application. It costs nothing, requires no institution, and — critically — fixes the date of Kuiper's actual notice for any later spoliation argument. Address it to Dr. Vogel and copy Feldhammer Grau. Identify the log categories and date ranges specifically; a generic demand is worth little.
2. **File the Article 15bis / Appendix II emergency arbitrator application** in parallel. It is available under Section 14.3(d) notwithstanding the unexpired escalation period (Issue 1), so it need not wait until 11 November. EA-1 requires: description of circumstances and measures sought; reasons emergency relief is needed; reasons irreparable harm will follow absent relief before the tribunal is constituted; statement of relief sought; a copy of the arbitration agreement; and proof of payment. The fee is **CHF 20,000, non-refundable**, subject to a discretionary partial refund only if the application is withdrawn before appointment [App. II, EA-2]. Budget it as a sunk cost — see Issue 10, under which Greenleaf will not recover it.
3. **Rely on the timetable, which is the fastest route available.** SAI Court appoints the emergency arbitrator within **2 business days** of the application and fee [art. 15bis.2; EA-3]; the emergency arbitrator sets a schedule within **2 business days** of appointment [EA-4]; a reasoned decision follows within **15 calendar days** of appointment [EA-5]. The decision binds on issuance and survives until varied by the tribunal [art. 15bis.3].
4. **Consider parallel court relief.** Article 15.4 and Section 14.3(d) both preserve it, and Article 15.4 provides expressly that seeking it is not a waiver of the agreement to arbitrate. The Singapore High Court (as the court of the seat) is the natural forum for a preservation order; 28 U.S.C. § 1782 relief is a distinct and slower mechanism directed at obtaining evidence for use in a foreign proceeding rather than at preservation, and is unlikely to move faster than EA-5's 15-day track [Park Memo § 5].
5. **Preserve the spoliation position** in the preservation demand and in the EA application, by putting on record what has been lost, when Kuiper was on notice, and what inference should follow.

### ISSUE 3 — The 90-day award deadline conflicts with Article 24 on both length and trigger, and strips out the Rules' own saving provision

**Severity: CRITICAL** — Risk direction: adverse to Greenleaf.

**Clause wording.** "The arbitrator **shall** render a final award within **90 calendar days of the constitution of the tribunal**" [MSA § 14.2].

**Rule deviated from.** Article 24.1: "The Tribunal shall **use its best efforts** to render the final award within **six months (180 calendar days)** of the **last substantive hearing or the filing of the last authorized written submission, whichever is later**." Article 24.2: the SAI Court may extend on reasoned request or of its own initiative. Article 24.3: "The time limit under Article 24.1 is a **target** intended to promote efficiency. **Failure to render the award within the time limit shall not render the award invalid** or affect the Tribunal's authority to render the award." Article 24.4: "For the avoidance of doubt, the time limit under this Article runs from the last substantive hearing or last authorized submission, **not from the constitution of the Tribunal**. The period between the constitution of the Tribunal and the commencement of substantive proceedings is **not included** in the calculation."

**Analysis.** This is a triple deviation, and the third element is the dangerous one.

*Length.* 90 days against 180 days — the Clause halves the period.

*Trigger.* The Clause runs from **constitution**; Article 24.4 runs from the **last hearing or submission** and expressly excludes the period between constitution and the start of substantive proceedings. Article 24.4's "for the avoidance of doubt" language reads as though drafted against precisely this species of clause. The two triggers are not merely different in length; the Clause's 90 days must absorb the entire proceeding, whereas Article 24's 180 days begins only once the proceeding is over. The effective compression is far greater than 90 against 180.

*Character.* Article 24.1 imposes a **best-efforts target** with an express saving in Article 24.3 that breach does not invalidate the award. The Clause says the arbitrator "**shall** render" — mandatory language — **and carries no equivalent saving provision**. Under Article 1.4 the parties' agreement prevails over the Rules, so the argument is available to Kuiper that the parties have contracted out of the Article 24.3 saving and created a hard deadline. That is the deviation that converts a scheduling problem into an enforceability problem.

*Arithmetic.* The Rules' own default steps do not fit inside 90 days from constitution. Article 4.4: the Respondent has 30 days from receipt of the Request to file an Answer. Article 9.2: the tribunal has 21 days from receipt of the file to establish a provisional timetable. Article 13.2: a case management conference must be held no later than the date for establishing that timetable. Article 14.1: there must be at least one round of written submissions by each party unless otherwise agreed. Article 16.1: the tribunal must hold a hearing if any party requests one. Article 21.1: proceedings close only when the parties have had a reasonable opportunity to present their cases. Fitting a $13.3m technical dispute — with forensic expert evidence, document production, and a dual-language regime requiring translation (Issue 7) — through those steps and to a reasoned award inside 90 days is not realistic.

*Arbitrator recruitment.* The Park Memo asks whether a qualified arbitrator may decline appointment on account of this constraint [Park Memo § 4(d)]. The concern is well founded, and it compounds Issue 13: the intersection of (i) cloud-computing and data-management expertise, (ii) life-sciences regulatory familiarity, (iii) English-and-German capability, and (iv) willingness to accept a hard 90-day award deadline describes a very small pool. Kuiper's own counsel effectively concedes the difficulty of limbs (i) and (ii) [Vogel Email, Arbitrator Appointment Timeline]. Adding (iii) and (iv) may make the appointment unfillable on the Clause's terms — which in turn feeds Article 1.4(c) impracticability.

**Legal consequence.** Mapping to the closed Article V list, the danger is that **both** possible outcomes generate a ground for Kuiper:

- If the award is rendered **late** (day 150, say), Kuiper argues the arbitral procedure "was not in accordance with the agreement of the parties" — **Article V(1)(d)**. The Clause's mandatory "shall," unaccompanied by an Article 24.3 saving, is the foundation of that argument.
- If the tribunal **races** to meet day 90 by curtailing submissions, refusing a hearing, or truncating expert evidence, Kuiper argues it was "unable to present its case" — **Article V(1)(b)**.

Because the list is disjunctive, Kuiper needs only one. Both are available on the current clause text. There is a further layer: the same defects are, in substance, grounds for setting aside at the Singapore seat under the IAA [Park Memo § 2.2], and an award set aside at the seat engages **Article V(1)(e)** in any downstream enforcement forum. The residual discretion at stage NY-3 remains — an enforcing court may enforce despite a made-out ground, and a purely technical timetabling overrun with no prejudice is a strong candidate for that discretion — but Greenleaf should not be planning to rely on the discretion of a German enforcement court.

**Recommended fix.**

1. **Seek a written, bilateral variation before filing.** Propose to Kuiper that the parties jointly confirm that the 90-day period is a target in the sense of Article 24.3, and that the award timetable will be fixed by the tribunal under Article 9.2 in consultation with the parties. Frame it to Kuiper as mutual protection — the risk of a hard deadline is symmetrical, and Kuiper is the party who will need time to prepare a defence and prosecute its counterclaim.
2. **Failing agreement, put it before the tribunal at the first opportunity.** Raise it at the Article 13.2 case management conference and seek a procedural order recording the parties' positions and the tribunal's timetable, and, if Kuiper consents at that point, recording the variation in the Terms of Reference under Article 13.1.
3. **Failing both, create the Article 30 record.** If Kuiper participates in a timetable that cannot deliver an award by day 90 and does not object promptly, Greenleaf should say so on the record at the time — that is the material for an Article 30.1 waiver argument later, and it is worthless if not created contemporaneously.
4. **Keep the SAI Court's Article 24.2 extension power in reserve** as the institutional route, and note that Article 1.4(c) impracticability is a double-edged instrument: it may relieve the timetable, but it is also the mechanism by which the SAI Court could disapply other parts of the Clause.

### ISSUE 4 — The exclusion of consequential damages is a valid limitation on the arbitrator's authority and puts $3.2m of Greenleaf's own claim at risk

**Severity: CRITICAL** — Risk direction: adverse to Greenleaf. This is the largest quantified exposure on the file.

**Clause wording.** "The arbitrator shall not have the authority to award **punitive, exemplary, or consequential** damages" [MSA § 14.2].

**Rules engaged.** Article 34.1: the tribunal may award "damages, whether compensatory, **consequential**, or otherwise available under the applicable law governing the substance of the dispute." Article 34.2: "The Tribunal shall not award punitive or exemplary damages unless expressly authorized by the applicable law governing the substance of the dispute." Article 34.3: "Nothing in these Rules shall prevent the parties from agreeing to limit the remedies available to the Tribunal. **Any such agreement shall be given effect by the Tribunal**, subject to the mandatory provisions of the applicable law."

**Analysis.**

*The punitive and exemplary limb is a non-deviation.* Article 34.2 already prohibits punitive and exemplary awards absent express authorisation by the governing substantive law. The Clause's exclusion of them is duplicative and harmless. No action required.

*The consequential limb is not a deviation from the Rules either — and that is the problem.* Article 34.3 expressly authorises parties to limit the tribunal's remedial powers and directs that such agreements "shall be given effect." There is therefore no rules-based route to disapplying this exclusion. It binds. The exposure is self-inflicted rather than institutional, and Greenleaf should not spend effort looking for a Rules argument that does not exist.

*Quantum.* Greenleaf's claim is $8.7m in direct damages (data reconstruction, delayed trial timelines, FDA re-submissions) plus **$3.2m characterised as consequential** (lost productivity, third-party vendor penalties, delayed commercial milestones) [Park Memo § 3]. The exclusion therefore threatens approximately **27% of Greenleaf's claim**. The MSA's $15m liability cap [MSA § 12.1] is not the operative constraint; the remedial exclusion is.

*A critical asymmetry the file has not flagged.* Greenleaf is subject to **two** overlapping consequential-damages exclusions, and they are drafted differently:

- **MSA § 12.2** excludes indirect, incidental, special and consequential damages **"EXCEPT FOR A PARTY'S OBLIGATIONS UNDER SECTION 13 (INDEMNIFICATION) AND EXCEPT IN CASES OF WILLFUL MISCONDUCT OR FRAUD."** Section 12.2 also carves out loss of data from the exclusion to the extent of Kuiper's restoration obligation under Section 7.4.
- **MSA § 14.2** removes the arbitrator's *authority* to award consequential damages **with no carve-out whatsoever** — no indemnification exception, no willful-misconduct exception, no fraud exception.

The consequence is significant and adverse. Even if Greenleaf successfully pleads willful misconduct and thereby escapes the Section 12.2 exclusion as a matter of substantive contract law, **Section 14.2 still purports to strip the arbitrator of the power to award the damages**. Section 12.2 is a limitation on liability; Section 14.2 is a limitation on the tribunal's jurisdiction over remedies. Escaping the first does not escape the second. Greenleaf's counsel should not assume the willful-misconduct route solves this.

*Section 12.2's data-loss carve-out is worth pursuing.* Section 12.2 excludes "LOSS OF DATA (**OTHER THAN THE SERVICE PROVIDER'S OBLIGATION TO RESTORE DATA UNDER SECTION 7.4**)". Section 7.4 is not in the excerpt provided, but on its face this preserves a restoration obligation that sits outside the exclusion entirely. Given that this dispute is centrally about corruption of three clinical trial databases [Park Memo § 2.3], the scope of Section 7.4 could be materially valuable and should be obtained and analysed as a priority.

**Legal consequence.** Two directions.

*Recovery risk (primary).* Kuiper will invoke Section 14.2 to defeat the $3.2m head. Under Article 34.3 the tribunal is directed to give effect to the limitation. The realistic prospect of recovering the $3.2m **as pleaded** is poor.

*Enforcement risk (secondary but serious).* If Greenleaf persuades a tribunal to award the $3.2m notwithstanding Section 14.2, that portion of the award is exposed under **Article V(1)(c)** — the award "deals with a difference not contemplated by or not falling within the terms of the submission to arbitration, or contains decisions on matters beyond the scope of the submission." Where the parties have contractually removed a remedy from the arbitrator's authority, an award granting it is the paradigm case. Article V(1)(c) permits **partial** refusal: the separable, unaffected portion of the award may still be enforced. So the practical worst case is that Greenleaf enforces $8.7m and loses $3.2m at the enforcement stage having paid to litigate it — the worst of both outcomes.

*Which law decides?* The Park Memo asks whether enforceability is assessed under New York substantive law or Singapore procedural law [Park Memo §§ 2.2, 7.3]. The better analysis is that it is **substantive**, and therefore New York law under Section 14.1 — Article 34.3 makes the limitation binding "subject to the mandatory provisions of the applicable law," and Article 23.1 directs the tribunal to the substantive law chosen, excluding conflicts rules. New York law is generally hospitable to negotiated consequential-damages exclusions between sophisticated commercial parties, and Section 12.3 records the parties' express acknowledgment that the Section 12 limitations "reflect a reasonable allocation of risk … and form an essential basis of the bargain" [MSA § 12.3] — language that exists precisely to defeat later unconscionability arguments. Greenleaf should assume the exclusion holds.

**Recommended fix.** The battle is over **characterisation, not enforceability**.

1. **Re-plead the $3.2m, head by head, as direct loss where the facts support it.** Of the three components identified [Park Memo § 3]: *third-party vendor penalties* actually incurred as a direct result of the 47-day outage are the strongest candidates for direct-loss treatment, being liquidated, documented and immediately consequent on the breach; *lost productivity* is more naturally framed as the cost of wasted staff time and substitute capacity actually expended, i.e. a quantifiable expenditure rather than a lost expectation; *delayed commercial milestones* is the weakest and reads squarely as lost profit. Do not over-reach: pleading a plainly consequential head as direct invites a jurisdictional objection that colours the tribunal's view of the rest.
2. **Consider mitigation-cost framing.** Costs necessarily incurred to mitigate the effects of the outages are expenditures, not lost expectations, and are ordinarily recoverable as direct loss.
3. **Obtain MSA § 7.4** and assess whether restoration-obligation losses can be pleaded through the Section 12.2 data-loss carve-out, independent of Section 14.2's remedial limitation.
4. **Do not plead any head as "consequential" in the Request for Arbitration.** The Article 4.2(d) preliminary statement of relief is the first document the tribunal reads; a self-characterisation as consequential will be quoted back at Greenleaf for the rest of the case. The Park Memo's own characterisation was made for internal analytical purposes and should not carry into the pleading [Park Memo § 3].
5. **Flag the risk to Marcus Yuen before filing.** Approximately 27% of the headline claim is at risk on a clause Greenleaf accepted "with only minimal revisions" during negotiation [Park Memo § 2.2]. That should be surfaced to the client explicitly, in writing, before the Request is filed and before the claim figure is put on the public record of the arbitration.

### ISSUE 5 — Sole arbitrator against $13.3m in controversy: the exposure runs through Article 6.5, not the Article 6.3 default

**Severity: HIGH** — Risk direction: adverse to Greenleaf.

**Clause wording.** "The arbitration shall be conducted by a **sole arbitrator** mutually agreed upon by the parties" [MSA § 14.2].

**Rules engaged.** Article 6.2: "Where the parties have agreed on the number of arbitrators, such agreement **shall be given effect, subject to Article 6.5**." Article 6.3: "**Where the parties have not agreed** on the number of arbitrators: (a) For disputes where the amount in controversy exceeds CHF 5,000,000 … the default shall be a tribunal of three arbitrators…". Article 6.5: "**Notwithstanding any agreement of the parties** regarding the number of arbitrators, the SAI Court may, **in exceptional circumstances and upon the reasoned request of a party**, determine that a different number of arbitrators is appropriate, having regard to the complexity of the dispute, the amount in controversy, and the interests of the parties." Article 5.3: a counterclaim is treated as a separate claim for determining the amount in controversy, "including for purposes of Article 6 (number of arbitrators)."

**Analysis — a correction to the framing in the assignment memorandum.** The Park Memo states that "under the SAI Rules, the default threshold for appointment of a three-member tribunal is CHF 5 million … Yet the arbitration clause mandates a sole arbitrator," and asks whether the SAI Court could challenge the sole-arbitrator designation [Park Memo § 3]. On a close reading of Article 6 there is **no conflict with Article 6.3 at all**. Article 6.3 is expressly conditioned on the parties *not* having agreed; here they have agreed, so Article 6.3 never engages and its CHF 5m threshold is irrelevant as a default. Article 6.2 then commands that the agreement "shall be given effect."

The exposure is narrower, and runs entirely through **Article 6.5**, which has **two cumulative predicates**:

1. **exceptional circumstances**; and
2. **the reasoned request of a party**.

Both are required. This has three practical consequences that the broader framing obscures:

- **The SAI Court cannot act of its own motion under Article 6.5.** Absent a party's reasoned request, the sole-arbitrator agreement stands. (Contrast Article 24.2, where the SAI Court is given an express own-initiative power — the drafters knew how to confer one and did not do so here.)
- **Kuiper is the only party who could trigger it**, and it has not signalled any intention to. Its 28 October email contests the *timeline* for appointing the sole arbitrator and does not contest the sole-arbitrator designation itself [Vogel Email, Arbitrator Appointment Timeline]. Its complaint that the arbitrator must combine cloud/data expertise with life-sciences regulatory expertise would, if anything, be the seed of an Article 6.5 argument — but it has not made it, and drawing attention to it would be counterproductive.
- **Amount in controversy alone is unlikely to be "exceptional."** The total is $13.3m ($11.9m claim plus $1.4m counterclaim, aggregated as Article 5.3 requires) [Park Memo § 3], against a threshold of CHF 5m ≈ $5.6m [Park Memo § 3]. That is roughly 2.4 times the threshold — a substantial but unremarkable multiple for a case sitting comfortably within Appendix I's CHF 5,000,001–20,000,000 band, which is a routine band, not an exceptional one.

**Legal consequence.** If the SAI Court were to override the parties' express sole-arbitrator agreement and constitute a three-member tribunal, the resulting award would be exposed under **Article V(1)(d)** — "the composition of the arbitral authority … was not in accordance with the agreement of the parties." This is the most textually direct fit of any ground on the closed list, and the burden of establishing it would sit with the party resisting enforcement, which on the anticipated facts is Kuiper.

The strategic point follows and is important: **if Greenleaf itself requests a three-member tribunal, it manufactures the very Article V(1)(d) argument that will be used against its own award.** Greenleaf might be tempted — a three-member tribunal is more robust for a $13.3m technical dispute, less exposed to a single arbitrator's idiosyncrasy, and less likely to be unavailable within the Clause's compressed timetable. Those advantages do not survive the enforcement analysis.

Cost reinforces the same conclusion. Under Appendix I, at this amount in controversy, indicative arbitrator fees are **CHF 50,000–200,000** for a sole arbitrator against **CHF 120,000–500,000** for a three-member tribunal. Because each party bears its own costs and institutional costs are shared (Issue 10), Greenleaf would fund roughly half of a two-to-three-fold increase and recover none of it.

**Recommended fix.**

1. **Do not seek a departure from the sole-arbitrator agreement**, and do not raise the amount-in-controversy point with the SAI Secretariat. The Article 4.2(e) observations in the Request for Arbitration should confirm a sole arbitrator, consistent with the Clause.
2. **Oppose any Article 6.5 request by Kuiper**, on the ground that both predicates are unmet: the SAI Court has no own-motion power, and an amount 2.4 times a routine threshold in a single-contract, two-party dispute is not "exceptional circumstances." Reserve the cost argument as a secondary point.
3. **If a three-member tribunal is nonetheless imposed, obtain Kuiper's recorded consent** to it in the Terms of Reference under Article 13.1. Consent recorded at the time is what closes off the Article V(1)(d) argument later; an unopposed imposition does not, absent a clear Article 30 waiver record.
4. **Direct the tribunal-composition anxiety into arbitrator quality instead** (see Issue 13). The robustness Greenleaf wants from three arbitrators is better obtained by securing the right sole arbitrator.

### ISSUE 6 — The 15-day appointment window shortens Article 6.4's 30 days, Kuiper has pre-announced non-compliance, and either path forward generates an Article V(1)(d) argument

**Severity: HIGH** — Risk direction: adverse to Greenleaf.

**Clause wording.** "The arbitration shall be conducted by a sole arbitrator mutually agreed upon by the parties **within 15 calendar days of the filing of the Request for Arbitration**" [MSA § 14.2].

**Rule deviated from.** Article 6.4: "Where the parties have agreed on a sole arbitrator, the parties shall endeavour to agree on the identity of the sole arbitrator **within 30 calendar days of the filing of the Request for Arbitration**. If the parties fail to agree within that period, the SAI Court shall appoint the sole arbitrator."

**Analysis.**

*The deviation is length only, not trigger.* Both the Clause and Article 6.4 run from **the filing of the Request for Arbitration**. The Clause halves the period; it does not change the starting point. That is a narrower deviation than it first appears and should be stated precisely in any correspondence, because Kuiper's email mischaracterises Article 6.4 as one that "affords the parties 30 calendar days" [Vogel Email, Arbitrator Appointment Timeline] — a default period for endeavouring to agree is not an entitlement.

*The Clause omits the fallback; the Rules supply it favourably.* The Clause says nothing about what happens if the parties fail to agree. Article 6.4's second sentence fills the gap: the SAI Court shall appoint. This gap-filling is neutral-to-favourable for Greenleaf, since it prevents Kuiper from stalling the constitution of the tribunal indefinitely.

*A practical defect in the 15-day trigger.* Under Article 4.3 the Secretariat sends the Request to the Respondent **only "upon receipt of the filing fee."** The Clause's 15-day clock runs from *filing*, not from Kuiper's receipt. Depending on fee processing, a material portion of the 15 days can elapse before Kuiper has the Request in hand — which materially strengthens Kuiper's impracticability argument and correspondingly weakens Greenleaf's ability to insist on day 16. Greenleaf can mitigate this partially by paying the Article 4.2(g) filing fee simultaneously with filing, and it should.

*Kuiper's announced position.* Kuiper states that it "will not consider itself bound by the 15-day contractual deadline and will instead rely on the full 30-day period," and that if Greenleaf invokes the SAI Court's appointment authority before day 30 it "will object" [Vogel Email, Arbitrator Appointment Timeline]. This is a pre-announced procedural dispute, which is unusual and, from Greenleaf's perspective, useful: it is on the record before filing and can be resolved before filing.

**Assessment of Kuiper's position: partly wrong on the law, partly right on the consequence.**

Kuiper is **wrong** that Article 6.4 displaces the Clause. Article 1.4 provides that in a conflict between the Rules and the parties' agreement, "the agreement of the parties shall prevail," subject only to the three exceptions. Kuiper does not identify Article 6.4 as mandatory, does not identify any contravention of Singapore seat law, and cannot itself invoke Article 1.4(c), which is a determination reserved to the SAI Court. On the text, 15 days governs.

Kuiper is **right** that its practical objection has force. Its stated grounds — that identifying a sole arbitrator with dual expertise in cloud computing/data management and life-sciences regulation, who is available, independent and conflict-free, requires due diligence that cannot be compressed into 15 days [Vogel Email] — are exactly the kind of grounds on which the SAI Court would entertain an Article 1.4(c) impracticability determination. And on the merits the objection is not unreasonable: the pool is genuinely narrow (see Issues 3 and 13).

**Legal consequence.** Under the closed Article V list, **both** available paths hand Kuiper a **V(1)(d)** argument on the composition of the tribunal:

- **Path A — Greenleaf insists on 15 days** and asks the SAI Court to appoint on day 16 over Kuiper's objection. Greenleaf has the better textual argument. But the resulting appointment is made over a recorded objection, and Kuiper will argue at enforcement that the composition was not in accordance with the applicable procedure. Greenleaf would be relying on its opponent's Article 30 failure to preserve, which is a thin foundation given Kuiper has *already* objected in advance.
- **Path B — Greenleaf acquiesces in 30 days.** Now the composition follows a period the parties contractually replaced, and Kuiper (or, less plausibly, Greenleaf) can argue the composition departed from the agreement. Greenleaf's acquiescence would likely be an Article 30 waiver on its own side, but does not bind Kuiper unless Kuiper's consent is recorded.

Both paths are curable by the same step, which is why this issue should be resolved before filing rather than litigated after.

**Recommended fix.**

1. **Agree a written extension with Kuiper before filing.** Propose a short, bilateral written agreement recording that, as a modification agreed under Article 1.2, the parties will endeavour to agree the sole arbitrator within 30 days of the filing of the Request, failing which the SAI Court shall appoint under Article 6.4. Kuiper has already stated it wants exactly this, so agreement should be obtainable at effectively no negotiating cost.
2. **Extract value from the exchange.** Use the same correspondence to resolve the language protocol (Issue 7), the award-timetable variation (Issue 3), and the arbitrator qualification profile (Issue 13). Kuiper wants something here; that is the moment to trade.
3. **Begin the arbitrator search now, before filing.** Nothing prevents the parties exchanging candidate names before the Request. Doing so is the only way 15 or even 30 days is realistically usable, and it removes the practical foundation of Kuiper's objection.
4. **Pay the Article 4.2(g) filing fee at the moment of filing**, so that Article 4.3 transmission is not delayed and the clock — whichever clock applies — is not eroded.
5. **If no agreement is reached, take Path A but document it.** Write to Kuiper on day 15 recording the failure to agree, expressly reserving Greenleaf's position, and invite Kuiper to consent to the SAI Court appointing. Then apply. The correspondence is the Article 30 record.

### ISSUE 7 — Dual language with no primary language and no award language: a gap the Rules fill, but expensively and against Greenleaf

**Severity: HIGH** — Risk direction: adverse to Greenleaf.

**Clause wording.** "The language of the arbitration shall be **English and German**, with **all submissions accepted in either language**" [MSA § 14.2].

**Rules engaged.** Article 12.1: the language or languages "shall be as agreed by the parties." Article 12.2: "Where the parties have designated more than one language, **the Tribunal shall determine procedures for the use of such languages**, including: (a) which language or languages shall be used for written submissions; (b) which language or languages shall be used for hearings; (c) **which language shall be used for the award**; and (d) whether translation of documents or interpretation at hearings shall be required, and **if so, at whose cost**." Article 12.4: the tribunal may order translations into the language(s) of the arbitration.

**Analysis.**

*This is a gap, not a conflict.* The Clause validly designates two languages under Article 12.1. It then fails to designate a primary language, fails to allocate languages between submissions, hearings and the award, and fails to address translation and its cost. Article 12.2 supplies the mechanism: **the tribunal decides all four**. So the gap is filled — but it is filled by a decision-maker who will not exist until the tribunal is constituted, which means Greenleaf must prepare the Request for Arbitration without knowing the answer.

*A material internal conflict within the MSA that the file has not identified.* MSA § 15.4 provides: "**All Notices shall be in the English language.** Where a Notice is accompanied by supporting documentation in a language other than English, the sending Party shall provide an **English translation** of such documentation, which translation need not be certified **unless required for use in legal or arbitral proceedings**" [MSA § 15.4]. Notices "relating to dispute resolution under Section 14" are expressly within the notice regime [MSA § 15.3]. So the parties, in the same instrument, made English the **exclusive** language of dispute-resolution notices and contemplated **certified** English translations of non-English material "for use in … arbitral proceedings." That is a strong contextual argument for English primacy in the arbitration itself, and it is the best textual card Greenleaf holds on this issue. It should be deployed in Article 4.2(e) observations and at the Article 13.2 conference.

*Kuiper's position.* Kuiper asserts that German is co-equal, that it will file **all** substantive submissions, witness statements and expert reports in German, that the MSA was negotiated in significant part in German, that its witnesses and technical personnel are German-speaking, and that **the award should be rendered in German** [Vogel Email, Language of the Arbitration].

**Assessment of Kuiper's position: partly right, partly overreaching.**

Kuiper is **right** that the Clause permits it to submit in German. "All submissions accepted in either language" is unambiguous and Article 12.1 gives it effect. Greenleaf cannot object to German-language submissions as such, and should not try.

Kuiper is **wrong** that the award should be in German, or that its preference resolves the question. Article 12.2(c) commits the language of the award to the **tribunal's** determination. Kuiper's own email in fact concedes this — "the tribunal will need to determine this question pursuant to Article 12" [Vogel Email] — while simultaneously asserting the outcome. On the merits of that determination, the factors favour English: the MSA is written in English; New York law governs the substance [MSA § 14.1]; § 15.4 makes English the exclusive language for dispute notices; and the seat is Singapore, an English-language jurisdiction whose courts would supervise any set-aside application.

**Legal consequence.**

*Cost, borne asymmetrically.* If Kuiper files everything in German, Greenleaf must translate everything it receives and, if English is directed for the award or hearings, everything Kuiper files must be translated again. Under Article 12.2(d) the tribunal allocates translation cost — but the Clause's own-costs provision (Issue 10) means Greenleaf will very likely fund its own translation burden with no prospect of recovery, whatever the outcome. On a $11.9m claim with forensic expert evidence, that is a material and entirely non-recoverable line item, and it should be budgeted before filing rather than discovered afterwards.

*Enforcement, in a direction Greenleaf may not expect.* The instinct is to push hard for English. Greenleaf should push, but carefully. If Greenleaf procures a language direction that materially disadvantages Kuiper — for example, a requirement that Kuiper's German-speaking technical witnesses testify in English, or that Kuiper file only in English contrary to the Clause's express terms — Kuiper acquires an **Article V(1)(b)** argument that it was "unable to present its case," and an **Article V(1)(d)** argument that the procedure departed from the agreement (which plainly permits German submissions). Because the list is disjunctive, that single ground would be enough to put Greenleaf's own award at risk in Germany. The correct objective is therefore not maximal English, but a **consented, recorded language protocol** that is defensible on its face.

*Timetable.* The translation burden also collides with the 90-day award deadline (Issue 3). A dual-language proceeding with translated submissions and interpreted hearings cannot be run in 90 days from constitution. The two issues should be raised together, as a single practicality point.

**Recommended fix.**

1. **Propose an agreed language protocol before filing**, as part of the same package as Issues 3 and 6. Suggested terms: (i) **English** as the procedural language for the award, procedural orders and hearings, with simultaneous interpretation into German available at the hearing for German-speaking witnesses; (ii) submissions may be filed in **either** language per the Clause, with the filing party providing a translation into the other language at its own cost; (iii) witness and expert evidence may be given in the witness's own language with interpretation; (iv) the tribunal to be requested to record the protocol under Article 12.2 in its first procedural order.
2. **Put the protocol in the Article 4.2(e) observations** in the Request for Arbitration, whether or not Kuiper agrees — Article 4.2(e) expressly invites observations on the language of the arbitration.
3. **Lead with MSA § 15.4** as the textual anchor for English primacy for the award. It is the strongest point available and it is in the parties' own instrument.
4. **Do not seek to exclude German submissions.** The Clause permits them; attempting to exclude them is both unwinnable and the source of the Article V(1)(b) risk described above.
5. **Budget the translation cost now** and flag it to Marcus Yuen alongside the Issue 10 costs analysis, as an unrecoverable case cost.
6. **Instruct that no substantive German-language material be treated as understood until translated** by a qualified translator; the Palladian technical material will need bilingual handling.

### ISSUE 8 — Perpetual confidentiality with no carve-outs deviates from Article 20's five-year term and, more importantly, omits Article 20.2's five permitted disclosures

**Severity: HIGH** — Risk direction: adverse to Greenleaf.

**Clause wording.** "All arbitration proceedings, including the existence of the arbitration and any awards, shall remain strictly confidential **in perpetuity**" [MSA § 14.2]. There are no exceptions of any kind.

**Rule deviated from.** Article 20.1 imposes confidentiality on all proceedings, submissions, evidence, awards and orders. Article 20.2 then provides that "**[n]otwithstanding Article 20.1**, disclosure of information relating to the arbitration **is permitted** in the following circumstances: (a) to the extent required by applicable law, regulation, or legal process, including **securities regulations, tax reporting obligations, and regulatory filings**; (b) to the extent necessary to protect or pursue a legal right in **bona fide legal proceedings** before a court or other judicial or regulatory authority; (c) to the extent necessary for the **enforcement or challenge of any award**; (d) with the written consent of all parties; and (e) to **professional advisors of a party who are bound by professional duties of confidentiality**." Article 20.3: obligations survive "for a period of **five (5) years** from the date of the final award," after which they cease, "**without prejudice to any confidentiality obligations arising under the parties' underlying agreement or applicable law**." Article 20.4: the tribunal may issue confidentiality orders, "including orders restricting access to certain confidential information to named individuals."

**Analysis — two separate deviations, of very different weight.**

*Duration (the lesser deviation).* Perpetual against five years. This deviation probably **stands**, and Greenleaf should not waste effort resisting it. Article 20.3's own closing words preserve "any confidentiality obligations arising under the parties' underlying agreement," and Article 1.4 gives the parties' agreement priority in any event. The MSA obligation is a contractual obligation that outlives the Rules obligation by design. Practically the consequence is limited: it means Greenleaf's confidentiality duties do not expire in 2030-something but continue, which is a compliance and records-management burden rather than a live litigation risk.

*Absence of carve-outs (the material deviation).* This is where the risk sits, and it is where Kuiper is applying pressure.

The Clause is unqualified. Read literally and in isolation, it would prohibit Greenleaf from: making a required regulatory disclosure; producing arbitration material in bona fide proceedings; disclosing the award in order to **enforce** it; or sharing information with professional advisors. Each of those is expressly permitted by Article 20.2.

Under Article 1.4 the parties' agreement prevails over the Rules — but the exceptions bite hard here:

- **Article 1.4(b):** an agreement construed to bar disclosure *required by applicable law or regulation* would contravene the law of the seat and mandatory law generally. It cannot have that effect.
- **Enforcement disclosure is self-defeating.** A confidentiality term construed to prevent a party from disclosing an award in order to enforce it would nullify the object of the arbitration agreement. No tribunal or court would adopt that construction. Article 20.2(c) exists to make the point explicit; its absence from the Clause does not create the prohibition.
- **The better construction overall** is that Section 14.2 fixes the *duration* of the obligation and Article 20.2 continues to define its *permitted exceptions*, the two operating together rather than the Clause silently repealing the Rules' carve-outs. Article 20.3's savings language, which contemplates contractual and Rules obligations coexisting, supports this reading.

**The specific and immediate risk to Greenleaf.** Kuiper's 28 October email asserts that the perpetual obligation "extends to all information relating to the dispute, including the fact that a dispute exists, all correspondence between the parties concerning the dispute, the content of Kuiper's October 3, 2024 response, and any future arbitration filings, submissions, hearings, and awards," and demands that Greenleaf ensure that "neither Greenleaf nor any of its advisors, consultants, or third-party service providers — **including any forensic experts** Greenleaf may have retained — disclose any information regarding the dispute" [Vogel Email, Confidentiality]. Two concrete exposures follow:

1. **Regulatory disclosure.** Greenleaf's direct damages expressly include "regulatory re-submissions to the U.S. Food and Drug Administration" arising from corruption of three clinical trial databases [Park Memo §§ 2.3, 3]. Data integrity in clinical trial records is a regulated matter. If Greenleaf has any disclosure obligation to the FDA, **Article 20.2(a) permits it and Kuiper cannot prevent it** — and if Kuiper's construction were accepted, Greenleaf would face a contractual obligation that conflicts with a regulatory one. That is precisely the collision Article 1.4(b) is designed to resolve, in Greenleaf's favour. Greenleaf should not accept Kuiper's construction even implicitly by silence.
2. **Palladian.** Kuiper's demand is aimed squarely at Greenleaf's forensic expert [Park Memo § 2.3; Vogel Email]. Article 20.2(e) covers "professional advisors of a party **who are bound by professional duties of confidentiality**." Whether a digital forensics vendor is a "professional advisor bound by professional duties of confidentiality" — as distinct from a contractor bound by an NDA — is genuinely arguable. This gap is closable immediately and cheaply, and should be closed before Kuiper tests it.

**Legal consequence.** Direct breach-of-contract exposure to Kuiper if Greenleaf discloses outside the carve-outs it is relying on, plus the tactical cost of a confidentiality skirmish at the outset. A confidentiality dispute is also capable of feeding **Article V(2)(b)** (public policy) in a limited way — a term construed to prevent mandatory regulatory disclosure could be characterised as contrary to public policy, and public policy is a ground the enforcing court may raise **of its own motion** under stage NY-2, without either party pleading it. On the persuasive HK standard in *Hebei Import & Export v Polytek Engineering* (1999) 2 HKCFAR 111 the public-policy threshold is high and narrow, so this is a tail risk rather than a live one, and it points *against* Kuiper's construction rather than against enforcement of an award in Greenleaf's favour. (*Hebei* is Hong Kong authority and is comparative only at a Singapore seat; it is flagged `unverified` in the source library and must be pulled and checked before any citation.)

**Recommended fix.**

1. **Put Palladian under a written confidentiality undertaking immediately**, in terms that expressly mirror Article 20 and identify Palladian's personnel by name. This converts the Article 20.2(e) argument from contestable to straightforward and should be done this week.
2. **Respond to Kuiper's 28 October assertion in writing, and do not let it stand unanswered.** State that Greenleaf accepts the confidentiality obligation and will comply, but records that the obligation is subject to the Article 20.2 exceptions — in particular (a) legally or regulatorily required disclosure, (b) bona fide legal proceedings, (c) enforcement or challenge of an award, and (e) professional advisors — and that Greenleaf does not accept any construction that would impede a required regulatory disclosure. Silence in the face of a stated construction is exactly what Article 30.1 penalises.
3. **Seek an Article 20.4 confidentiality order once the tribunal is constituted**, listing permitted recipients by name (Greenleaf personnel, Thorncastle & Whitmore, Palladian, insurers, auditors). This replaces argument with a tribunal order and is the cleanest solution.
4. **Take instructions from Marcus Yuen on FDA disclosure obligations now**, before any obligation crystallises. It is far easier to establish the Article 20.2(a) position in advance than to defend a disclosure after the fact.
5. **Audit who already knows.** The obligation covers "the existence of the arbitration." Check whether any disclosure has already been made to auditors, insurers or others, and bring it within Article 20.2(e) or 20.2(a) retrospectively where possible.

### ISSUE 9 — "Discovery … limited to document production only": an imported term of art creating avoidable ambiguity

**Severity: MODERATE** as naturally read — **HIGH** if Kuiper advances the documents-only construction. Risk direction: ambiguous.

**Clause wording.** "**Discovery shall be limited to document production only**, and each party shall bear its own costs regardless of the outcome" [MSA § 14.2].

**Rules engaged.** Article 14.3: the tribunal may order production of "documents or other evidence." Article 14.4: the tribunal may appoint experts. Article 14.5: "Any party may submit witness statements and expert reports with its written submissions." Article 16.1: "The Tribunal may decide the dispute on the basis of documents alone or may hold oral hearings… **The Tribunal shall hold a hearing if requested by any party, unless the parties have agreed to a documents-only arbitration.**" Article 17.1: "Any party may present the testimony of witnesses, including expert witnesses." Article 17.2: the tribunal may order a party to make a person available at a hearing. Article 18.2: the tribunal may take evidence "in such manner as it sees fit, including through witness testimony, expert evidence, site inspections."

**Analysis.**

*The natural reading.* "Discovery" is a term of art of United States civil procedure, imported into a Singapore-seated, SAI-administered international arbitration where it has no native meaning. Read naturally and in context, the provision limits **pre-hearing disclosure** to document production, thereby excluding depositions, interrogatories and requests for admission — which is how the Park Memo reads it [Park Memo § 4(i)], and which is the reading most consistent with international practice, where those devices are in any event unusual. On that reading the provision is unremarkable and consistent with Articles 14.3 and 18.2.

*The aggressive reading.* Kuiper could argue that limiting the evidentiary process to "document production only" means the parties agreed to a **documents-only arbitration** within Article 16.1, thereby displacing Greenleaf's right to demand an oral hearing and, on the widest version, curtailing witness and expert evidence under Articles 14.5 and 17.1. This reading is weaker — the sentence is directed at *discovery*, a pre-hearing process, not at the *hearing* — but the word "only" gives it a foothold, and Kuiper's demonstrated appetite for aggressive procedural positions [Vogel Email, throughout] makes it worth pre-empting.

*Why this matters more than usual on these facts.* Greenleaf bears the burden of proving its case (Article 14.2) and its case turns on **Kuiper's** internal records — server logs, incident reports, engineering communications — none of which Greenleaf holds. Document production under Article 14.3 is therefore not a peripheral procedural tool; it is the mechanism by which Greenleaf's claim is proved. Conversely, the exclusion of depositions is a real loss: Greenleaf cannot examine Kuiper's engineers before the hearing, in a case where Kuiper's defence is a technical causation argument about Greenleaf's API configurations [Park Memo § 2.3]. Article 17.2 partially compensates by allowing the tribunal to compel a party's officers or employees to attend a hearing for examination — but only if there is a hearing, which brings the Article 16.1 point back into focus.

*Expert evidence is untouched.* Nothing in the provision limits expert evidence. Palladian's forensic analysis enters through Articles 14.5 and 17.1 and is unaffected. Greenleaf should say so early and clearly so that the point is never in play.

**Legal consequence.** If the documents-only construction were accepted and Greenleaf were refused a hearing it had requested, the deprivation would be serious — and, in the mirror image, if the tribunal were to conduct a full evidentiary hearing over a Kuiper objection based on this provision, Kuiper acquires an **Article V(1)(d)** argument that the procedure departed from the parties' agreement, and possibly an **Article V(1)(b)** argument. The exposure is symmetrical, which again points to a recorded, consented interpretation rather than a contested one. The fix costs nothing, so it should be taken regardless of how remote the aggressive construction is thought to be.

**Recommended fix.**

1. **Seek an express interpretive agreement with Kuiper before filing**, or failing that a procedural order at the Article 13.2 case management conference, recording that Section 14.2: (a) limits pre-hearing disclosure to document production and excludes depositions, interrogatories and requests for admission; (b) does **not** constitute an agreement to a documents-only arbitration for Article 16.1 purposes; and (c) does **not** limit witness statements, expert reports or oral testimony under Articles 14.5, 17.1 and 17.2.
2. **Record it in the Terms of Reference under Article 13.1.** A Terms of Reference recital is the most durable form this agreement can take and is the answer to any later Article V(1)(d) argument by either side.
3. **Prepare a Redfern-style document request schedule now**, targeting Kuiper's server logs, incident reports, root-cause analyses, internal engineering correspondence for June–August 2024, and retention-policy documentation. The last category does double duty for Issue 2.
4. **Seek an early Article 14.3 / 18.2 production and preservation order** at the first procedural conference, independent of the emergency arbitrator application.
5. **Request an oral hearing expressly and early**, in the Article 4.2 observations if possible, so that any Article 16.1 argument is joined at a point when it can be resolved cheaply.

### ISSUE 10 — "Each party shall bear its own costs regardless of the outcome": valid, adverse, and narrower than it looks

**Severity: MODERATE** — Risk direction: adverse to Greenleaf.

**Clause wording.** "Discovery shall be limited to document production only, and **each party shall bear its own costs regardless of the outcome**" [MSA § 14.2].

**Rules engaged.** Article 26.1: the costs of the arbitration comprise "(a) the fees and expenses of the arbitrator(s); (b) the administrative charges of the SAI; (c) the fees and expenses of any expert appointed by the Tribunal; and (d) **reasonable legal and other costs incurred by the parties**." Article 25.4: "The award **shall** fix the costs of the arbitration and decide which of the parties shall bear them or in what proportion." Article 26.3: the tribunal allocates costs having regard to outcome, conduct, complexity and other factors, and "**[a]greements between the parties regarding allocation of costs shall be given effect unless the Tribunal determines that such allocation would be manifestly unreasonable in the circumstances of the case**." Article 26.4: advance payments in equal shares unless the SAI Court directs otherwise. Article 27: deposits fixed by the SAI Court.

**Analysis.**

*The provision is valid and will be applied.* Article 26.3 expressly directs that party agreements on cost allocation "shall be given effect." There is no rules-based route around it. The only hook is the **"manifestly unreasonable"** override, which is a high threshold directed at the circumstances of the case rather than at the terms of the bargain — an American-rule allocation freely agreed between two well-advised commercial parties is not manifestly unreasonable simply because one side loses.

*The provision is narrower than it reads.* It says "each party shall bear **its own** costs" — which addresses Article 26.1(**d**) only. It says nothing about categories (a), (b) and (c): **arbitrator fees, SAI administrative charges, and tribunal-appointed expert fees**. Those are not "own" costs; they are institutional costs of the arbitration. On the text, the Clause does not allocate them, and they therefore fall to be dealt with under Articles 25.4, 26.3 and 26.4 — meaning the **tribunal retains a discretion to shift the institutional costs** even though the parties' own legal costs lie where they fall. This is a genuine and useful residual entitlement, and it is the one part of the costs analysis worth arguing.

*The Clause cannot displace Article 25.4.* The award "shall fix the costs of the arbitration and decide which of the parties shall bear them." The tribunal must address costs in the award whatever the Clause says; the Clause supplies the answer for category (d), not permission to omit the exercise.

**Quantified exposure.** Applying Appendix I to an amount in controversy of $13.3m [Park Memo § 3], which converts to roughly CHF 11–12m on the memorandum's own indicative rate (CHF 5m ≈ $5.6m) and therefore sits in the **CHF 5,000,001–20,000,000** band:

| Item | Appendix I / Rules | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Filing fee (exceeding CHF 5,000,000) | App. I, § I | **CHF 5,000**, non-refundable, payable by Claimant |
| Administrative charges | App. I, § II | **CHF 25,000 – 60,000** |
| Arbitrator fees — sole arbitrator | App. I, § III | **CHF 50,000 – 200,000** |
| *(Three-member tribunal, for comparison — see Issue 5)* | App. I, § III | *CHF 120,000 – 500,000* |
| Emergency arbitrator fee | App. II, EA-2 | **CHF 20,000**, non-refundable, payable by applicant |
| Greenleaf's own legal and expert costs (incl. Palladian and translation, Issue 7) | art. 26.1(d) | **Not recoverable** |

The conversion is indicative only; the SAI Court fixes the amounts within the ranges having regard to complexity [App. I, §§ II, III].

*Strategic asymmetry.* The provision is structurally regressive for a claimant. Greenleaf brings the larger claim ($11.9m against $1.4m), carries the burden of proof (Article 14.2), must fund forensic expert evidence, and — as Issue 7 shows — will very likely bear the translation burden of a dual-language proceeding. Kuiper's defensive posture is cheaper by nature. Greenleaf therefore funds the larger share of an unrecoverable cost base. This should feed the settlement analysis: a fully successful $11.9m award is worth materially less than $11.9m, and the gap is larger for Greenleaf than the mirror figure is for Kuiper.

*Emergency arbitrator costs.* EA-6 provides that the emergency arbitrator "may allocate the costs of the emergency arbitrator procedure, including the emergency arbitrator fee and reasonable legal costs, between the parties." The Clause's own-costs rule arguably neutralises the "reasonable legal costs" limb but, on the analysis above, **not** the CHF 20,000 fee itself, which is an institutional cost of the procedure rather than a party's own cost. That is worth an express request when the application is made.

**Legal consequence.** No enforcement risk — this provision is Rules-compliant and creates no Article V exposure. It is a pure economics issue, and it is priced.

**Recommended fix.**

1. **Reserve the institutional-costs argument expressly.** In the Request for Arbitration and at the case management conference, state Greenleaf's position that Section 14.2 allocates only the parties' own costs under Article 26.1(d), and that arbitrator fees, SAI administrative charges and tribunal-expert fees remain allocable by the tribunal under Articles 25.4 and 26.3. Do this early; raising it for the first time in a closing costs submission invites the answer that it is an afterthought.
2. **Ask the emergency arbitrator to allocate the CHF 20,000 fee under EA-6** when the application is made.
3. **Keep the "manifestly unreasonable" override alive** by documenting any unreasonable conduct by Kuiper as it happens — Article 26.3 makes conduct a relevant factor, and the override is far more plausible when supported by a contemporaneous record.
4. **Consider proposing a reciprocal variation** to costs-follow-the-event as part of the pre-filing package (Issues 3, 6, 7). Kuiper is unlikely to agree, being the defendant to the larger claim, but the proposal costs nothing and its refusal is itself useful context.
5. **Give Marcus Yuen a full net-recovery estimate before filing**, netting institutional costs, unrecoverable legal fees, Palladian's fees, translation costs, and the $3.2m at risk under Issue 4. The realistic net recovery on a fully successful claim is materially below the $11.9m headline.

### ISSUE 11 — Blanket waiver of "any right to appeal the award on any grounds" is broader than Article 29 permits and inert to the extent of the excess

**Severity: MODERATE** — Risk direction: largely inert; a drafting overreach rather than a live risk.

**Clause wording.** "The parties **waive any right to appeal the award on any grounds**" [MSA § 14.2].

**Rule deviated from.** Article 29.2: "The parties waive their right to any form of recourse **insofar as such waiver can validly be made under the applicable law**." Article 29.3: "**For the avoidance of doubt, Article 29.2 does not affect any right of a party to seek the setting aside of an award that is available under the law of the seat of the arbitration and that cannot be waived under that law.**"

**Analysis.** The deviation is one of **qualification**. The Rules' waiver is expressly self-limiting on two axes — validity under the applicable law (29.2) and non-waivable seat-law set-aside rights (29.3). The Clause's waiver is unqualified on both. Three observations follow.

*To the extent the Clause purports to waive non-waivable set-aside rights, it is ineffective.* This follows from Article 1.4(b) (an agreement contravening the law of the seat does not prevail), from Article 29.3's express preservation, and from first principles: the mandatory supervisory jurisdiction of the seat is not contractually disposable. The seat is Singapore [MSA § 14.2] and the procedural law is the IAA (Cap. 143A) [Park Memo § 2.2]. The specific content of the non-waivable set-aside rights under Singapore law is a matter for verification with Singapore counsel — it is not established by anything in this record, and this report does not opine on it.

*The word "appeal" is doing imprecise work.* "Appeal" against an award, "setting aside" at the seat, and "resisting enforcement" abroad are three distinct mechanisms. The Clause addresses only the first by name. Read literally and broadly, an unqualified waiver of the right to "appeal … on any grounds" might be argued to reach resistance to enforcement — but it cannot have that effect. Article V grounds are exercised by the **enforcing court**, which is not a party to the MSA, and stage NY-2 grounds (non-arbitrability and public policy) are ones the court may raise **of its own motion** regardless of what the parties have agreed. Whatever the Clause says, Kuiper will be able to resist enforcement on Article V grounds. Greenleaf should plan on that basis and should not comfort the client with the waiver.

*Direction of benefit.* On the anticipated facts Greenleaf is the award creditor, so a waiver that narrows post-award challenge is nominally in Greenleaf's favour. But that benefit is largely illusory for the reason just given: the waiver does not reach the Article V grounds that actually threaten Greenleaf's award, and it does not reach non-waivable set-aside rights at the seat. Its practical effect is close to nil.

*A residual risk in the other direction.* If the 90-day deadline (Issue 3) or the language regime (Issue 7) produces an award that materially prejudices **Greenleaf**, this provision purports to waive Greenleaf's own recourse. Greenleaf should not assume it can rely on Article 29.3 to escape its own clause without argument.

**Legal consequence.** Minimal in itself; the provision is largely inert. Its significance is as a **drafting indicator**: it is the fourth provision in a single clause (with the 90-day deadline, the perpetual confidentiality and the remedial exclusion) drafted in absolute terms where the Rules use qualified terms. That pattern is consistent with the file's note that the Clause was proposed by Kuiper's German counsel and accepted by Greenleaf "with only minimal revisions" [Park Memo § 2.2], and it should inform how the Clause is read as a whole: as a set of hard-edged provisions drafted by the counterparty, several of which now operate against the party who accepted them.

**Recommended fix.**

1. **Do not rely on the waiver.** Assume Kuiper will have full Article V resistance rights in any enforcement forum and full non-waivable set-aside rights at the Singapore seat, and build the procedural record accordingly. That assumption is the discipline that makes the fixes to Issues 1, 3, 5, 6 and 7 worth taking.
2. **Obtain Singapore counsel's confirmation** of which set-aside rights under the IAA are non-waivable, and of the effect (if any) of an unqualified waiver on those that are waivable. This is a discrete question for a Singapore firm and should be commissioned early, since the answer also bears on Issues 3 and 7.
3. **Do not seek to have the waiver "clarified" with Kuiper.** Reopening it is more likely to produce a contested exchange than a useful concession, and the provision is inert in the direction that matters most.

### ISSUE 12 — The Rules edition is locked to the date of signing, engaging a Preamble branch the Clause does not cleanly satisfy and preserving a residual SAI Court discretion

**Severity: MODERATE** — Risk direction: ambiguous.

**Clause wording.** "…finally resolved by arbitration administered by the Stonebridge Arbitral Institute in accordance with the **SAI Arbitration Rules in effect at the time of the signing of this Agreement**" [MSA § 14.2].

**Rules engaged.** Preamble: "Where the parties' arbitration agreement references the SAI Arbitration Rules **without specifying an edition**, the Rules in effect **at the time the Request for Arbitration is filed** shall apply. Where the parties' arbitration agreement references a **specific edition** of the Rules, the SAI shall administer the arbitration under that edition, **subject to any modifications the SAI Court deems necessary for proper administration**." Article 35.1: the Rules apply to arbitrations in which the Request is filed on or after 1 January 2022. Article 35.3: "Where a party's arbitration agreement references a **specific prior edition** of the SAI Rules, the SAI shall administer the arbitration under the referenced edition **to the extent practicable**. The SAI Court may, **in its discretion, apply provisions of the current edition that are procedural or administrative in nature**, provided that such application does not prejudice the rights of either party."

**Analysis.**

*Which edition.* The MSA was signed on 15 March 2023 [MSA, Recitals]. The 2022 Edition took effect on 1 January 2022 [art. 35.1]. The SAI published a 2024 Edition effective 1 July 2024 [Park Memo § 4]. The Clause therefore points to the **2022 Edition**, and this report is written against it.

*A textual wrinkle that must be checked.* The Preamble creates a binary: an agreement that references the Rules **without specifying an edition** attracts the edition in force when the Request is filed (which would be the **2024 Edition**); one that references a **specific edition** attracts that edition. The Clause does neither cleanly. It does not name "the 2022 Edition." It identifies an edition **by reference to a date** — "in effect at the time of the signing." The better view is that functional identification by date is "specifying an edition," since the reference is unambiguous and identifies exactly one edition; Article 35.3's phrase "references a specific prior edition" supports treating the Clause as within the second branch. But the point is not free from doubt, and if the SAI Secretariat took the first branch the **2024 Edition** would apply — an edition that is **not in the record** and whose contents cannot be assessed here.

*The residual discretion.* Even on the favourable reading, the Clause does not deliver a pure 2022 regime. Two overrides survive: the Preamble subjects administration under a specified edition to "any modifications the SAI Court deems necessary for proper administration," and Article 35.3 permits the SAI Court to apply current-edition provisions that are "procedural or administrative in nature" where doing so does not prejudice either party. Greenleaf should therefore plan on a **2022 core with possible 2024 procedural overlay**, not on a hermetically sealed 2022 proceeding. Given that several of the Clause's most problematic terms are procedural (Issues 3, 6, 7), the boundary between "procedural or administrative" and substantive could become contested.

*Interaction with Issue 2.* The emergency arbitrator conclusion at Issue 2 is reached under the 2022 Edition. Article 15bis.4(b) turns on the effective date of "the edition of the Rules first introducing the emergency arbitrator procedure," and Appendix II EA-7 is expressly framed as applying to "arbitrations under the SAI Rules (2022 Edition)." If the 2024 Edition were applied, the analysis must be re-run against that edition's text. This is the single most important practical reason to settle the edition question before filing.

**Legal consequence.** Uncertainty rather than a distinct enforcement ground. The most likely manifestations are administrative friction and delay — a Secretariat query on filing, which is precisely the delay the 90-day deadline (Issue 3) cannot absorb — and, in the worst case, application of an edition whose material differences from the 2022 Edition are unknown to Greenleaf at the point of filing. If the SAI applied an edition contrary to the parties' agreement and a party objected promptly, an **Article V(1)(d)** argument on the arbitral procedure would in principle be available, though a court is unlikely to refuse enforcement on this basis alone, and stage NY-3's residual discretion would weigh heavily against refusal.

**Recommended fix.**

1. **Obtain the 2024 Edition and compare it against the 2022 Edition before filing**, focusing on the provisions engaged by this report: Articles 6 (number and appointment), 12 (language), 15bis and Appendix II (emergency arbitrator), 20 (confidentiality), 24 (award time limit), 26 (costs), 34 (remedies). Greenleaf cannot make an informed election, or respond to a Secretariat query, without knowing what the differences are. This is a gap in the current record and should be closed as a priority.
2. **State the position expressly in the Request for Arbitration.** Article 4.2(b) requires a copy of the arbitration agreement; add a short statement that the parties specified the edition in effect at signature, namely the 2022 Edition, and that Greenleaf files on that basis.
3. **Raise it with the SAI Secretariat before filing if practicable.** A pre-filing enquiry costs nothing and avoids a post-filing query that consumes days from the appointment clock (Issue 6).
4. **Re-verify the Issue 2 emergency arbitrator conclusion** against the 2024 Edition if there is any prospect it applies.
5. **Watch the Article 35.3 / Preamble overlay.** If the SAI Court proposes to apply 2024 provisions as "procedural or administrative," assess prejudice under Article 35.3 and object promptly if there is any — Article 30.2 gives Greenleaf until its next submission or hearing, and no longer.

### ISSUE 13 — The Clause agrees no arbitrator qualifications, forfeiting the second limb of Article 7.1 in a case that needs it

**Severity: MODERATE** — Risk direction: adverse to Greenleaf. *This issue is not raised in the assignment memorandum.*

**Clause wording.** The Clause specifies only that the arbitration "shall be conducted by a sole arbitrator mutually agreed upon by the parties" [MSA § 14.2]. It is **silent** on qualifications, expertise, professional background, nationality and language capability.

**Rules engaged.** Article 7.1: "Any party may challenge an arbitrator **if circumstances exist that give rise to justifiable doubts as to the arbitrator's impartiality or independence, or if the arbitrator does not possess qualifications agreed to by the parties**." Article 7.2: challenge within 15 calendar days of becoming aware of the circumstances. Article 7.3: "The SAI Court shall decide challenges. **The decision of the SAI Court shall be final and not subject to review.**" Article 8.2: each arbitrator must submit a declaration of independence and impartiality "disclosing any facts or circumstances that might be of such a nature as to call into question the arbitrator's independence or impartiality **in the eyes of any of the parties**." Article 8.3: the disclosure duty is continuing.

**Analysis.**

*The forfeited limb.* Article 7.1 offers two independent challenge routes: justifiable doubts, and failure to possess **qualifications agreed to by the parties**. Because the Clause agrees no qualifications, the second route is unavailable to Greenleaf. If the SAI Court appoints under Article 6.4 someone with no cloud-computing or life-sciences background, Greenleaf has **no qualification-based challenge**. Its only route is the impartiality/independence limb, which addresses a different defect entirely.

*Why that bites here.* This dispute requires the arbitrator to assess forensic evidence about the origin of database corruption on a cloud platform, evaluate a technical causation defence about API configuration compatibility, and understand the regulatory consequences of clinical trial data integrity for FDA re-submissions [Park Memo §§ 2.3, 3]. Kuiper's own counsel identifies the needed profile as expertise "in both cloud computing and data management, on the one hand, and life sciences regulation, on the other" [Vogel Email, Arbitrator Appointment Timeline]. **That is a written concession by the counterparty on the qualification profile**, made before any dispute over appointment has crystallised, and it is directly usable — in the appointment negotiation, and in submissions to the SAI Court if it comes to appointment under Article 6.4. It should be quoted.

*Applying the challenge standard from the connector.* The "justifiable doubts" test in Article 7.1 corresponds to the Model Law art. 12 standard, which the element library classifies as a **balancing** test, not a checklist: the question is whether an objective, informed observer would consider there to be a real possibility of doubt as to impartiality, with **no single factor dispositive**. The library's weighting has direct practical consequences for how Greenleaf should behave at the appointment stage:

- **Failure to disclose — heavy.** The most significant single factor. This is what makes Articles 8.2 and 8.3 the operative levers rather than Article 7.1 itself.
- **Financial interest — heavy.**
- **Overlapping appointments — substantial**, but *not* heavy on its own.
- **Relationship with a party or counsel — substantial.**
- **Previously expressed views — moderate.**
- **Custom of the particular field — light, and typically operates to weaken a challenge**, on the footing that what is normal in a specialised market is less likely to trouble the informed observer.

The pattern this produces is the one identified in *Halliburton v Chubb* [2020] UKSC 48: **overlapping appointments alone rarely carry a challenge; overlapping appointments combined with non-disclosure is what carries it.** For this case the inference is concrete. The pool of arbitrators combining cloud/data expertise, life-sciences familiarity, English-and-German capability, and availability on the Clause's compressed timetable is small — so **repeat and overlapping appointments within that pool are likely**, and the "custom of the field" factor (light, mitigating) will be deployed against any challenge Greenleaf brings on that basis alone. Banking on a later challenge is therefore a poor strategy.

*The Article 7.3 finality point compounds this.* The SAI Court's decision on a challenge is "final and not subject to review." There is no institutional appeal. Combined with the sole-arbitrator structure (Issue 5), Greenleaf gets one arbitrator, one challenge attempt, and no review — which is why the front-end disclosure process matters far more than the back-end challenge mechanism.

**Legal consequence.** Two distinct exposures.

*Quality risk (primary).* An arbitrator without the technical background may misapprehend the forensic evidence in a case where Greenleaf bears the burden of proof (Article 14.2). This is not an enforcement issue; it is an outcome issue, and on a $11.9m claim it is the more important of the two.

*Challenge and enforcement risk (secondary).* A successful challenge mid-proceeding triggers Article 7.4 (replacement following the original appointment procedure) and Article 7.5 (the reconstituted tribunal decides what must be repeated) — which, against the 90-day deadline (Issue 3), would be destructive of the timetable. An unsuccessful challenge, if the underlying non-disclosure were serious enough, can feed an **Article V(2)(b)** public-policy argument at enforcement, a ground the enforcing court may raise of its own motion under stage NY-2. (The authorities in the library, including *Halliburton*, are flagged `unverified` and must be checked before citation; *Halliburton* is English authority and persuasive only at a Singapore seat.)

**Recommended fix.**

1. **Agree the qualification profile with Kuiper in writing before filing.** This is the fix, and Kuiper has already supplied the language. Propose that the parties record an agreed profile — demonstrable experience in cloud computing/data management disputes; familiarity with life-sciences regulatory frameworks; working proficiency in English and German; availability consistent with the agreed timetable. Once recorded, it becomes "qualifications agreed to by the parties" and **restores the second limb of Article 7.1** for the rest of the case. Fold it into the same pre-filing package as Issues 3, 6 and 7.
2. **Put the profile in the Request for Arbitration** under Article 4.2(f), which expressly contemplates a proposed arbitrator or a proposal for the method of appointment, whether or not Kuiper agrees. If the SAI Court ends up appointing under Article 6.4, the profile on the file is what it will work from.
3. **Press for full Article 8.2 disclosure at the outset and hold the arbitrator to the Article 8.3 continuing duty.** Given that non-disclosure is the heavy factor and overlapping appointments alone are not, the value is in what gets disclosed early. Ask specifically about appointments involving either party, either party's counsel (Thorncastle & Whitmore; Feldhammer Grau), and other cloud-services or life-sciences disputes.
4. **Record known overlaps rather than storing them up.** If a candidate discloses an overlapping appointment that Greenleaf is content to accept, say so in writing. That prevents Kuiper from later characterising Greenleaf's silence as acquiescence in something it did not know, and it keeps the Article 30 record clean.
5. **Diarise the Article 7.2 fifteen-day challenge window** from the moment of any disclosure. It is short and it is easily lost.

### ISSUE 14 — New York substantive law with a Singapore seat: no deviation, but the bifurcation determines which law answers several of the questions above

**Severity: LOW** — Risk direction: neutral. Cross-cutting; included because the assignment memorandum raises it three times [Park Memo §§ 2.2, 7 (item 3), closing].

**Clause wording.** Section 14.1: the Agreement and all related claims "shall be governed by and construed in accordance with the **laws of the State of New York**, … without giving effect to any choice-of-law or conflict-of-law rules." Section 14.1(b): "The governing law designated in this Section 14.1 governs the **substantive** rights and obligations of the Parties… For the avoidance of doubt, the **procedural law** governing any arbitration commenced pursuant to Section 14.2 shall be determined by the **seat** of arbitration designated therein." Section 14.2: "The seat of arbitration shall be **Singapore**."

**Rules engaged.** Article 10.1: the seat "shall be as agreed by the parties." Article 11.1: the tribunal "shall apply the rules of law designated by the parties as applicable to the substance of the dispute." Article 23.1: "Any designation of the law of a given State shall be construed, unless otherwise expressed, as **directly referring to the substantive law of that State and not to its conflict-of-laws rules**." Article 10.4 and 25.3: the award is deemed made at the seat.

**Analysis.** There is **no deviation**. The Clause's designations are exactly what Articles 10.1, 11.1 and 23.1 contemplate, and Section 14.1's express exclusion of renvoi matches Article 23.1's default construction. Section 14.1(b) is unusually well drafted: it states the substantive/procedural division expressly, which removes an argument that often has to be constructed from first principles. Notably, this is the one part of the dispute-resolution provisions drafted with care — which sharpens the contrast with Section 14.2.

The bifurcation is nonetheless the organising principle for several issues above, and it is worth setting out which law answers which question:

| Question | Governed by | Where analysed |

| Is the consequential-damages exclusion effective? | **New York** substantive law (arts. 23.1, 34.3) | Issue 4 |
| Are the $3.2m service-outage losses direct or consequential? | **New York** substantive law | Issue 4 |
| Is the $15m liability cap effective? | **New York** substantive law | Issue 4 (not currently binding) |
| Can the 90-day award deadline invalidate the award? | **Singapore** law of the seat (IAA), plus art. 1.4(b) | Issue 3 |
| Which set-aside rights are non-waivable? | **Singapore** law of the seat (art. 29.3) | Issue 11 |
| Is a modification of the Rules permitted? | Rules art. 1.4(a) and **Singapore** seat law, art. 1.4(b) | Issues 3, 6, 8, 10, 11 |
| Which court supervises the arbitration? | **Singapore** (seat), arts. 10.1, 10.4 | Issues 2, 11 |
| Where is the award deemed made? | **Singapore** (arts. 10.4, 25.3) | drives Art. V(1)(e) analysis |
| On what grounds can enforcement be resisted? | **The enforcing forum's** application of NY Convention Art. V — most likely Germany | Section I.4 above |

Two practical points follow. First, the award will be a **Singapore** award for Convention purposes (Articles 10.4 and 25.3), so any set-aside application belongs in Singapore and a successful one engages **Article V(1)(e)** everywhere else. ## VII. Cross-Cutting: Preserving Objections Under Article 30

Article 30 is not a deviation but it governs whether any of the above can still be raised when it matters, and it should be treated as a standing operating instruction for the team.

A party that knows of a departure from the Rules or from the arbitration agreement and proceeds "without promptly raising an objection" is **deemed to have waived** the right to object [art. 30.1]. An objection is prompt only if raised "at the earliest opportunity and in any event **no later than the next written submission or hearing**" after the party becomes aware [art. 30.2].

Practical consequences for this matter:

1. **Kuiper's 28 October positions are already on the record**, so Kuiper has preserved its appointment-timeline objection (Issue 6) and its confidentiality construction (Issue 8) in advance. Greenleaf has not yet answered either. **Answer both in writing before filing.**
2. **Every deviation catalogued in this report should be addressed on the record at the first opportunity** — in the Request for Arbitration under Article 4.2(e)–(f), and then at the Article 13.2 case management conference. An objection stored for later is an objection lost.
3. **Article 30 also protects Greenleaf.** If Kuiper participates in a timetable inconsistent with the 90-day deadline (Issue 3), files in German without objecting to an English award (Issue 7), or acquiesces in a departure from the appointment window (Issue 6) without prompt objection, Greenleaf should record Kuiper's silence contemporaneously. That contemporaneous record is the raw material for a waiver argument at the enforcement stage, and it cannot be manufactured after the fact.
4. **Maintain a running objections log** from the date of filing: date, departure, date of awareness, date and form of objection, Kuiper's response. It is a five-minute-a-week discipline that materially improves the enforcement position.
