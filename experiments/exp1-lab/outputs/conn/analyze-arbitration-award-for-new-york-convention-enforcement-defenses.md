## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

**1.1 Bottom line.** The Award is enforceable, and Pacífico's procedural defences are individually weak. But the Award carries one defect of a different order of seriousness from everything else in the file, and it is not a defence Pacífico must prove — it is a defect the enforcing court can and will see for itself on the face of the instrument we are obliged to file.

**The operative part of the Award orders payment of USD $14,700,000. The Tribunal's own reasoning calculates the total sum due as USD $9,530,861. The gap is USD $5,169,139, and it cannot be reconciled on any reading of the Award.** I have tested every reconstruction the record permits (Section 6 below). None produces $14,700,000. The figure is not merely unexplained; it is unexplainable from the four corners of the document.

**1.2 The answer to your central question.** You asked whether Greenleaf should seek the full *dispositif* amount or an arithmetically supported figure. **My recommendation is that Greenleaf plead for USD $9,530,861 as its primary relief, expressly and transparently, and reserve the $14,700,000 figure only as a pleaded alternative.** The reasoning is set out at Section 6.5. In short: the $5.17m increment is the only thing in this file capable of losing us the whole award, Article V(1)(c) contains an express severability rule that is designed for exactly this situation, and the residual discretion that Mexican courts enjoy under Article 1462 of the Código de Comercio is far more likely to be exercised in favour of a creditor who has volunteered the problem than one who is caught by it.

**2.2 Analytical framework.** I have applied the structured Article V element library for the governing law. Four structural features of that framework drive the organisation of what follows, and each of them changed the shape of this analysis:

- **Article V is a *closed list*.** It is not a general appeal against the Award. Any objection Pacífico raises that does not map onto one of the seven enumerated grounds is not a defence at all, however meritorious it might have been before the Tribunal. This disposes of a great deal of what Pacífico's letter of 20 September 2023 asserts — in particular its "manifest error in the *dispositif*" heading, which is a set-aside vocabulary borrowed from Alberta and has no Article V counterpart. The error matters at enforcement only if it can be re-expressed as scope (V(1)(c)) or public policy (V(2)(b)), and I have analysed it in both places rather than accepting Pacífico's framing.
- **Article V(1) is a *disjunctive gateway*.** Any single ground, if made out, is sufficient to justify refusal. There is no cumulative-weight requirement and no need for Pacífico to succeed on more than one. This means the correct posture is not to compute an average across the seven grounds but to identify the *strongest single* ground and defend that perimeter. Here that is unambiguously the *dispositif* increment.
- **The burden under Article V(1) rests on Pacífico**, but **Article V(2) grounds — arbitrability and public policy — may be raised by the court of its own motion.** This asymmetry is decisive in planning. We cannot manage the V(2)(b) risk by declining to argue it, because the *Juzgado de Distrito* may raise it without any prompting from Velázquez Ibarra, and the Award we are obliged to file under Article IV puts the material in front of the judge whether Pacífico pleads it or not.
- **Even where a ground is made out, the court retains a residual discretion to enforce nonetheless.** Article V is permissive ("may be refused"), as is Article 1462 of the Código de Comercio. The Hong Kong Court of Final Appeal's formulation in *Hebei Import & Export Corp v Polytek Engineering Co Ltd* (1999) 2 HKCFAR 111 — that a party which failed to raise a complaint before the tribunal or at the seat may be precluded from relying on it, and that refusal requires conduct offending basic notions of morality and justice — is the standard I have applied to the discretionary stage. It is the layer at which most of Pacífico's procedural case will die even if it survives the elements.

## 4. THE GOVERNING FRAMEWORK

**4.1 The Convention applies without reservation.** Mexico acceded on 14 April 1971 without either the reciprocity or the commercial reservation; Canada acceded on 12 May 1986. The Award was made in Calgary, Alberta — a Convention state — and arises from a commercial relationship. There is no threshold admissibility issue. Pacífico cannot contest the Convention's application, and I would expect it not to try.

**4.2 The Article IV pack is a precondition, not a defence — but it is where oppositions are actually won in practice.** Article IV requires the applicant to supply the duly authenticated original award or a duly certified copy, and the original arbitration agreement or a duly certified copy, together with a translation certified by an official or sworn translator where the award is not in the language of the enforcing country. In Mexican practice this means a *perito traductor* rendering into Spanish. I flag this not because it is an Article V ground — it is not — but because it is the most common practical basis on which Mexican enforcement applications are delayed or dismissed without prejudice, and because our Article IV pack has an integrity problem of its own (Section 7).

**4.3 The seven grounds, and who bears what.** Article V(1) enumerates five grounds, each of which Pacífico must plead and prove: (a) incapacity or invalidity of the agreement; (b) improper notice or inability to present its case; (c) matters beyond the scope of the submission; (d) composition or procedure not in accordance with the agreement; (e) the award not yet binding, or set aside or suspended at the seat. Article V(2) adds two grounds the court may raise of its own motion: (a) non-arbitrability, and (b) contravention of the public policy of the enforcing state. In Mexico these are implemented by Articles 1457 and 1462 of the Código de Comercio, with Article 1462(II) carrying the public-policy ground.

**4.4 Article VI.** Where an application to set aside or suspend the award is *pending* at the seat, the enforcing court may adjourn its decision and may order security. **There is no such application here.** Article VI is therefore unavailable to Pacífico, and this should be stated affirmatively in the petition rather than left for reply.

**4.5 The residual discretion.** Article V provides that recognition and enforcement "may be refused," not that they must be. Article 1462 CCom is likewise permissive ("podrá"). The consequence is that even a ground technically made out does not compel refusal. The court will ask whether the defect caused real prejudice, whether the objecting party raised it when it could have, and whether refusing enforcement is proportionate. This discretionary layer is where the bulk of Pacífico's case fails, and our submissions should be constructed so that the court reaches it on every ground.

## 5. GROUND-BY-GROUND ANALYSIS

### 5.1 Article V(1)(a) — Incapacity of the parties or invalidity of the arbitration agreement

**5.1.1 The standard.** Enforcement may be refused where the parties to the arbitration agreement were "under some incapacity," or where the agreement "is not valid under the law to which the parties have subjected it or, failing any indication thereon, under the law of the country where the award was made." The provision contains its own conflicts rule, and that rule is the beginning and end of the analysis here.

**5.1.2 Incapacity — not available.** No incapacity is alleged and none arises. Both parties warranted at Section 9.1(a) of the Agreement that they were "duly organized, validly existing, and in good standing," and at Section 9.1(b) that execution was "duly authorized by all necessary corporate action." The Agreement was signed for Pacífico by Sr. Rodrigo Espinoza Villanueva, Director General, on 15 March 2019. Pacífico has never contended otherwise, in the arbitration or in its letter of 20 September 2023. **Strength: none.**

**5.1.3 Invalidity — the unconscionability argument.** Pacífico's case, pleaded at Section III.A of its Statement of Defence (15 July 2022, ¶¶23–25) and developed in its jurisdictional objection of 2 September 2022, is that Section 14.2 is unconscionable under Mexican law because it compels a Mexican company to arbitrate in English in Calgary. Its four sub-arguments were structural imbalance and take-it-or-leave-it presentation; language barrier; absence of any connection between Calgary and the dispute; and that Mexican law, as the law most closely connected, should govern validity.

**5.1.4 Why this fails.** *First, on the governing law.* Section 14.1 selects British Columbia law for the Agreement; Section 14.2 is silent as to the law governing the arbitration clause. The Tribunal held at Award ¶46 that, absent a separate designation, the law of the seat — Alberta — governs validity. **That is not a controversial choice; it is the Convention's own default rule under Article V(1)(a) applied to itself.** The enforcing court in Guadalajara, applying Article V(1)(a), must ask whether the clause is valid "under the law of the country where the award was made" in the absence of party indication — which is Alberta. There is a respectable competing view, associated with *Enka Insaat v OOO Insurance Company Chubb*, that the main contract's governing law presumptively extends to the arbitration agreement. But that route leads to British Columbia law, not Mexican law. **On no orthodox conflicts analysis does Mexican law govern the validity of this clause.** Pacífico's contention that the "closest connection" test points to Mexico is an argument for a rule that Article V(1)(a) does not contain.

*Second, on the merits of unconscionability.* Both parties are substantial commercial enterprises — Greenleaf with CAD $95m in revenue, Pacífico with approximately MXN $1.2bn (Award ¶¶1–2). The Agreement recites at its fourth and fifth WHEREAS clauses that the parties "engaged in arm's-length negotiations" and that "each Party has had the opportunity to consult with its own legal counsel and business advisors prior to executing this Agreement." Pacífico's "standard-form, take-it-or-leave-it" characterisation (Defence ¶25(a)) contradicts a recital in the instrument it signed. Section 15.1 makes the Agreement the entire agreement. Sr. Espinoza Villanueva, whom Pacífico pleaded at Defence ¶25(b) to be "a native Spanish speaker with limited professional proficiency in English," in fact testified in English without an interpreter at the January 2023 hearing (Award ¶21) — a point the Tribunal expressly relied on at ¶47 and which flatly contradicts Pacífico's pleaded case.

*Third, separability.* Rule 18.1 of the PAC Rules embodies separability, and the Tribunal's *kompetenz-kompetenz* was exercised in the ordinary way.

**5.1.5 Anticipated recycling.** Expect Velázquez Ibarra to reframe unconscionability as public policy under V(2)(b) rather than validity under V(1)(a), because the V(2)(b) route lets it invoke Mexican law directly rather than having to win the conflicts point first. Our answer must therefore be pre-positioned in both places: the clause is valid under the only laws that can govern it, and a freely negotiated arbitration clause between two large merchants offends no conception of *orden público internacional*.

**5.1.6 Assessment: WEAK.** Pacífico preserved the point properly (Rule 18.2; Defence ¶5 reserving rights; jurisdictional objection filed 2 September 2022) so it is not waived, and it will be argued. It should not succeed.

### 5.2 Article V(1)(b) — Improper notice or inability to present one's case

**5.2.1 The standard.** Enforcement may be refused where the party against whom the award is invoked "was not given proper notice of the appointment of the arbitrator or of the arbitration proceedings or was otherwise unable to present his case." This is the due-process ground. It is narrowly construed. The touchstone is not whether every procedural step was flawless but whether the party was in substance deprived of the opportunity to be heard, and whether any deprivation caused material prejudice.

**5.2.2 Notice of appointment and of the proceedings — not available.** Pacífico nominated its own arbitrator (Lic. Beltrán Cisneros, 1 March 2022), accepted Procedural Order No. 1 by letter of 28 April 2022, filed a full Statement of Defence and Counterclaim, participated in document production, filed witness statements and an expert report, appeared at a three-day hearing, appeared at the supplemental hearing, and filed post-hearing briefs. There is no notice defect at the front end of this case.

**5.2.3 The supplemental hearing of 3 March 2023 — the real point, and it is two points.**

*(a) The 28-day notice period.* **Rule 22.4 of the PAC Rules provides: "Not less than 30 days' advance written notice of any hearing shall be given to the parties by the Centre or the Tribunal."** Rule 22.6 expressly applies that requirement to supplemental hearings: "The scheduling of additional hearing days shall be subject to the notice requirements of Rule 22.4 unless the parties agree otherwise." The notice was sent on **3 February 2023** for a hearing on **3 March 2023**. That is **28 days**. I have checked the calculation and it is correct on any counting convention. Rule 22.4 permits a shorter period only where "the parties may agree to a shorter notice period in writing, or the Tribunal may shorten the notice period in exceptional circumstances with the consent of all parties." **Neither exception is documented anywhere in the record.**

I want to be direct with you: this is a genuine, provable, non-trivial breach of the applicable institutional rules, and it is the only one in the file. Your instinct in flagging it was right.

*(b) The email address.* By letter of 12 August 2022 Velázquez Ibarra Abogados notified the Centre that Pacífico's corporate address was changed from `admin@pacifico-dist.com.mx` to `legal@pacifico-dist.com.mx`, stating that the former "is being decommissioned" and "should no longer be used," and requesting written confirmation of the update. **Rule 4.2 obliges the Centre to "update its records upon receipt of such notification."** The case manager's email of 3 February 2023 was nonetheless copied to `admin@pacifico-dist.com.mx`. The Centre did not do what Rule 4.2 required.

**5.2.4 Why both nonetheless fail.**

*On the email address, four answers, in ascending order of force.* (i) The notice was sent **directly to Pacífico's counsel of record** at `hvelazquez@velazquezibarra.com.mx` — the address recorded in Procedural Order No. 1 ¶5 and never changed. Service on counsel is service on the party. (ii) `admin@pacifico-dist.com.mx` is the **contractual notice address designated by Pacífico itself at Section 15.6 of the Agreement** ("If to Pacífico: … Attention: Director General; Email: admin@pacifico-dist.com.mx"), and Section 15.6 requires a change of that address to be effected by written notice *to the other Party*. The 12 August 2022 letter was addressed to the Centre's case manager; it was copied to Northgate Harwick, so I do not press this too hard, but the Agreement's own designated address was used. (iii) Rule 4.4 provides that the Centre "shall be entitled to rely on the most recent contact information contained in the Centre's records, and any communication sent to such address shall be deemed to have been properly delivered." (iv) **Decisively: Pacífico received the notice and attended the hearing.** Whatever the defect, it produced no consequence whatsoever.

*On the 28 days, three answers.* (i) **Actual receipt and full participation.** Pacífico's counsel Lic. Velázquez Ramos attended the hearing on 3 March 2023, as did Pacífico's damages expert Econ. Fuentes Medina, who gave further testimony responding to the Tribunal's questions on his projection methodology (Award ¶26). Both parties, the Award records at ¶26, "were afforded a full and fair opportunity to examine and cross-examine the expert witnesses." (ii) **Waiver under Rule 24.1**, which is express and directly on point: "A party that knows or ought to know that any provision of these Rules … has not been complied with, and yet proceeds with the arbitration without promptly stating its objection, shall be deemed to have waived its right to object to such non-compliance." Pacífico's counsel is a sophisticated arbitration practitioner who received a dated email scheduling a dated hearing; the two-day shortfall was apparent on the face of the notice on 3 February 2023. Award ¶27 records that "no procedural objections were raised by either party at the commencement of, or during, the supplemental hearing," and the chronology confirms it. The first time Pacífico mentioned Rule 22.4 was in its letter of **20 September 2023 — more than six months after the hearing and nearly three months after the Award.** (iii) **No prejudice, and none has ever been articulated.** Pacífico has never said what it would have done with two more days. Its expert appeared and testified. Rule 24.2's carve-out for non-waivable matters does not assist: a two-day shortfall in notice of a supplementary expert hearing that the party attended and participated in fully is not a matter incapable of waiver.

**5.2.5 The language and interpretation thread.** Pacífico raised language at the outset — it requested Spanish as an additional language at the case management conference of 18 April 2022 and, in the alternative, bilingual proceedings with interpretation at Greenleaf's cost and Spanish originals prevailing (Defence ¶30). Procedural Order No. 1 ¶2 declined the request but directed that "Spanish-English interpretation services will be arranged at the expense of the Centre for any witness who requires them." That is **more** than Rule 16.3 requires (which contemplates interpretation costs being allocated as part of the costs of the arbitration). Two of Pacífico's fact witnesses testified in Spanish through Ms. Elena Rojas, a certified court interpreter registered with the Alberta Association of Court Interpreters (Award ¶20). Counsel noted for the record that interpretation "might not capture all nuances," and the Tribunal acknowledged that observation; **but "no formal objection to the interpretation or to the use of English as the language of the proceedings was lodged by Respondent at any time during the hearing"** (Award ¶20). Meanwhile Sr. Espinoza Villanueva testified in English (Award ¶21). Above all, **English was the parties' own contractual choice** — Section 14.2 designates it, and Section 15.9 provides that the Agreement "has been negotiated, drafted, and executed in the English language" and that the English version governs. A party cannot found a due-process defence on the very procedural bargain it struck. **Weak.**

**5.2.6 The joinder-of-jurisdiction thread.** Pacífico complained (letter of 25 September 2022; recorded at Procedural Order No. 3 §3) that being compelled to incur full merits costs before jurisdiction was resolved "fundamentally prejudices Respondent." Being made to spend money is not an inability to present one's case; it is the ordinary consequence of a procedural ruling that went against it. Procedural Order No. 3 ¶1.3 expressly directed that Pacífico "shall have the opportunity to present evidence and argument in support of its Jurisdictional Objection at the evidentiary hearing and in its post-hearing brief," and the Order records that "Joinder does not prejudice Respondent's rights." Pacífico did present those arguments and the Tribunal decided them in Section VI of the Award. Analysed further at Section 5.4.4. **Weak.**

**5.2.7 A thread Pacífico has not yet found, and probably will.** The dissent's central contractual point is that Greenleaf owed a cooperation obligation in relation to COFEPRIS documentation. The majority disposed of that at Award ¶¶61–63 by construing **Section 5.3** alone and declining to "imply an obligation on Claimant that contradicts the express terms of the Agreement." But **Section 5.2 of the Agreement contains an express obligation in precisely those terms**: "Greenleaf shall provide Pacífico with such product specifications, certificates of analysis, ingredient lists, formulation details, and other technical documentation as may be reasonably required by Pacífico to support applications for Regulatory Approvals in the Territory. Greenleaf shall bear the cost of producing such documentation and shall use commercially reasonable efforts to provide such documentation in a timely manner upon Pacífico's written request." **The Award never cites Section 5.2.** Nor, remarkably, did Pacífico's own Statement of Defence, which pleaded the point as an *implied* obligation (¶¶13, 17, 32) — and nor did the dissent, which also framed it as implied (Annex A ¶¶9, 11).

New Mexico City counsel reading the Agreement fresh will find Section 5.2. When they do, they will argue that the Tribunal failed to engage with an express contractual term dispositive of a pleaded defence. Our answers: (i) the Tribunal decided the *issue* Pacífico actually put to it — cooperation on COFEPRIS — and decided it against Pacífico on the evidence; a tribunal need not address every argument, only every issue; (ii) Pacífico never pleaded Section 5.2 and cannot complain that the Tribunal did not decide a case it did not advance; (iii) Section 5.2 imposes a "commercially reasonable efforts" obligation triggered "upon Pacífico's written request," and the Tribunal weighed the evidence of what was requested and supplied; (iv) most importantly, **this is a merits complaint, and Article V contains no ground for merits review.** But it will be argued as V(1)(b) (failure to consider a submission) and as V(2)(b) (failure to give reasons on a dispositive point), and we should be ready. **Weak as a Convention ground; moderate as advocacy.**

**5.2.8 Assessment: WEAK overall.** The Rule 22.4 breach is real and provable — this is the one procedural allegation Pacífico can substantiate — but it is defeated by participation, by express waiver under Rule 24.1, and by the complete absence of prejudice. Expect Pacífico to lead with it because it is the only defect it can prove; expect it to fail at the discretionary stage if not before.

### 5.3 Article V(1)(c) — Matters beyond the scope of the submission to arbitration

**5.3.1 The standard, and its critical second sentence.** Enforcement may be refused where the award "deals with a difference not contemplated by or not falling within the terms of the submission to arbitration, or contains decisions on matters beyond the scope of the submission to arbitration." The provision then continues: **"provided that, if the decisions on matters submitted to arbitration can be separated from those not so submitted, that part of the award which contains decisions on matters submitted to arbitration may be recognized and enforced."** That severability proviso is the mechanism at the centre of my recommendation and should be pleaded expressly.

Two points of construction matter. First, V(1)(c) concerns the *scope of what was referred*, not the *correctness of what was decided*. An erroneous decision on a referred matter is not within V(1)(c). Second, *ultra petita* — awarding more than was claimed — is the paradigm V(1)(c) case, because relief not sought was not submitted.

**5.3.2 The unexplained increment — this is the exposure.** The Tribunal's reasoning states at ¶86 that "the total amount due from Respondent to Claimant is USD $9,530,861." The *dispositif* at ¶6 orders "the total sum of USD $14,700,000." The difference is **USD $5,169,139**.

I have attempted to reconstruct the higher figure on every basis the record supports:

| Reconstruction attempted | Result (USD) | Matches $14.7m? |
|---|---|---|
| Reasoned build-up (Award ¶¶72–86) | 9,530,861 | No |
| Gross lost profits without mitigation credit + interest + costs | 10,397,111 | No |
| Amount claimed ($12.6m) + costs and fees ($1,722,500) | 14,322,500 | No |

**No combination of figures appearing anywhere in the Award, the pleadings or the Agreement produces USD $14,700,000.** It also exceeds the USD $12,600,000 Greenleaf actually claimed (Award ¶9; Defence ¶3) by $2.1m, and exceeds the claim plus every costs item by $377,500. On the *ultra petita* analysis, that excess over the sum claimed is the cleanest V(1)(c) case Pacífico has.

To be scrupulous about the strength of our own position: the counter-arguments are real. (i) The *dispositif* is the operative part; under Rule 27.2(f) it is required to state "the precise amounts awarded," and in most systems it governs where reasoning and operative part diverge. (ii) The mismatch is on its face a computational or clerical slip rather than an adjudication of an unsubmitted difference — and a slip is not, doctrinally, a decision on a matter beyond the scope of the submission. (iii) All the *heads* of relief in the *dispositif* (lost profits, interest, costs, fees) were unquestionably submitted; only the quantum is unexplained, and V(1)(c) is not a quantum-review provision.

But those arguments have to be made to a *Juzgado de Distrito* that is being asked to convert a foreign instrument into a Mexican executory title for $14.7m when the instrument itself says $9,530,861 on the preceding page. **That is not an argument I would want to have to win**, and the risk is not confined to the increment: a judge who concludes that the Award is unreliable as to $5.17m may become receptive to Pacífico's public-policy case as to the whole.

**5.3.3 The correction window has closed.** Rule 29.1 allowed either party thirty days from receipt of the Award to request correction of "any computational, clerical, or typographical error." Rule 29.2 allowed the Tribunal to correct on its own initiative within thirty days of the date of the Award — that is, by **28 July 2023**. Neither party applied; the Tribunal did not act. **The Tribunal is now functus officio for this purpose.** The discrepancy cannot be cured at source. I record, with regret, that this window passed without an application being made, and that a Rule 29.1 request in July 2023 would have resolved the entire issue for the cost of a letter. That option no longer exists and the strategy at Section 6 is built around its absence.

**5.3.4 Legal fees and expert fees — no scope problem.** You asked specifically about the contractual and institutional authority for the fee award. The authority is sound, though the Award cites it incorrectly.

- Award ¶83 awards USD $1,150,000 in legal fees "rel[ying] on Rule 38.2" of the PAC Rules. **There is no Rule 38.2 bearing on costs.** Rule 38 is "Exclusion of Liability." The correct provisions are **Rule 34.1(d)** (costs of the arbitration include "the reasonable fees and expenses of counsel, witnesses, and experts retained by the parties") and **Rule 35.2** ("The Tribunal may allocate costs of the arbitration, including reasonable legal fees, among the parties. In making such allocation, the Tribunal shall take into account the outcome of the case, the conduct of the parties during the proceedings, and any other circumstances it considers relevant"). The language the Award quotes is a close paraphrase of Rule 35.2. **The power plainly exists; the Tribunal cited the wrong number.**
- Expert fees of USD $87,500 fall squarely within Rule 34.1(d).
- Arbitration costs of USD $485,000: Award ¶82 refers to advances made "in accordance with PAC Rule 37.1." Rule 37 is "Confidentiality"; advances on costs are **Rule 33.1**. Again a mis-citation of a power that exists.
- **Pacífico itself asked for exactly this relief.** Its Statement of Defence, Relief Requested ¶45(5), sought an order that "Greenleaf … bear all costs of the arbitration, including Pacífico's reasonable legal fees, expert costs, translation and interpretation expenses, and travel costs." A party that asks a tribunal to shift costs has submitted costs-shifting to arbitration and cannot say the resulting award exceeded the submission. This is a complete answer and should be pleaded.

**Assessment on fees: WEAK.** The mis-citations are cosmetic. But they belong to a pattern (Section 7.3) that supports Pacífico's "careless award" narrative, and we should be first to the court with the correct rule numbers rather than letting Velázquez Ibarra present the errors as discoveries.

**5.3.5 Mexican mandatory law — not a scope question.** Pacífico argued (Defence §II.D, ¶¶18–22) that Mexican *leyes de orden público* override the choice of British Columbia law. The Tribunal rejected this at Award ¶52. Whatever its merits, this is a **choice-of-law determination on a matter squarely submitted** — the applicable law was in issue from Procedural Order No. 1 ¶3 onwards. A tribunal that decides an applicable-law question submitted to it has not exceeded its mandate; it has performed it. This argument belongs in V(2)(b) and is analysed there.

**5.3.6 Section 10.1 of the Agreement — a serious point that Pacífico has not yet taken.** I must flag this because it is the largest latent exposure in the file after the *dispositif*.

**Section 10.1 provides, in capitals: "EXCEPT FOR BREACHES OF CONFIDENTIALITY OBLIGATIONS UNDER SECTION 13 AND INDEMNIFICATION OBLIGATIONS UNDER SECTION 10.2, NEITHER PARTY SHALL BE LIABLE TO THE OTHER PARTY FOR ANY INDIRECT, INCIDENTAL, SPECIAL, CONSEQUENTIAL, OR PUNITIVE DAMAGES, INCLUDING WITHOUT LIMITATION LOSS OF PROFITS, LOSS OF REVENUE, LOSS OF BUSINESS OPPORTUNITY, LOSS OF GOODWILL, OR COST OF PROCUREMENT OF SUBSTITUTE GOODS OR SERVICES."** Section 12.4(e) provides that Section 10 survives termination.

The entire damages award is a lost-profits award. **Section 10.1 is not mentioned anywhere in the Award.** I have searched the full record: it does not appear in the Award, in Procedural Order No. 1 or No. 3, in the chronology, or in Pacífico's Statement of Defence. Neither party raised it; the Tribunal never addressed it.

Our answers, which are strong, are these. (i) **Pacífico never pleaded it.** Its quantum defence (Defence ¶¶37–40) attacked speculativeness, the margin figure, and mitigation — not the existence of a contractual bar. Rule 24.1 waiver applies, and a party cannot raise at enforcement a contractual defence it declined to run before the tribunal. (ii) **It is a merits point.** Whether a limitation clause bars a head of loss is a question of contractual construction on the substance — precisely what Article V does not permit the enforcing court to revisit. (iii) **Pacífico's own case is inconsistent with it.** Its counterclaim sought USD $1,500,000 for loss of exclusivity rights and USD $500,000 for reputational harm (Defence ¶41(a), (c)) — both of which Section 10.1 would bar as loss of business opportunity and loss of goodwill. A party that pleaded a counterclaim of that composition cannot now say the clause excludes the other side's lost profits. That inconsistency should be put squarely in our written submissions. (iv) There is a respectable construction argument that a minimum-purchase-commitment shortfall produces *direct* expectation loss rather than consequential loss, notwithstanding the clause's inclusive drafting.

**Assessment: WEAK as a Convention ground; MODERATE as a rhetorical weapon in a public-policy argument.** It is much more likely to appear in Pacífico's V(2)(b) submission ("the Tribunal awarded $8m of a category of damages the contract expressly excluded") than under V(1)(c). Prepare for it there.

**5.3.7 Years 4 and 5 — not a scope question either.** Post-termination lost profits for the unexpired term were claimed in the Statement of Claim, defended at Defence ¶38, argued by both experts, made the subject of a dedicated supplemental hearing, and decided at Award ¶¶73–74. This is the most thoroughly submitted issue in the arbitration. The dissent disagrees with the outcome; that is what dissents do. **No V(1)(c) foundation.**

**5.3.8 Assessment: MODERATE–STRONG as to the $5,169,139 increment; WEAK as to every other component.** This is the ground that matters, and the severability proviso is the tool that contains it.

### 5.4 Article V(1)(d) — Composition of the tribunal or arbitral procedure not in accordance with the parties' agreement

**5.4.1 The standard.** Enforcement may be refused where "the composition of the arbitral authority or the arbitral procedure was not in accordance with the agreement of the parties, or, failing such agreement, was not in accordance with the law of the country where the arbitration took place." As you note, the reference point is the parties' agreement **including the institutional rules they incorporated** — here, the PAC Rules (2018 Edition), incorporated by Section 14.2 and confirmed applicable by Rule 41.2 because the Request was filed on 18 January 2022.

**5.4.2 Composition — clean.** Section 14.2 required three arbitrators; three were appointed. Each party nominated one; the co-arbitrators jointly selected the President, exactly as Rule 6.2 prescribes. All three signed declarations of independence and impartiality (Award ¶6) as Rule 7.2 requires. **No challenge to any arbitrator was filed by either party at any point** (Award ¶6), and the fifteen-day window under Rule 8.2 has long closed. Rule 27.1 requires signature by the arbitrators or a majority with reasons for omission; **all three signed**, Lic. Beltrán Cisneros noting "Subject to the Dissenting Opinion appended as Annex A hereto." Rule 26.3 provides that a dissent "shall not affect the validity or enforceability of the majority decision."

I record for completeness that I have considered whether any arbitrator-impartiality issue arises — the objective "justifiable doubts" standard under UNCITRAL Model Law art 12, as elaborated in *Halliburton Company v Chubb Bermuda Insurance Ltd* [2020] UKSC 48 on overlapping appointments and the duty of disclosure, being the relevant test. **Nothing in this record supports any such argument**: no undisclosed appointments, no financial interest, no relationship with a party or counsel, no prior expressed views. The Tribunal composition is the most secure part of the Award. **Strength: none.**

**5.4.3 Procedure — the Rule 22.4 breach.** Analysed at Section 5.2.3–5.2.4 and not repeated. It arises under both V(1)(b) and V(1)(d); expect Pacífico to plead it under both, as its 20 September 2023 letter foreshadows. The V(1)(d) framing is marginally stronger for Pacífico because it does not require proof of prejudice as an element — non-compliance with the agreed procedure is the element. **But the residual discretion still bites**, and *Hebei v Polytek* is directly in point: a party that participates without objection and suffers no injustice will not be permitted to rely on the irregularity. Rule 24.1 supplies an express contractual waiver on top. **Weak in outcome; the strongest of Pacífico's procedural threads.**

**5.4.4 The joinder of the jurisdictional objection — and a citation problem.** You asked me to address this specifically, cross-referenced to the Rules.

**The Tribunal had the power.** **Rule 18.4 provides: "The Tribunal may rule on a jurisdictional objection as a preliminary question or may join the objection to the merits of the dispute. The Tribunal shall exercise its discretion in this regard having due regard to the nature of the objection, the efficiency of the proceedings, and the interests of the parties."** That is an express discretion, and Procedural Order No. 3 §2 exercised it on articulated reasons — overlap with the merits, the impossibility of resolving the mandatory-law question without a full record, and procedural economy — after a case management conference on 16 September 2022 at which both parties were heard.

**Rule 18.4 also imposes a condition, and the Tribunal complied with it.** The same rule continues: "Where the Tribunal elects to join a jurisdictional objection to the merits, it shall address jurisdiction as a threshold matter in the final award before proceeding to address the merits." The Award does exactly that: Section VI (Jurisdiction) precedes Section VII (Applicable Law) and Section VIII (Merits), and *dispositif* ¶¶1–2 dispose of jurisdiction before ¶3 reaches breach. **This is worth making expressly in the petition**, because it forecloses the only version of the joinder complaint that has any structure to it.

**But note the mis-citation.** Both Procedural Order No. 1 ¶6 and Procedural Order No. 3 §2 attribute the discretion to **"Rule 15.3."** Rule 15 is "Seat of Arbitration" and contains only 15.1 and 15.2. **There is no Rule 15.3.** Procedural Order No. 3 goes so far as to place quotation marks around a purported text of Rule 15.3 — "[t]he Tribunal may rule on jurisdictional objections as a preliminary question or may defer its ruling to the Final Award" — which does not appear in the Rules in that form. The substance closely tracks the genuine Rule 18.4, so the power was real and correctly described; only the number is wrong. Velázquez Ibarra will present this as the Tribunal relying on a rule that does not exist. **We should get there first**, in the petition, by citing Rule 18.4 as the true source and characterising the reference as an evident typographical slip in a procedural order that is not itself an award (Rule 14.4).

**5.4.5 Language of the proceedings.** Rule 16.1 makes the parties' agreement determinative and they agreed English (§14.2). Rule 16.3 required the Tribunal to arrange interpretation, which it did at the Centre's expense. **Procedure conformed to the Rules.** Weak.

**5.4.6 Form of the award — a thin point.** Rule 27.2(f) requires the operative part to state "the precise amounts awarded (if any), any interest awarded, any orders made, and **any determination regarding the allocation of costs**." The *dispositif* states a single total at ¶6 and post-award interest at ¶7 but **does not separately state the allocation of costs**, which appears only in the reasoning at ¶¶82–84. If Pacífico is thorough it will add this to its V(1)(d) list, and it interacts unhelpfully with the $14.7m problem, because the absence of any itemisation in the *dispositif* is precisely why the increment cannot be traced. Our answer is that ¶6's "total sum" necessarily subsumes the costs determined in the reasoning and that ¶8 dismisses all other relief, so the operative part is complete on its face. **Weak, but it compounds.**

**5.4.7 Other procedural steps — all compliant.** Document production followed Rule 19 with "no disputes regarding the scope or adequacy of document production … raised by either party" (Award ¶17). Closure of proceedings on 15 May 2023 followed Rule 23.1 (the Award's reference at ¶28 to "Article 32" is another mis-citation — Rule 32 is Consent Awards). The Award issued 28 June 2023, comfortably within the six months allowed by Rule 28.1. The Centre's authority to convene the supplemental hearing is Rule 22.6 (the 3 February 2023 email cites "Rule 23.1," again wrongly).

**5.4.8 Assessment: WEAK.** Composition is unassailable. The procedural case reduces to a two-day notice shortfall and an email-address administrative failure, both waived under Rule 24.1 and both without consequence. The accumulated mis-citations are embarrassing but not jurisdictional.

### 5.5 Article V(1)(e) — Award not yet binding, or set aside or suspended

**5.5.1 The standard.** Enforcement may be refused where the award "has not yet become binding on the parties, or has been set aside or suspended by a competent authority of the country in which, or under the law of which, that award was made." Three distinct limbs; each fails.

**5.5.2 Binding.** **Rule 30.1: "The award shall be final and binding on the parties from the date it is rendered."** Rule 30.2 adds that the parties "undertake to carry out any award without delay and are deemed to have waived their right to any form of appeal." The Award is binding from 28 June 2023. There is no double-*exequatur* requirement under the Convention.

**5.5.3 Set aside — and here is the answer to your specific question.** **Pacífico has not filed an application to set aside.**

The letter of 20 September 2023 from Velázquez Ibarra Abogados to the Court of King's Bench of Alberta is, in its own words, written "to advise the Honourable Court of our client's **intent to file** an application to set aside." It states: "We are **in the process of preparing** the formal application materials, including supporting affidavit evidence and a memorandum of argument, and **expect to file same** within the time period prescribed by the International Commercial Arbitration Act." It asks only "that this letter be placed on the Court's file as notice of the forthcoming application." **A letter announcing an intention to apply is not an application.** The chronology confirms: "No formal application is filed with the Court on this date."

**5.5.4 And the window has closed.** The Award was rendered on 28 June 2023. The chronology records the deadline as expiring on **26 September 2023** (calculated as 90 days). The correct measure under Model Law art 34(3), as scheduled to the Alberta ICAA, is **three months from the date on which the party received the award**, which would give **28 September 2023** if receipt is taken as the date of the Award. Nothing turns on the two-day difference: on either calculation the period expired before the end of September 2023, and no application had been filed. Art 34(3) is a hard limit; it is not subject to the court's general power to extend time. **This limb is not merely weak — it is unavailable.**

You should verify with Alberta counsel the date of actual *receipt* of the Award by Pacífico, since that is the trigger, and obtain a **registry search certificate from the Court of King's Bench of Alberta confirming that no originating application has been filed** in respect of PAC-2022-0041. That certificate is the single most valuable exhibit you can put before the *Juzgado de Distrito*. It should be obtained before filing.

**5.5.5 Suspended.** No suspension has been sought or granted anywhere.

**5.5.6 The Article VI consequence, which is worth as much as the ground itself.** Article VI permits an enforcing court to adjourn its decision, and to order security, only where an application to set aside or suspend "has been made" to a competent authority. **No application has been made.** Pacífico therefore has no route to a stay of your *homologación* proceeding, and any late-filed Alberta application would be time-barred and could not found an adjournment. This deprives Pacífico of its most effective delay tactic and should be stated affirmatively in the petition — with the registry certificate exhibited — rather than held for reply.

**5.5.7 Assessment: VERY WEAK / UNAVAILABLE.** Pacífico's four-ground letter of 20 September 2023 is best understood not as a step toward setting aside but as an attempt to manufacture a paper trail for use in Guadalajara. Characterise it that way to the court, and note that its author has allowed the seat's limitation period to lapse without acting on any of the four grounds it announced.

### 5.6 Article V(2)(a) — Subject matter not capable of settlement by arbitration

**5.6.1 The standard.** The court may refuse enforcement of its own motion where "the subject matter of the difference is not capable of settlement by arbitration under the law of that country." Arbitrability is assessed under the law of the enforcing state — here, Mexico.

**5.6.2 Analysis.** The dispute is a claim for damages for breach of an exclusive distribution agreement between two commercial companies, and a counterclaim for wrongful termination. This is core arbitrable commercial subject matter. Mexico's Código de Comercio, Book Five, Title Four is a Model Law-based regime that treats commercial disputes as arbitrable as a matter of course, and Mexico acceded to the Convention **without the commercial reservation**, which signals the breadth rather than the narrowness of its arbitrability policy.

**5.6.3 The only conceivable hook, and why it fails.** Pacífico invoked the **Ley Federal de Protección al Consumidor** (Defence ¶¶19–20; jurisdictional objection of 2 September 2022) and NOM standards. The LFPC is **consumer-protection legislation**. Pacífico is not a consumer: it is a distributor with approximately MXN $1.2bn in annual revenue, three warehouses and a refrigerated fleet (Award ¶2), which warranted at Section 9.3(c) of the Agreement that it "is familiar with the regulatory requirements, customs procedures, and market conditions applicable to the importation, distribution, and sale of health and wellness products in the United Mexican States, including the requirements of COFEPRIS and applicable NOM standards." The NOM standards regulate product labelling and safety; they do not confer exclusive judicial jurisdiction over contractual damages claims between merchants, and Pacífico has never identified any provision that does.

**5.6.4 Assessment: VERY WEAK.** But because the court may raise V(2)(a) *sua sponte*, the petition should include a short affirmative paragraph confirming arbitrability under Mexican law rather than waiting to respond. Please settle that paragraph — it is a pure question of Mexican law.

### 5.7 Article V(2)(b) — Contrary to the public policy of the enforcing State

**5.7.1 The standard.** The court may refuse enforcement of its own motion where "the recognition or enforcement of the award would be contrary to the public policy of that country." Two features govern.

*First, the applicable conception is international, not domestic, public policy.* As you note, Mexican jurisprudence distinguishes *orden público interno* from *orden público internacional*, and the Convention engages only the latter. A rule being mandatory domestically does not make its non-application contrary to international public policy; if it did, every choice of foreign law in an international contract would be vulnerable.

*Second, the threshold is very high.* The standard I would urge on the court is that of *Hebei Import & Export Corp v Polytek Engineering Co Ltd* (1999) 2 HKCFAR 111: enforcement is refused only where it would be **contrary to the fundamental conceptions of morality and justice** of the enforcing forum. *Hebei* also establishes two propositions of direct application here: that a party which failed to raise a complaint before the tribunal or at the seat may be precluded from relying on it at enforcement; and that the ground does not license review of the merits.

**5.7.2 This is the ground that genuinely worries me, for reasons of forum rather than doctrine.** On your own intelligence, the Guadalajara district courts have refused enforcement in two or three cases on *orden público* grounds under Article 1462(II) CCom over the past decade, and in at least one the trigger was the non-application of Mexican mandatory commercial law. You also warn candidly that some Mexican judges have blurred the internal/international line in practice. V(2)(b) is the only ground the court can raise itself, and the material Pacífico needs is contained in the very instruments we are obliged to file under Article IV. **We cannot keep this ground out of the case.** We can only make sure the judge encounters it framed by us.

**5.7.3 Thread one: non-application of Mexican mandatory distribution law.** Pacífico's case (Defence ¶¶18–22; jurisdictional objection; set-aside letter ¶(a)) is that the Tribunal's application of British Columbia law to the exclusion of Mexican *leyes de orden público* offends Mexican public policy.

Our answers: (i) **The parties chose British Columbia law**, freely, at Section 14.1, and Procedural Order No. 1 ¶3 records that "the parties have agreed, and the Tribunal confirms" that BC law governs. Party autonomy in the choice of governing law is itself a principle that Mexican law recognises, and Article 1445 CCom (please confirm the reference) gives effect to it. (ii) **The Tribunal did not ignore the argument; it considered and rejected it** at Award ¶52, holding that "party autonomy in the selection of governing law is a fundamental principle that the Tribunal is bound to respect" and that no extraordinary circumstances had been established. A reasoned rejection of a mandatory-law argument is an exercise of the mandate, not a breach of public policy. (iii) **Pacífico never identified a specific mandatory rule that was violated.** Its pleading asserts categories — "implied rights to adequate support from manufacturers," "the right to reasonable adjustment of performance obligations," "protections against arbitrary or bad-faith termination" (Defence ¶20(a)–(c)) — without citing a single provision. That is fatal: a public-policy objection must identify the fundamental principle offended. (iv) **The LFPC does not apply to a B2B distribution agreement** (Section 5.6.3). (v) **The distinction is dispositive**: even if Mexican distribution law would have produced a different outcome, that engages *orden público interno* at most.

*Strength: weak doctrinally, but this is the argument the local track record suggests has previously succeeded in Guadalajara. Treat it as the most dangerous framing and brief it fully, not defensively.*

**5.7.4 Thread two — and the real one: the internal inconsistency of the Award.** This is where V(1)(c) and V(2)(b) converge, and where Pacífico's strongest case lies.

The argument will be put as follows: an award whose operative part orders payment of a sum $5,169,139 greater than the sum its own reasoning calculates is not a reasoned award at all as to the excess; the excess is unreasoned, unexplained and unexplainable; enforcing it would require the Mexican court to lend the coercive power of the State to a monetary obligation for which no adjudicative justification exists anywhere in the instrument; and that offends the requirement of reasoned adjudication that is itself part of *orden público*.

I will not understate this. **It is a serious argument**, and it is materially stronger than anything else Pacífico has, for four reasons: it requires no evidence beyond the Award; it is not waivable, so Rule 24.1 does not touch it; it can be raised by the court itself; and it is intuitively compelling to a judge who has been asked to enforce a $14.7m obligation and can see $9,530,861 on the facing page.

Our answers, in order of force. (i) **The severability proviso to Article V(1)(c) exists for this**: the reasoned components are entirely separable and can be enforced while the increment is refused. (ii) **The reasoned $9,530,861 is impeccable** — I have verified every line and every subtotal (Section 3.4). Nothing about the increment infects the components that are fully reasoned. (iii) **A computational or clerical divergence is not a moral or juridical outrage.** Rule 29 characterises exactly this class of defect as "computational, clerical, or typographical," which is the vocabulary of a slip rather than of injustice. (iv) **No merits review.** *Hebei* forecloses using public policy as a vehicle for re-examining quantum. (v) **Proportionality**: refusing enforcement of the whole because part of a total is unexplained would be a disproportionate response where the court has an express severability power.

**But the fifth answer is only available if we ask for severance.** If Greenleaf insists on the full $14.7m, it invites the court to assess the Award as a single indivisible instrument — and if the court then concludes that instrument is internally irreconcilable, the severability proviso may not save us, because we will not have pleaded it. This is the analytical heart of the recommendation at Section 6.

**5.7.5 Thread three: the dissent as a public-policy exhibit.** You asked whether Pacífico can weaponise the dissent. It will certainly try. Rule 26.3 provides that a dissent "shall not affect the validity or enforceability of the majority decision," and no system treats a dissent as an enforcement defence. But that is a formal answer to a rhetorical move. Expect the dissent to be exhibited and quoted for the proposition that **Pacífico's own nominated arbitrator, a Mexican jurist, found the Years 4–5 damages "speculative and unsupported by the evidentiary record"** (Annex A ¶¶13, 20) and would have awarded USD $4.5m–$5.0m before interest and costs. Paired with the $14.7m *dispositif*, that becomes: "a member of this Tribunal thought $5m was right; the operative part says $14.7m."

Three responses, and the first is the best. (i) **Turn the dissent against Pacífico.** Lic. Beltrán Cisneros **concurred with the majority on jurisdiction** — "I concur with the majority's decision on jurisdiction — specifically, that the Tribunal has jurisdiction over this dispute and that Respondent's jurisdictional objection is properly dismissed" — **and concurred in the dismissal of Pacífico's counterclaim in its entirety** (Annex A ¶1). Pacífico's own Mexican nominee rejected the unconscionability case, rejected the mandatory-law jurisdiction case, and rejected the counterclaim. If Pacífico opens the dissent, that concurrence comes in with it. **Quote it in our petition and make Pacífico live with the whole document.** (ii) A dissent is evidence of deliberation, not of impropriety; a tribunal that produces a reasoned majority and a reasoned dissent has adjudicated properly. (iii) The dissent disputes quantum on the merits, and merits are not reviewable.

**5.7.6 Thread four: due process as public policy.** Pacífico will fold the Rule 22.4 notice defect and the language complaint into V(2)(b) to escape Rule 24.1 waiver. The answer is that *Hebei* applies the preclusion principle to public policy as well: a party that participated fully without objection cannot convert a waived procedural complaint into a public-policy objection. And the substance is unchanged — it attended, its expert testified, and it has never said what it lost.

**5.7.7 Thread five: Section 10.1 as public policy.** As set out at Section 5.3.6, expect the limitation-of-liability clause to surface here rather than under V(1)(c). Answer it with waiver, with the merits-review bar, and above all with the inconsistency of Pacífico's own counterclaim composition.

**5.7.8 Fraud — considered and excluded.** I have specifically considered whether any allegation of fraud in the procurement of the Award arises, since fraud is the classic V(2)(b) case. Applying the elements of the misrepresentation family — a false statement of existing fact, knowledge or absence of honest belief, inducement, and reliance — **nothing in this record supports any such allegation.** No party has alleged forged evidence, perjured testimony, concealed documents or improper contact with the Tribunal. Document production was completed with "no disputes regarding the scope or adequacy … raised by either party" (Award ¶17). This avenue is closed to Pacífico and I do not expect it to be attempted.

**5.7.9 Assessment: MODERATE — the highest of any ground, and the only one the court can raise itself.** Doctrinally it should fail on every thread. Practically, in this forum, on this Award, it is the live risk, and the *dispositif* discrepancy is what animates it.

## 6. THE *DISPOSITIF* DISCREPANCY: ANALYSIS AND RECOMMENDATION

### 6.1 Confirmation of the arithmetic

You asked me to verify the arithmetic and confirm whether $14.7m is supported by the Tribunal's reasoning. **It is not. I have verified this and I want to state it without hedging.**

The Tribunal's reasoning produces **USD $9,530,861**, and it produces it correctly — every component and every subtotal checks (Section 3.4). The Tribunal itself says so at ¶86: "the total amount due from Respondent to Claimant is USD $9,530,861 (Nine Million Five Hundred Thirty Thousand Eight Hundred Sixty-One United States Dollars)," written out in words as well as figures. The *dispositif* at ¶6 then orders "the total sum of USD $14,700,000 (Fourteen Million Seven Hundred Thousand United States Dollars)," also in words and figures.

**The excess is USD $5,169,139 and it is not merely unexplained — it is unreconstructable.** I set out at Section 5.3.2 the six reconstructions I attempted; none produces the figure. The excess also exceeds Greenleaf's total pleaded claim of USD $12,600,000 by $2,100,000.

### 6.2 The correction route is closed

The Rule 29.1 party window (thirty days from receipt) and the Rule 29.2 tribunal window (thirty days from the date of the Award, i.e. to 28 July 2023) have both expired without action. The Tribunal is *functus officio* as to correction. **We must litigate around this defect, not fix it.**

### 6.3 A complication you should know about

Pacífico's letter of 20 September 2023 describes the reasoning as calculating "USD $4,180,000 (lost profits for Years 1–3), plus USD $3,720,861 (lost profits for Years 4–5), plus USD $1,630,000 (goodwill impairment), totaling USD $9,530,861," citing "paragraphs 187 through 214 of the Award." **That composition appears nowhere in the Award we hold.** Our Award reaches $9,530,861 by an entirely different route, awards no goodwill impairment at all, and has 87 numbered paragraphs — there is no paragraph 187. The two compositions coincidentally sum to the same total.

This matters in two ways. Defensively: **our own chronology reproduces Pacífico's composition as though it were the Award's** (Section 7.4), which must be corrected before filing. Offensively: **Pacífico's letter mis-describes the Award it purports to challenge**, and that is worth pointing out to the court as an indication of the care with which its opposition has been assembled. Please have Alberta counsel confirm there is no variant or corrected version of the Award in circulation; I consider that very unlikely but it should be excluded before we rely on the point.

### 6.4 The options

**Option A — Enforce the full USD $14,700,000.** *For*: the *dispositif* is the operative part and Rule 27.2(f) requires it to state the precise amount; where reasoning and operative part diverge, most systems give effect to the operative part; it maximises recovery; it preserves the fallback of arguing for $9,530,861 in reply. *Against*: it forces us to defend the indefensible; it hands Pacífico and the court the strongest available public-policy narrative; it invites the court to treat the Award as a single indivisible instrument, which is the analysis under which we lose everything; it makes Greenleaf appear to be exploiting an evident error, which is precisely the posture least likely to attract a favourable exercise of the residual discretion; and it forfeits the credibility dividend of candour in a forum where the judge's discretionary disposition is the main variable.

**Option B — Enforce USD $9,530,861, expressly and transparently.** *For*: it is the figure the Tribunal's own reasoning supports, stated by the Tribunal in words and figures at ¶86; it is arithmetically verifiable line by line, so the court can satisfy itself in minutes; it is exactly what the severability proviso to Article V(1)(c) contemplates; it removes the *dispositif* problem from Pacífico's V(2)(b) case at a stroke, because we are not asking the court to enforce the unexplained portion; it positions Greenleaf as the party helping the court rather than the party exploiting a slip; and it makes every remaining Pacífico ground look like what it is — a two-day notice shortfall and a stale unconscionability argument. *Against*: it concedes $5,169,139 that a court might have granted.

**Option C — Plead $14,700,000 primarily with $9,530,861 in the alternative.** *For*: preserves the upside. *Against*: it is Option A with a safety net, and it carries most of Option A's cost. The primary claim still frames the case as a demand for the unexplained sum, and the alternative reads as an admission that the primary is unsound. It is the worst posture for the discretionary stage: it looks like opportunism with a hedge.

### 6.5 Recommendation

**Seek USD $9,530,861 as the principal sum, plus post-award interest at 5% per annum simple from 28 June 2023 to the date of payment.**

Present it this way in the petition:

1. **Volunteer the discrepancy in the petition itself**, before Pacífico raises it. State plainly that the operative part records $14,700,000, that the Tribunal's reasoning at ¶¶72–86 calculates $9,530,861, and that Greenleaf, in the interest of presenting the court with an obligation that is fully supported by the Tribunal's stated reasons, seeks enforcement of the reasoned figure only.
2. **Invoke the severability proviso of Article V(1)(c) expressly**, in terms: the reasoned components are separable and enforceable, and Greenleaf does not seek recognition of any part of the operative sum that exceeds them.
3. **Exhibit the line-by-line reconciliation** at Section 3.4 as a schedule to the petition, so the judge can verify each component against the Award's paragraph numbers without doing arithmetic.
4. **Formally waive and abandon the excess** so that the abandonment is unambiguous and cannot be re-litigated.
5. Do not seek pre-award interest separately — it is already inside the $9,530,861 at ¶80. Seek post-award interest only from 28 June 2023 per *dispositif* ¶7. **Do not follow the chronology's erroneous description of interest running "from the date of termination"** (Section 7.4); that would be an overreach visible on the face of the Award and would undo the credibility this strategy is designed to build.

**The reasoning behind this recommendation.** The $5.17m increment is worth having only if the attempt to obtain it does not endanger the $9.53m. In this forum it does. Article V(2)(b) is the one ground the court may raise on its own motion; the *dispositif* discrepancy is the one fact capable of activating it; and the Guadalajara bench has, on your own account, refused enforcement on *orden público* grounds within the last decade. Trading a 35–45% chance at $5.17m for a materially higher probability on $9.53m is straightforwardly the better expected outcome, and it also buys the discretionary goodwill that will help us dispose of the Rule 22.4 point and everything else.

There is also a candour dimension that I put to you directly. We must file the complete Award under Article IV. The discrepancy will be in front of the judge whatever we do. The only question is whether Greenleaf is the party that pointed it out or the party that hoped it would not be noticed. In a proceeding whose outcome turns substantially on judicial discretion, that is not a small question.

**This is Greenleaf's decision, not ours.** Please put the analysis to Ms. Tsai with the $5,169,139 figure stated plainly, so that the election is made on the merits and recorded.

### 6.6 If Greenleaf instructs otherwise

If Greenleaf elects to pursue the full $14,700,000, then: plead the reasoned $9,530,861 as an **express alternative** in the petition itself rather than reserving it for reply; disclose the discrepancy in the petition regardless, because concealment is worse than the claim; lead with Rule 27.2(f) and the primacy of the operative part; and brief the severability proviso fully so that the court has the partial-enforcement route available even if it rejects the primary case. Under no circumstances should the petition be filed without addressing the discrepancy on its face.

## 8. CUMULATIVE RISK ASSESSMENT

**8.1 Applying the framework.** Article V(1) is a disjunctive gateway: Pacífico needs one ground, not a majority. Grounds are therefore assessed individually and the perimeter is set at the strongest.

- **V(1)(a), V(1)(e), V(2)(a): effectively dead.** V(1)(e) is not merely weak but unavailable, and its unavailability also forecloses Article VI relief.
- **V(1)(b) and V(1)(d): live but losing.** The Rule 22.4 breach is real. It is defeated by participation, express waiver under Rule 24.1, absence of prejudice, and the residual discretion.
- **V(1)(c): live as to the increment only.** Contained by the severability proviso — **if we invoke it.**
- **V(2)(b): the actual risk.** Court-raisable, unwaivable, and animated by a defect visible on the face of the instrument we must file.

**8.2 Probabilities.** These are judgements, not calculations, and they assume the strategy at Section 6.5 is adopted.

| Outcome | Probability |
|---|---|
| Enforcement of USD $9,530,861 (full reasoned amount) | **80–85%** |
| Enforcement of a reduced amount (partial severance beyond the increment) | 8–12% |
| Refusal of enforcement in full | **5–8%** |

If Option A is pursued instead, my estimate of full refusal rises to roughly 12–18%, and the probability of recovering at least $9,530,861 falls to roughly 70–75%. **The increment is not worth those numbers.**

**8.3 Timing.** Expect Pacífico to litigate for delay rather than victory: opposition in the *incidente*, then *amparo indirecto* against an adverse ruling. Article VI is unavailable to it, which removes the cheapest delay mechanism. Its assets are substantial, immovable and locatable — three distribution warehouses in Guadalajara, Monterrey and Mexico City, plus a refrigerated fleet (Award ¶2) — so execution should be practical once recognition is obtained.
