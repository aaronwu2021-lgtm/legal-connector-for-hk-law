## IV. THE APPLICABLE STANDARD

### A. The primary source: the ICC Rules

17. The challenge is made under **Article 14(1)** of the ICC Rules, which permits a challenge "whether for an alleged lack of impartiality or independence, or otherwise", made by written statement to the Secretariat specifying the facts and circumstances relied upon. Under **Article 14(3)**, the Court decides on admissibility and, if necessary, on the merits, after the Secretariat has given the arbitrator, the other party and the other members of the tribunal an opportunity to comment.

18. The content of the obligation Dr. Helvétius owed is set by **Article 11(2)** of the ICC Rules, which required him, before appointment, to disclose in writing to the Secretariat "any facts or circumstances which might be of such a nature as to call into question the arbitrator's independence in the eyes of the parties, as well as any circumstances that could give rise to reasonable doubts as to the arbitrator's impartiality". **Article 11(3)** makes that duty a continuing one throughout the arbitration. Dr. Helvétius expressly acknowledged both obligations in Sections V and VII of the Disclosure Statement and expressly submitted his Statement "in accordance with Article 11(2) of the ICC Rules... and the Note to Parties and Arbitral Tribunals on the Conduct of the Arbitration", which itself identifies the categories of relationship — with parties, with affiliates, with counsel, and prior appointments — that a prospective arbitrator is expected to consider and disclose.

19. Two features of Article 11(2) matter here. It is framed by reference to what "might" call independence into question **in the eyes of the parties** — a deliberately low and party-facing threshold for *disclosure*, distinct from and broader than the objective threshold for *disqualification*. And it is unqualified: it does not exempt relationships the arbitrator personally regards as immaterial. It was not open to Dr. Helvétius to substitute his own assessment of materiality for the parties' entitlement to make that assessment themselves.

### B. The lex arbitri: Swiss law

20. The seat is Zurich. Chapter 12 of the Swiss Federal Act on Private International Law of 18 December 1987 ("**PILA**") therefore supplies the mandatory law of the arbitration. Article 179(6) PILA requires a person who is to serve as arbitrator to disclose without delay the existence of circumstances that could give rise to justifiable doubts as to their independence or impartiality. Article 180(1)(c) PILA permits an arbitrator to be challenged "if circumstances exist that give rise to justifiable doubts as to their independence or impartiality". The Swiss courts apply that standard objectively, by reference to appearance rather than proof of actual partiality.

21. This is not merely of academic interest to the Court. Under Article 190(2)(a) PILA, an award may be set aside where the arbitral tribunal was not properly constituted. A tribunal presided over by an arbitrator whose independence is open to justifiable doubt on facts he failed to disclose exposes any eventual award — in a dispute worth €78 million plus lost profits — to annulment risk at the seat. The interests of the parties, of the Court and of the integrity of the process all point the same way at this early stage, when the tribunal has taken no procedural step and replacement costs nothing but time.

### C. The internationally uniform formulation

22. The "justifiable doubts" formula is not peculiar to Swiss law. Article 12(2) of the UNCITRAL Model Law on International Commercial Arbitration provides that an arbitrator may be challenged "only if circumstances exist that give rise to justifiable doubts as to his impartiality or independence", and Article 12(1) imposes the corresponding continuing duty of disclosure. That formulation has been adopted across the Model Law world, including in Hong Kong (Arbitration Ordinance, Cap. 609) and Singapore, the Respondent's own jurisdiction. The standard the Claimant invokes is therefore the standard the Respondent's own courts would apply.

23. The leading modern statement of the principle in a common-law jurisdiction is *Halliburton Company v Chubb Bermuda Insurance Ltd* [2020] UKSC 48. The Claimant relies on it as persuasive comparative authority only — the seat is Zurich and English law does not govern — but three of its propositions are of general application and are directly in point:

   (a) the test is **objective**: whether the fair-minded and informed observer, having considered the facts, would conclude that there is a real possibility of bias;

   (b) an arbitrator is under a **legal duty**, not merely a matter of best practice, to disclose facts and circumstances which would or might reasonably give rise to the appearance of bias; and

   (c) **a failure to disclose is itself a matter the objective observer takes into account** in assessing whether the appearance of bias arises, because non-disclosure may suggest a lack of even-handedness or a want of the candour on which the parties' confidence depends.

24. The Claimant does not overstate *Halliburton*. The challenge in that case ultimately failed, in significant part because the arbitrator's non-disclosure concerned overlapping appointments in a specialist market where the disclosure practice was then unsettled, the arbitrator had acted in good faith, and there was no financial relationship between the arbitrator and any party. The present case is materially different in each of those respects, as Section X explains.

### D. The structure of the test: a single weighted assessment

25. It is important to be precise about the *form* the governing test takes, because the parties' submissions are apt to be structured around the IBA Guidelines' lists in a way that obscures it.

26. The "justifiable doubts" standard is **not a conjunctive test** with elements each of which must be established, and it is **not a closed disjunctive gateway** under which only enumerated categories of connection can qualify. It is a **single, objective, weighted assessment** of the totality of the circumstances, made from the standpoint of a reasonable and informed third party. Three consequences follow, each of which is material to how the Court should approach this challenge:

   (a) **No single circumstance need be independently dispositive.** It is not an answer to the challenge that each ground, taken in isolation, might be characterised as falling short. The assessment is of the accumulated weight of everything the observer knows.

   (b) **Conversely, the absence of any one factor is not exculpatory.** That Dr. Helvétius has no direct contractual relationship with Pacifica itself, and no interest in the outcome of the dispute, does not dispose of the question. It removes one factor from the scale; it does not empty it.

   (c) **The factors do not carry equal weight.** Undisclosed financial benefit received from, or by reason of, a party's corporate group, and failure of disclosure itself, sit at the heavy end of the scale. Repeat appointments involving the same counsel, and professional relationships with counsel, carry substantial but lesser weight. Previously expressed views on a topic carry moderate weight only. Established custom in a particular field carries the least weight of all — and, importantly, ordinarily operates as a *mitigating* consideration, not an aggravating one. The Claimant has structured its grounds, and the weighing exercise in Section X, accordingly.

### E. The IBA Guidelines: indicative, not determinative

27. The IBA Guidelines on Conflicts of Interest in International Arbitration (2014, as revised 2024) are the accepted soft-law framework and the Claimant invokes them. But their status must be stated accurately. They are indicative guidance, not the legal test; their lists are expressly non-exhaustive; and the Guidelines themselves make clear that a failure to disclose does not *necessarily* mean that a conflict exists or that disqualification must follow. The Claimant therefore does not argue that non-disclosure is automatically disqualifying. It argues that non-disclosure is the heaviest single factor in the weighing exercise the governing standard requires, and that here it is not one omission but a pattern of four, coupled with an affirmative denial.

28. The Guidelines nonetheless supply four propositions the Claimant relies on directly:

   (a) **General Standard 2(b)–(c):** justifiable doubts exist where a reasonable third person, having knowledge of the relevant facts and circumstances, would reach the conclusion that there is a likelihood that the arbitrator may be influenced by factors other than the merits of the case; and doubts are justifiable where, among other things, the arbitrator has a significant financial interest in a party.

   (b) **General Standard 3(a):** the arbitrator must disclose facts or circumstances that may, **in the eyes of the parties**, give rise to doubts as to impartiality or independence — a broader and more party-facing test than the objective test for removal. An arbitrator may not decline to disclose on the basis of his own view that the circumstance is immaterial.

   (c) **The Guidelines' express direction that any doubt as to whether to disclose is to be resolved in favour of disclosure.**

   (d) **General Standard 7:** the arbitrator is expected to make reasonable enquiries to identify conflicts, and is deemed to know circumstances a reasonable enquiry would have revealed. Every fact relied upon in this challenge was found in a public register, a published annual report, a public case database or a published event programme, by the Claimant's counsel, in nine days.

29. Where the Claimant identifies the relevant Orange List categories below, it does so to locate each circumstance within the accepted taxonomy — not as a substitute for the weighing exercise the governing standard requires.

## V. THE DISCLOSURE STATEMENT AND ITS DEFICIENCIES

30. Dr. Helvétius's Disclosure Statement (Exhibit C-7) is dated 2 May 2025, signed at Geneva, and was circulated on 5 May 2025. It made three disclosures: the 2019–2020 LCIA arbitration (Ref. No. 204719) in which Tan Wei & Okafor LLP acted for a party; the article co-authored with Prof. Liang Chen in the *Journal of International Arbitration* (Vol. 38, Issue 4, 2021); and the coincidence of his Swiss nationality with the Zurich seat.

31. The Claimant does not criticise those three disclosures. Taken alone, none founds a challenge, and the Claimant does not advance any of them as a ground.

32. The difficulty lies in what accompanied them. Section V of the Disclosure Statement contains not an omission but an affirmative, unqualified representation, set out in full at paragraph 2 above and repeated here in its operative parts: "**I have no relationship, whether direct or indirect, with either of the parties, their affiliates, subsidiaries, or parent companies, or with any entity within their respective corporate groups**"; and "**I have not previously advised either party, or any entity within either party's corporate group, in any capacity whatsoever**." Section V continues: "Subject to the specific disclosures set forth in Section VI below, I have no past or present relationship with any of the parties, **their counsel**, or the co-arbitrators that could bear on my independence or impartiality." The declaration was thus expressly extended to relationships with counsel, and expressly extended to indirect relationships and to affiliates, subsidiaries and parent companies.

33. Three features of the drafting deserve the Court's attention.

34. **The denial is deliberately comprehensive.** Dr. Helvétius did not confine himself to denying a relationship with Pacifica. He denied any relationship, "whether direct or indirect", with any entity in Pacifica's corporate group, and any prior advisory engagement, "in any capacity whatsoever", with any such entity. Having chosen to frame the denial that widely, he assumed the corresponding obligation of accuracy.

35. **The one disclosure concerning counsel is narrowly qualified.** In Disclosure 1, Dr. Helvétius stated: "I have had no further professional engagement with Tan Wei & Okafor LLP **in connection with that matter** since the conclusion of the proceedings" (emphasis added). The qualifying words confine the assurance to the LCIA matter. Two subsequent engagements involving the same firm fall outside it. Whether or not that formulation was chosen deliberately, its effect was to leave the reader with the impression of a single, historic, concluded contact with the Respondent's counsel, when in fact there had been three engagements, the most recent concluding less than two years before the appointment.

36. **The disclosure of the LCIA matter establishes awareness.** Dr. Helvétius identified prior involvement with Tan Wei & Okafor LLP as a category requiring disclosure. He cannot now say that the category did not occur to him. The question is not whether he understood the obligation, but why he satisfied it in respect of one matter out of three.

37. Against that Statement, the Claimant sets the four categories of undisclosed circumstance addressed in Grounds 1 to 3 below.

## VI. GROUND 1 — UNDISCLOSED FIVE-YEAR COMPENSATED ADVISORY BOARD SERVICE AT NORTHVALE PARTNERS AG, A FIRM ADVISING A RESPONDENT AFFILIATE

Ltd. ... since March 2020, providing cross-border investment counsel with a focus on **European industrial and technology sector opportunities**... The advisory relationship has expanded steadily since inception, reflecting the breadth of Pacifica Holdings Group's European investment programme." The two therefore overlapped for approximately **three years and nine months**, throughout which he was paid CHF 45,000 a year and, on the Report's own account, briefed periodically on the firm's client portfolio.

43. **The proximity to this dispute.** Pacifica Capital Advisors is the Pacifica group's investment arm, and — as pleaded in the Request for Arbitration (Exhibit C-1) — the entity that structured and financed Pacifica Industrial Solutions Ltd.'s participation in the Meridian Automation joint venture. The JVA was executed on 12 June 2021, squarely within the overlap period. The mandate Northvale held for that entity was, in Northvale's own words, "cross-border investment counsel with a focus on European industrial and technology sector opportunities". The joint venture in dispute is a European industrial and technology investment. Mr. Gruber's Managing Director's letter in the same Report speaks of "deepening relationships with key strategic clients, including those in the **industrial, maritime, and financial services** sectors across Southeast Asia" — the precise composition of the Pacifica Holdings Group.

44. **Corroboration: the AutoBuild co-investment.** The Swiss Commercial Register extract for AutoBuild Technologies SA (Route de Chêne 30, 1208 Geneva; UID CHE-412.758.319), issued at Geneva on 12 May 2025 under Extract Ref. CH-660.0.483.927-4 (Exhibit C-15), records in its register of shareholders: **Dr. Marcus Helvétius, Helvétius Arbitration Chambers, Rue du Rhône 118, 1204 Geneva — 350 shares — 3.5% — registered 22 March 2022**; and **Stefan Gruber, Managing Director, Northvale Partners AG, Bahnhofstrasse 67, 8001 Zurich — 800 shares — 8.0% — registered 22 March 2022**. The journal entry for 22 March 2022 records that both holdings arose from the *same* seed investment round, in which the company's 1,150 treasury shares were issued to Mr. Gruber and Dr. Helvétius together. Following the Series A round of 14 June 2023 at a pre-money valuation of CHF 4,000,000, Dr. Helvétius's stake is worth approximately **CHF 140,000**.

### B. Why it required disclosure, and why it matters

45. The Claimant advances the co-investment as corroboration within this ground, not as a freestanding ground. Standing alone, a 3.5% minority holding in an unrelated Geneva proptech start-up, several links removed from any party, would not sustain a challenge, and the Claimant does not suggest otherwise. Its significance is different and narrower: it shows that the relationship between Dr. Helvétius and Mr. Gruber was not confined to a formal seat on an advisory board. The two men subscribed for shares in the same private company, in the same round, on the same day. That is the relationship of co-investors, and it bears on how the objective observer would characterise the Northvale connection — as a genuine and personal professional association rather than a nominal appointment. It is also notable that Northvale's own Annual Report discloses Mr. Gruber's AutoBuild holding (Section IV.A) while recording that "no material conflicts of interest requiring remedial action were identified" during 2023; the fact that a sitting member of its Advisory Board was a co-shareholder appears nowhere.

46. **The circumstance falls squarely within the disclosure obligation.** Article 11(2) of the ICC Rules required disclosure of anything that "might" call independence into question "in the eyes of the parties". A five-year paid appointment at a firm whose declared key client is a wholly owned subsidiary of the Respondent's ultimate parent — a subsidiary that structured the Respondent's participation in the joint venture in dispute — plainly satisfies that threshold. Within the IBA taxonomy, the circumstance engages the Orange List categories addressing an arbitrator's professional association and business relationships with a party or an affiliate within the preceding three years, and his financial interests. The Claimant accepts that no listed item maps onto these facts with perfect precision, because Northvale is not itself a Pacifica affiliate but a service provider to one. That is a reason to apply the governing standard directly rather than to search the lists for a label: the lists are expressly non-exhaustive, and General Standard 3(a) requires disclosure of anything that may, in the eyes of the parties, give rise to doubts.

47. **The financial dimension is the heaviest element on the scale.** This is not an unpaid honorific. Dr. Helvétius received CHF 225,000 in fees from Northvale, and holds an interest valued at approximately CHF 140,000 in a company in which Northvale's Managing Director is a fellow shareholder. Financial benefit connected, however indirectly, to a party's corporate group carries the greatest weight in the objective assessment, because it is the factor most apt to suggest that an arbitrator might be influenced by something other than the merits.

48. **The Claimant is careful about what this shows.** There is no evidence that Dr. Helvétius personally advised on the Pacifica mandate, that he knew of the Meridian joint venture before his appointment, or that he received any benefit referable to Pacifica. The Claimant does not allege any of those things. The point is structural and it is about appearance and candour: an arbitrator who has been paid CHF 225,000 over five years by a firm that advises the opposing side's corporate investment arm — and who was briefed periodically on that firm's client portfolio — cannot truthfully tell the parties that he has "no relationship, whether direct or indirect, with... their affiliates... or with any entity within their respective corporate groups", and cannot leave them to discover the position for themselves from a published annual report.

49. **The relationship is recent.** The appointment ended on 31 December 2023: fourteen and a half months before the Request for Arbitration was filed on 14 March 2025, and sixteen months before Dr. Helvétius was appointed on 28 April 2025. It is comfortably within any temporal window used in practice to identify disclosable circumstances.

## VII. GROUND 2 — UNDISCLOSED REPEAT APPOINTMENTS IN ARBITRATIONS IN WHICH THE RESPONDENT'S COUNSEL ACTED

*(Discovered 9 May 2025. Exhibits C-11 and C-12.)*

### A. The facts

50. Dr. Helvétius has sat as arbitrator in at least **three** arbitrations in which Tan Wei & Okafor LLP, the Respondent's counsel in this reference, acted as counsel for a party. He disclosed **one**.

| | Matter | Role | Subject / value | Period | Outcome | Disclosed? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | LCIA Ref. No. 204719 | Sole arbitrator | Commodities trading | 2019–2020 | Final award 3 September 2020 | **Yes** (Exhibit C-7, Disclosure 1) |
| 2 | SCC Case No. V 2021/038 | Sole arbitrator | Construction, ~USD 15m | 2021–2022 | Final award 18 November 2022 | **No** (Exhibit C-11) |
| 3 | Ad hoc, seated in London | Arbitrator | Supply chain disruption, ~GBP 8m | 2022–2023 | Settled 12 July 2023 | **No** (Exhibit C-12) |

51. The SCC matter was identified on 9 May 2025 from the SCC's publicly available case records (Exhibit C-11). The ad hoc London matter was identified the same day from arbitration database and counsel records, including a published settlement notice (Exhibit C-12). If Dr. Helvétius accepts appointment in the present reference, it will be the fourth matter in six years in which he sits and the Respondent's counsel appears.

52. The three matters are consecutive rather than remote: LCIA 2019–2020, SCC 2021–2022, ad hoc London 2022–2023. The most recent concluded on 12 July 2023, twenty-two months before Dr. Helvétius's appointment in this reference. In at least two of the three he sat as **sole** arbitrator, a form of appointment that ordinarily reflects a higher degree of individual selection and confidence than appointment to a three-member tribunal.

### B. Why it required disclosure, and why it matters

53. Repeat appointments involving the same counsel are among the paradigm disclosable circumstances. The IBA Orange List addresses them expressly, both in the category concerned with repeat appointments by a party or an affiliate and in the category concerned with the arbitrator's relationship with counsel, including appointments by the same counsel or law firm within the preceding three years. The Due Diligence Memorandum at Exhibit C-17 classifies the present facts under Orange List item 3.3.7.

54. The Claimant is candid about the limits of the taxonomy. The strict three-year window used in the Orange List does not capture all three matters, and the Claimant does not assert that the numerical threshold in any particular item is met on these facts. That is not the end of the analysis, for two reasons. First, the lists are non-exhaustive and the disclosure obligation in General Standard 3(a) and Article 11(2) is not confined to listed items. Second, and more importantly, the Court is not applying a threshold test but weighing circumstances. Three appointments in six years involving the same firm, two of them as sole arbitrator, is a fact a reasonable third party would want to know when assessing whether the presiding arbitrator approaches the Respondent's counsel with complete detachment.

55. **The decisive feature of this ground is not the number of appointments but the selectivity of the disclosure.** In *Halliburton*, the arbitrator's non-disclosure was assessed against a market practice that was, at the time, genuinely unsettled, and the arbitrator was found to have acted in good faith. Here there is no such uncertainty, because Dr. Helvétius resolved the question himself: he identified the LCIA matter as disclosable and disclosed it. Having done so, the omission of two later matters involving the same firm admits of only two explanations. Either he did not conduct a reasonable enquiry into his own prior appointments — which, for an arbitrator who has sat in more than one hundred and twenty proceedings and who by definition keeps records of them, is difficult to accept, and which in any event does not assist him, since General Standard 7 requires such an enquiry — or he made a judgment to disclose the oldest and least significant of the three and omit the two more recent ones. Neither explanation is consistent with the candour the disclosure regime requires.

56. The practical effect of the selective disclosure was to present the parties with a materially misleading picture. One historic, concluded appointment involving opposing counsel looks like the ordinary incidence of a small professional market. Three appointments in six years, two as sole arbitrator, the most recent concluding twenty-two months ago, looks different. The parties were shown the first picture and were entitled to the second.

## VIII. GROUND 3 — UNDISCLOSED PAID KEYNOTE AT A FORUM ORGANISED, SPONSORED AND FUNDED BY THE RESPONDENT'S ULTIMATE PARENT

*(Discovered 10 May 2025. Exhibits C-13 and C-14.)*

### A. The facts

57. On **7 November 2024**, Dr. Helvétius delivered the keynote address at the **Asia-Pacific Industrial Innovation Forum 2024**, held at the Marina Bay Sands Convention Centre, 10 Bayfront Avenue, Singapore. The event programme (Exhibit C-13) states on its face, beneath the Pacifica Holdings Group logo: "**Platinum Sponsor and Organizer: Pacifica Holdings Group**", and in the sponsor acknowledgments: "Platinum Sponsor and Organizer — **Pacifica Holdings Group (Singapore) — 12 Marina Boulevard, #28-01, Singapore 018982**." That is the Respondent's own registered address. The programme's closing note records that "The Asia-Pacific Industrial Innovation Forum is an annual event **organised and funded by Pacifica Holdings Group, Singapore**."

58. The Welcome Message from **Ms. Margaret Tan, Group Chief Executive Officer of Pacifica Holdings Group**, expressly identifies the group's "family of companies — including **Pacifica Industrial Solutions Ltd.**, Pacifica Capital Advisors Pte. Ltd., and Pacifica Maritime Engineering Pte. Ltd." The Respondent is therefore named on the face of the programme as a member of the organising group.

59. Dr. Helvétius's keynote ran from 10:00 to 10:45 and was entitled "**Navigating Cross-Border Joint Venture Disputes: Lessons from Recent Arbitral Practice**". The programme's own description of the address states that he "will draw on his extensive arbitral experience to examine the key challenges faced by parties in cross-border joint venture disputes, including issues of **technology transfer**, **capital contribution defaults**, and the interplay between **contractual termination mechanisms** and dispute resolution."

60. Those three subjects are the three pleaded limbs of this arbitration: unauthorised technology transfers; three defaulted capital calls totalling €12.4 million; and termination of the JVA under Article 14.2.

61. **The Respondent's lead counsel was on the same platform.** The session immediately following the keynote — Panel Discussion I, 10:45 to 12:00, "The Future of Smart Manufacturing in Southeast Asia" — was chaired by "**David Okafor, Lead Partner, Tan Wei & Okafor LLP (Singapore/London)**" (Exhibit C-13). Mr. Okafor is the Respondent's lead counsel in this reference. The programme further records that "Speakers and panellists are invited to a **private luncheon hosted by Pacifica Holdings Group in the VIP Lounge, Level 5**" — an event to which both Dr. Helvétius, as keynote speaker, and Mr. Okafor, as panel chair, were invited. A further panellist that afternoon was Mr. Raymond Teo, Head of Corporate Strategy of **Pacifica Capital Advisors Pte. Ltd.** — the same Pacifica affiliate that is Northvale's key client under Ground 1.

62. **Dr. Helvétius was paid.** Fee payment records (Exhibit C-14) show a speaking fee of **SGD 18,000** together with **SGD 7,500** in travel and accommodation, **SGD 25,500** in total, paid by or through the organiser, which the public record identifies as Pacifica Holdings Group.

63. **The timing.** The Forum took place on 7 November 2024: after all three capital call defaults (15 January, 1 May and 15 September 2023); **twenty-three days before** Redstone served its notice of termination on 30 November 2024; approximately **four months before** the Request for Arbitration was filed on 14 March 2025; and approximately **five and a half months before** Dr. Helvétius's appointment on 28 April 2025.

64. None of this was disclosed.

### B. Why it required disclosure, and why it matters

65. **The payment is the core of this ground.** Speaking at conferences is a normal part of an arbitrator's professional life, and the Claimant does not suggest otherwise. What requires disclosure is not the speech but the *payment*: SGD 25,500 received, directly or through the organiser, from the ultimate parent of a party, for an engagement at an event that the parent organised, funded and branded as its own, five and a half months before appointment. That is a financial benefit flowing from the Respondent's corporate group to the presiding arbitrator, and financial benefit is the category of circumstance that carries the greatest weight in the objective assessment.

66. **The professional interaction with the Respondent's lead counsel is a separate element.** Dr. Helvétius and Mr. Okafor appeared consecutively on the same platform, at the same event, and were both invited to the same private luncheon hosted by the Respondent's parent. Within the IBA taxonomy, this engages the Orange List categories concerned with professional relationships between an arbitrator and counsel. The Claimant does not suggest that the two men are friends or that anything improper occurred; it says only that a recent, funded, shared professional appearance with opposing counsel, hosted by the opposing party's parent, is a circumstance the parties were entitled to know about.

67. **The subject-matter congruence heightened the duty to disclose; the Claimant does not advance it as prejudgment.** The Claimant makes this limitation expressly, because the point is capable of being overstated and it declines to overstate it. It has no transcript, note or recording of the keynote. It does not allege that Dr. Helvétius expressed any view on any issue in this dispute, still less that he has prejudged one. The Orange List category addressing an arbitrator who has publicly advocated a specific position *regarding the case* is not engaged, and general commentary on a legal topic falls on the Green List (item 4.1.1). Previously expressed views carry, at most, moderate weight in the weighing exercise, and the Claimant claims no more than that.

68. The congruence nonetheless has real significance for the *disclosure* question, which is judged on the broader, party-facing standard. An arbitrator invited to accept a fee from a corporate group to speak, in that group's own forum, on cross-border joint venture disputes involving technology transfer, capital contribution defaults and contractual termination — who is then appointed, five and a half months later, to preside over a cross-border joint venture dispute between that group's subsidiary and its partner concerning technology transfer, capital contribution defaults and contractual termination — is in a position where any doubt about disclosure must be resolved in favour of disclosure. There was no such doubt here; there was no disclosure at all.

## IX. GROUND 4 — THE PATTERN OF NON-DISCLOSURE AND THE AFFIRMATIVE MISSTATEMENT

69. The Claimant advances the failure of disclosure as a distinct ground, and as the weightiest single factor in the assessment.

70. **The scale of the omission.** Dr. Helvétius disclosed three matters, all innocuous, and omitted four categories of circumstance, all material:

   (a) a five-year compensated advisory board appointment at a firm advising the Respondent's investment affiliate, worth CHF 225,000 in fees (Exhibits C-9, C-10);

   (b) two arbitrations in which the Respondent's counsel acted, one as sole arbitrator (Exhibits C-11, C-12);

   (c) a paid keynote at the Respondent's parent's own forum, worth SGD 25,500, alongside the Respondent's lead counsel (Exhibits C-13, C-14); and

   (d) a shareholding acquired in the same round, on the same day, as the Managing Director of the firm in (a) (Exhibit C-15).

71. **The affirmative denial.** Non-disclosure here did not take the form of silence. Dr. Helvétius made a positive representation that he had "no relationship, whether direct or indirect" with any entity in the Respondent's corporate group and had "not previously advised" any such entity "in any capacity whatsoever". A party reading that sentence would understand that no enquiry of its own was necessary. The Claimant made the enquiry regardless, and found the position to be otherwise.

72. **Every fact was publicly available.** The Northvale appointment and the Pacifica client relationship were in a published annual report and a FINMA filing. The SCC case was in a public case database. The event programme was published. The AutoBuild shareholding was in the Geneva Commercial Register, obtainable on payment of CHF 30. The Claimant's counsel assembled all of it from public sources between 5 and 14 May 2025. General Standard 7 required Dr. Helvétius to make reasonable enquiries; whatever enquiry he made did not surface facts that nine days of public-source research produced.

73. **What weight this bears, stated precisely.** The Claimant does not submit that non-disclosure is automatically disqualifying; the IBA Guidelines say in terms that it is not, and the governing standard admits of no automatic disqualifiers. The Claimant submits that non-disclosure is the heaviest factor in the weighing exercise, for the reason identified in *Halliburton*: the objective observer takes an arbitrator's failure to disclose into account in assessing appearance, because the parties' confidence in a tribunal rests on the arbitrator's candour about his own connections. Where the omissions are multiple, varied, all in one direction, and accompanied by an unqualified denial, the observer's concern is not merely that particular facts were withheld. It is that the arbitrator's own assessment of what the parties are entitled to know has been shown to be unreliable — and it is that assessment on which the parties must otherwise depend for the remainder of the proceedings, given the continuing duty under Article 11(3) that Dr. Helvétius expressly accepted in Section VII of the Disclosure Statement.

74. The Claimant notes, without inviting any inference of bad faith, that the Disclosure Statement has not been supplemented in the fourteen days since it was circulated.

## X. THE OBJECTIVE OBSERVER'S OVERALL ASSESSMENT

75. Standing back, the Court is asked to weigh the following, as a reasonable and informed third party would see them together:

   (a) **Undisclosed financial benefit connected to the Respondent's group** — CHF 225,000 in fees from a firm advising the Respondent's investment affiliate over an overlap of three years and nine months, and SGD 25,500 received from the Respondent's ultimate parent five and a half months before appointment;

   (b) **An undisclosed continuing financial association** with the Managing Director of that firm, in a company in which both subscribed in the same round on 22 March 2022, valued at approximately CHF 140,000;

   (c) **An undisclosed pattern of appointments involving the Respondent's counsel** — three matters in six years, two as sole arbitrator, only the oldest disclosed;

   (d) **An undisclosed recent professional appearance alongside the Respondent's lead counsel**, at the Respondent's parent's own funded event, on a topic congruent with this dispute; and

   (e) **An affirmative and unqualified denial** that no relationship, direct or indirect, existed with any entity in the Respondent's group.

76. The Claimant anticipates four answers and addresses each.

77. **"Each ground is individually insufficient."** This misstates the test. The standard is not a series of thresholds each of which must independently be crossed; it is a single weighted assessment of the totality. The Claimant does not need any ground to be independently dispositive, and expressly does not contend that the AutoBuild shareholding is. The question is whether the accumulated weight of (a) to (e) above gives rise to justifiable doubts. It does.

78. **"The connections are attenuated."** The attenuation argument has real force against the AutoBuild chain, which the Claimant advances only as corroboration for that reason. It has none against the Northvale appointment, which is not a chain at all: Dr. Helvétius was personally paid CHF 225,000 by a firm whose declared key client is the Respondent's investment affiliate, and was briefed on that firm's client portfolio. It has none against the SGD 25,500, which was paid by or through the Respondent's ultimate parent. And it has none at all against Ground 4, which concerns what the arbitrator told the parties, not how many corporate links separate him from them.

79. **"This is normal practice in international arbitration."** Custom in a particular field is the lightest of the relevant considerations, and it ordinarily operates to *reduce* the weight of a circumstance rather than to increase it — which is precisely why it cannot answer this challenge. Custom might explain why an arbitrator does not disclose every conference he has attended. It does not extend to a five-year paid advisory appointment at a firm advising the opposing side's investment arm; it does not extend to omitting two of three prior matters involving opposing counsel while disclosing the third; and it does not extend to an affirmative representation of no relationship where relationships exist. In *Halliburton*, the mitigating force of market practice was decisive because the practice was genuinely unsettled and there was no financial relationship between the arbitrator and any party. Neither condition holds here. Custom cannot be invoked to offset the two heaviest factors in the assessment — undisclosed financial benefit and failure of disclosure itself.

80. **"There is no evidence of actual bias."** There is none, and none is alleged. The standard is one of appearance, deliberately so, because proof of an arbitrator's actual state of mind is unobtainable and because the legitimacy of arbitration depends on the parties' justified confidence in the tribunal as well as on its actual rectitude. The relevant question is not whether Dr. Helvétius would in fact decide impartially. It is whether a reasonable and informed third party, knowing everything set out above and knowing that the parties were told none of it, would entertain justifiable doubts. The Claimant respectfully submits that such a person would.

81. **The balance of prejudice favours removal now.** No case management conference has been held; no procedural timetable exists; the tribunal has taken no substantive step. Replacement at this stage costs the parties a short delay and nothing else. Proceeding costs the parties, and the Court, the risk that an award in a dispute worth €78 million plus lost profits is challenged at the seat under Article 190(2)(a) PILA on the ground that the tribunal was not properly constituted. The Claimant's client has weighed that delay against the integrity of the tribunal and has instructed this challenge with full knowledge of the timetable consequences.
