## I. PRELIMINARY STATEMENT

1. Greystone does not ask this Court to second-guess an arbitrator's view of the evidence. It asks this Court to vacate an award produced by a process that failed at its foundations — before any question of merits arose.

2. **First, the arbitrator did not disclose that her son is a senior associate at the law firm representing the claimant.** Throughout the entirety of this arbitration — from a point nine months before the Request for Arbitration was filed, through her appointment, through eight days of evidentiary hearing, through the issuance of the Award — Garrett Rourke was employed as a Senior Associate at Ridgeline Chambers LLP, counsel of record for claimant Veridian Aerospace Systems, LLC ("Veridian"). He worked in the same office (900 K Street NW, Washington, D.C.) and in the same industry practice group (Aerospace & Defense — the precise subject matter of this arbitration) as Veridian's lead counsel, Marcus D. Wheaton, who serves as that group's co-chair. Arbitrator Rourke never disclosed this relationship. Worse, in her PAS Disclosure Statement of August 22, 2023, she affirmatively represented that she had "no prior personal, professional, or financial relationship with Marcus D. Wheaton or Ridgeline Chambers LLP," and certified that she had "made a diligent and thorough inquiry into all facts and circumstances that might give rise to justifiable doubts as to my impartiality or independence." Greystone had no reason to look behind that certification, and did not learn the truth until April 17, 2025 — more than a month after the Award issued.

3. **Second, the arbitrator excluded the single most probative piece of evidence on the dispositive factual question, and did so as a penalty for a delay that the opposing party caused and that she herself declined to sanction.** The central issue in the arbitration was the defect rate of a February 28, 2023 shipment of 4,200 titanium turbine blade blanks: Veridian said 12%; Greystone said 1.8%; the contractual threshold was 3%. In Procedural Order No. 3, the arbitrator found the retained physical samples "plainly relevant and material," found that denying access "would effectively deprive Respondent of any meaningful ability to rebut Claimant's metallurgical evidence," ordered Veridian to produce them by January 15, 2024, and stated that the deadline had been "specifically calibrated" so that Greystone could test the samples and incorporate the results into its expert report by the February 15, 2024 deadline. She added: "Time is of the essence." Veridian ignored the order and produced nothing until March 1, 2024 — forty-five days late. The arbitrator imposed no sanction. Greystone's expert, Dr. Nikolai Ferren, completed independent re-testing at an accredited laboratory and issued his report twenty-one days after finally receiving the samples. The arbitrator excluded that report as "untimely," reasoning that Greystone "had ample opportunity throughout the discovery period to arrange for independent testing" — a statement irreconcilable with her own Procedural Order No. 3. She rejected a continuance, considered no lesser remedy, and heard no oral argument. The excluded report found a defect rate of 2.1% — below the contractual threshold, and consistent with Greystone's 1.8%. Its exclusion did not merely weaken Greystone's case; it eliminated it, on the finding from which every other adverse holding in the Award descends.

4. **Third, the arbitrator awarded damages the parties' agreement expressly forbade her to award.** MSA Section 14.1 — the very clause from which the arbitrator's authority derives — provides that "the arbitrator shall not award consequential, punitive, or speculative damages." The arbitrator quoted that language, acknowledged "the tension between [her] characterization and the express language of Section 14.1," and then awarded $2,700,000 in penalties passed through to Veridian by its defense prime contractor anyway, on the reasoning that applying the clause as written would produce an "unreasonable result." That is not interpretation of the parties' bargain; it is substitution for it.

5. Each of these three defects is an independently sufficient statutory ground for vacatur. Section 10(a) of the FAA sets out a closed and exclusive list of grounds, and the list operates disjunctively: a petitioner who establishes any one of them is entitled to relief; it need not establish all. Greystone establishes three. Together they describe an arbitration in which the party against whom $18.7 million was awarded — and whose $21.1 million in counterclaims were denied in their entirety — was denied both a neutral decision-maker and a fair opportunity to be heard.

6. Greystone respectfully requests that the Award be vacated in its entirety and that the dispute be reheard before a new arbitrator.

Walters*, 596 U.S. 1 (2022) (a court may not "look through" a § 9 or § 10 application to the underlying dispute); *Vaden v. Discover Bank*, 556 U.S. 49 (2009). Jurisdiction here rests on diversity of citizenship, 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a)(1).

11. Greystone is a citizen of Delaware (state of incorporation) and Illinois (principal place of business). 28 U.S.C. § 1332(c)(1). Veridian is a limited liability company; its citizenship is that of each of its members. Upon information and belief, no member of Veridian is a citizen of Delaware or Illinois, and complete diversity therefore exists.

12. The amount in controversy exceeds $75,000, exclusive of interest and costs, by orders of magnitude: the Award directs payment of $18,700,000 in damages, $412,500 in allocated arbitration costs, and $137,500 in deposit equalization, and denies counterclaims valued at $21,100,000.

13. **Venue.** Venue is proper in this District under 9 U.S.C. § 10(a), which vests the power to vacate in "the United States court in and for the district wherein the award was made." The seat of the arbitration was Chicago, Illinois; the evidentiary hearing was conducted at PAS's Chicago offices; and the Award recites on its face that it "has been rendered at the seat of arbitration in Chicago, Illinois, United States of America" and that "the seat of arbitration shall determine the applicable legal framework for judicial review." 14. **Contractual forum selection.** MSA Section 14.3 provides that for "any matter that is not subject to arbitration under Section 14.1 of this Agreement, including but not limited to ... proceedings to confirm, vacate, or modify an arbitration award, the Parties irrevocably consent to the exclusive jurisdiction of the state and federal courts located in Cook County, Illinois, and each Party irrevocably waives any objection to venue in such courts." This Court sits in Cook County. Veridian has contractually waived any objection to venue and to the convenience of this forum.

15. **Form of application.** Section 6 of the FAA directs that "[a]ny application to the court hereunder shall be made and heard in the manner provided by law for the making and hearing of motions," 9 U.S.C. § 6. This Petition is accordingly presented as a motion, supported by the declarations and exhibits identified in Section IX below.

## IV. TIMELINESS — 9 U.S.C. § 12

The last day for service of this motion is therefore **Monday, June 16, 2025**.

## V. STANDARD OF REVIEW

22. Greystone acknowledges at the outset that judicial review of an arbitral award is among the narrowest known to law. "Judicial review of arbitration awards is tightly limited." *Baravati v. Josephthal, Lyon & Ross, Inc.*, 28 F.3d 704, 706 (7th Cir. 1994). A court does not sit to correct factual errors, evidentiary rulings, or contract interpretations with which it disagrees. *United Paperworkers Int'l Union v. Misco, Inc.*, 484 U.S. 29, 36–38 (1987). Greystone does not ask this Court to do any of those things.

23. **The statutory grounds are exclusive and closed.** In *Hall Street Associates, L.L.C. v. Mattel, Inc.*, 552 U.S. 576 (2008), the Supreme Court held that §§ 10 and 11 of the FAA "provide the FAA's exclusive grounds for expedited vacatur and modification." *Id.* at 584. The four grounds enumerated in § 10(a) are the whole of the list, and nothing outside that list will support vacatur. Greystone accordingly pleads within § 10(a) alone, and asserts no freestanding "manifest disregard of the law" ground — a doctrine the Seventh Circuit has held is not an independent basis for vacatur under the FAA. *See Affymax, Inc. v. Ortho-McNeil-Janssen Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 660 F.3d 281, 284–85 (7th Cir. 2011); *George Watts & Son, Inc. v. Tiffany & Co.*, 248 F.3d 577 (7th Cir. 2001).

24. **The grounds operate disjunctively.** Section 10(a) is framed in the alternative: the court "may make an order vacating the award ... (1) where the award was procured by corruption, fraud, or undue means; (2) where there was evident partiality or corruption in the arbitrators, or either of them; (3) where the arbitrators were guilty of misconduct in refusing to postpone the hearing, upon sufficient cause shown, or in refusing to hear evidence pertinent and material to the controversy; or of any other misbehavior by which the rights of any party have been prejudiced; or (4) where the arbitrators exceeded their powers, or so imperfectly executed them that a mutual, final, and definite award upon the subject matter submitted was not made." 9 U.S.C. § 10(a). Establishing **any one** of these grounds is sufficient to support vacatur. Greystone establishes three of the four, and addresses the fourth expressly in Section VII below in order to place the complete statutory list before the Court.

25. **The burden is Greystone's, and Greystone accepts it.** The party seeking vacatur bears the burden of establishing a statutory ground. Greystone carries that burden here principally by reference to the arbitrator's own written record: her Disclosure Statement, her Procedural Order No. 3, her Ruling of April 2, 2024, and the Award itself, which in several material respects documents the very defects Greystone identifies.

26. **Deference to the merits does not extend to the integrity of the process.** The deference described above is deference to an arbitrator's *decision*. It presupposes a decision-maker who was neutral and a proceeding in which both sides were heard. Where the challenge is directed not at the arbitrator's conclusions but at her impartiality and at the fairness of the process by which she reached them, the rationale for deference is absent. That is why Congress placed evident partiality and the refusal to hear material evidence in the statute at all. As the Supreme Court explained in *Commonwealth Coatings Corp. v. Continental Casualty Co.*, 393 U.S. 145 (1968), "any tribunal permitted by law to try cases and controversies not only must be unbiased but also must avoid even the appearance of bias." *Id.* at 150.

27. **Vacatur, not modification of the merits, is the relief sought.** Greystone does not ask this Court to decide the defect rate, to construe Section 7.4, or to enter judgment on the counterclaims. It asks the Court to vacate and to remit the dispute for rehearing before a neutral arbitrator under 9 U.S.C. § 10(b), so that those questions may be decided on a complete record by a decision-maker as to whom no reasonable question of impartiality exists.

## VI. FACTUAL BACKGROUND

### A. The Master Supply Agreement and the Arbitration Clause

28. On January 15, 2020, Greystone and Veridian executed a Master Supply Agreement (Agreement No. MSA-2020-0115) (the "MSA") under which Greystone agreed to manufacture and supply precision-machined titanium turbine blade blanks and aluminum structural connectors for Veridian's V-900 advanced turbofan engine program, which Veridian was developing under contract for a major defense prime contractor. The MSA had a five-year initial term running to January 14, 2025, with minimum annual purchase commitments totalling $164,000,000.

29. **MSA Section 9.2** established an "Acceptable Defect Threshold" of three percent (3%): "a shipment shall not be deemed Non-Conforming solely on the basis of individual unit defects, provided that the aggregate defect rate for such shipment does not exceed three percent (3%) of the total units delivered in such shipment." Where the defect rate is at or below 3%, "the shipment as a whole shall be deemed conforming for all purposes under this Agreement."

30. **MSA Section 7.4** permitted Greystone to request a price adjustment of up to 5% if the cost of titanium sponge or aerospace-grade aluminum billets "increases by more than eight percent (8%) year-over-year as measured by the Metal Market Index ('MMI') published by Ferrous Analytics Group." Section 7.4 contains no requirement that any such increase persist for two consecutive quarters, or for any other period, before the adjustment right may be exercised.

31. **MSA Section 14.1** contains the arbitration agreement. It provides for binding arbitration before a sole arbitrator administered by PAS under its Commercial Arbitration Rules (2022 Edition), with the seat at Chicago, Illinois, and Illinois substantive law. Critically, Section 14.1 also **limits the arbitrator's remedial authority**: the arbitrator "shall have the authority to award any remedy or relief that a court of competent jurisdiction could order or grant ... *provided, however, that the arbitrator shall not award consequential, punitive, or speculative damages*, it being the express agreement of the Parties that such damages are excluded from any award." The clause continues: "This limitation on the arbitrator's remedial authority reflects the Parties' mutual intent that neither Party shall be exposed to open-ended consequential or punitive liability in connection with disputes arising under this Agreement, and **is intended to be read in conjunction with the limitation of liability provisions set forth in Section 11.2**."

32. **MSA Section 11.2**, thus expressly incorporated into the definition of the arbitrator's remedial authority, provides in capitalized text: "IN NO EVENT SHALL EITHER PARTY BE LIABLE TO THE OTHER PARTY FOR ANY INDIRECT, INCIDENTAL, CONSEQUENTIAL, SPECIAL, OR PUNITIVE DAMAGES ARISING OUT OF OR RELATED TO THIS AGREEMENT, **INCLUDING BUT NOT LIMITED TO LOSS OF PROFITS**, LOSS OF REVENUE, LOSS OF USE, OR COST OF PROCUREMENT OF SUBSTITUTE GOODS ...."

33. **MSA Section 14.3** is the forum-selection clause quoted at paragraph 14 above, submitting proceedings "to confirm, vacate, or modify an arbitration award" to the exclusive jurisdiction of the state and federal courts in Cook County, Illinois.

### B. The Underlying Dispute

34. The parties performed without material incident through 2021. Beginning in mid-2022, global titanium sponge prices rose sharply following international sanctions that disrupted major producers. The Q3 2022 MMI data published by Ferrous Analytics Group showed a **31% year-over-year increase** in aerospace-grade titanium sponge prices — nearly four times the 8% contractual trigger. That increase was documented in the arbitration record (Exhibit C-47) and was undisputed.

35. On September 12, 2022, Greystone's CEO Harold K. Voss invoked Section 7.4 in writing, supported by the Q3 2022 MMI data, and requested the maximum permitted 5% adjustment effective January 1, 2023. Veridian responded on October 15, 2022, asserting for the first time that Section 7.4 "required the increase in raw material costs to be 'sustained for two consecutive quarters.'" No such language appears in Section 7.4. Greystone applied the 5% adjustment from January 1, 2023.

36. In March 2023, Veridian began rejecting shipments on quality grounds, including a February 28, 2023 delivery of 4,200 titanium turbine blade blanks under Purchase Order VAS-2023-0217. Veridian's in-house laboratory reported a **12% defect rate** (504 of 4,200 units). Greystone's contemporaneous quality-assurance records, generated at its Schaumburg facility before shipment, reflected a **1.8% defect rate** (76 of 4,200 units) — comfortably within the 3% threshold. Veridian withheld $6,300,000 in invoices and, on April 18, 2023, served a notice of material breach terminating the MSA effective May 18, 2023.

37. Veridian filed its Request for Arbitration on June 5, 2023, asserting four claims totalling $22,200,000. Greystone answered on July 21, 2023 and counterclaimed for $14,800,000 in lost profits from wrongful termination and $6,300,000 in unpaid invoices — $21,100,000 in aggregate.

### C. Appointment of the Arbitrator and the Disclosure Statement

38. The parties were unable to agree on a sole arbitrator within the thirty-day selection period prescribed by PAS Rule 13(b). PAS appointed the Honorable Diane C. Rourke (Ret.) from its Panel of Neutrals, notifying her on August 14, 2023. She accepted on August 22, 2023 and executed her Arbitrator Disclosure Statement (PAS Form D-12) the same day. It was transmitted to counsel on August 23, 2023.

39. The Disclosure Statement is detailed and, on its face, thorough. It runs to seven sections. It states that it is made "[i]n accordance with Rule 12(a) of the PAS Commercial Arbitration Rules (2022 Edition) **and the standards set forth in the IBA Guidelines on Conflicts of Interest in International Arbitration**," and that the arbitrator was making "affirmative disclosures regarding any facts or circumstances that might give rise to justifiable doubts as to my impartiality or independence."

40. Section IV.B of the Disclosure Statement — captioned "Prior Relationship with Counsel" — states, in full and unqualified terms:

> "I have no prior personal, professional, or financial relationship with Marcus D. Wheaton or Ridgeline Chambers LLP. I have not previously served as an arbitrator, mediator, or judge in any matter in which Ridgeline Chambers LLP appeared as counsel for any party. ... I have not had any professional or social contact with Mr. Wheaton or any other attorney at Ridgeline Chambers LLP in connection with any legal proceeding, educational program, or professional association activity that I am able to recall."

41. Section V of the Disclosure Statement acknowledged a **continuing** duty under PAS Rule 12(d) to disclose, "throughout the entirety of the arbitration proceedings, from the date of my appointment through the issuance of any final award," any circumstance that might give rise to justifiable doubts — and expressly acknowledged "that a failure to make such supplemental disclosures may constitute grounds for challenge to my service as arbitrator or to the validity of any award rendered."

42. Section VII certified: "I have made a **diligent and thorough inquiry** into all facts and circumstances that might give rise to justifiable doubts as to my impartiality or independence. I am not aware of any other facts or circumstances, beyond those set forth herein, that might create any such doubts."

43. The Disclosure Statement did disclose matters of considerably lesser significance. In Section IV.E, "[i]n the interest of full disclosure," the arbitrator volunteered that in 2019 she had served as sole arbitrator in an unrelated PAS matter involving a different aerospace components manufacturer (Crestfield Dynamics Corp.), in which neither party, neither counsel, and no related subject matter was involved. That disclosure demonstrates that the arbitrator understood her obligation to extend to remote and attenuated circumstances. It makes the omission described below all the more difficult to explain.

44. Nowhere in the Disclosure Statement — or at any point thereafter, over the nineteen months that followed — did Arbitrator Rourke disclose that her son was employed by claimant's counsel.

### D. Procedural Order No. 3 and Veridian's Forty-Five-Day Violation

45. The parties' competing defect-rate figures — 12% versus 1.8% — presented, in the arbitrator's own words, "a sharp factual dispute that goes to the very heart of the largest component of Claimant's damages claim, valued at $8.2 million." The physical sample units from the disputed batch were in Veridian's sole possession at its Reston, Virginia facility. MSA Section 9.3 obliged Veridian to retain them and to make them "available to Supplier upon reasonable written request for purposes of quality verification, defect investigation, or dispute resolution."

46. Greystone moved to compel production on December 4, 2023. Veridian opposed on December 11, 2023, arguing that its own report sufficed, that chain-of-custody risks counselled against transfer, and that transport would be burdensome.

47. On **December 15, 2023**, the arbitrator issued **Procedural Order No. 3** granting the motion. The Order contains the following findings, each of which the arbitrator would later contradict:

   a. The samples are "**plainly relevant and material** to the central factual dispute in this arbitration."

   b. "Respondent's right to present its case — including through the testimony and analysis of its own retained expert — is a **fundamental principle of arbitral fairness and due process**."

   c. "Denying Respondent physical access to the retained samples, which are in Claimant's sole possession, **would effectively deprive Respondent of any meaningful ability to rebut Claimant's metallurgical evidence** through independent testing. Such a result would be inconsistent with the Arbitrator's obligation to ensure that each party is afforded a full and fair opportunity to present its case."

   d. Veridian's objection that its own report was sufficient "effectively asks the Arbitrator to prejudge the weight and credibility of the competing expert evidence before the evidentiary hearing."

48. The Order directed Veridian to produce no fewer than 150 representative sample units to Lakeshore Testing Laboratories in Gary, Indiana **no later than January 15, 2024**, and stated: "**Time is of the essence with respect to this deadline.**"

49. The arbitrator explained precisely why she chose that date: "To ensure that Respondent has adequate time to receive the physical samples, conduct the necessary metallurgical testing, and incorporate the testing results into its expert submission by the February 15, 2024 deadline, production of the samples must occur promptly." Paragraph 5 of the Order repeats the point: "the January 15, 2024 production deadline ... **has been specifically calibrated** to afford Respondent sufficient time to receive the samples, conduct the necessary metallurgical testing, and incorporate the findings into its expert report by the February 15, 2024 deadline."

50. Paragraph 6 warned that "[f]ailure by any party to comply with the terms of this Order **may result in the imposition of sanctions**, including but not limited to adverse inferences, preclusion of evidence, cost-shifting, or such other remedies as the Arbitrator deems just and appropriate under Section 17.4 of the PAS Commercial Arbitration Rules."

51. **Veridian did not comply.** The Award records the facts without equivocation at paragraph 38:

> "Veridian did not comply with the January 15, 2024 production deadline established by Procedural Order No. 3. The retained samples ... were not produced to Greystone until March 1, 2024 — **forty-five (45) days after the deadline**. Greystone raised Veridian's noncompliance with the Tribunal on multiple occasions during this period, requesting that sanctions be imposed and that Veridian be compelled to produce the samples immediately. The Tribunal convened a teleconference on February 12, 2024 to address the issue. During the teleconference, Veridian's counsel represented that the delay ... was attributable to logistical difficulties in retrieving the retained samples from a secure storage facility .... **The Tribunal determined that no sanctions were warranted** and directed Veridian to produce the samples as expeditiously as possible."

52. The February 12, 2024 teleconference occurred three days before the February 15, 2024 expert report deadline. The arbitrator knew, at that moment, that her "specifically calibrated" schedule had collapsed through Veridian's default. She neither sanctioned Veridian, nor extended the expert report deadline, nor gave any direction as to how the testing she had ordered was now to be accomplished.

### E. The Ferren Report and Its Exclusion

53. The samples were shipped from Reston on March 1, 2024 and arrived at Lakeshore Testing Laboratories on March 4, 2024. Dr. Nikolai Ferren, Ph.D., P.E. — a licensed professional engineer with over twenty years' experience in aerospace metallurgy, principal of Blackthorn Materials Consulting LLC, and a published authority on grain-structure analysis of aerospace-grade titanium — personally inspected the units on arrival and confirmed no evidence of tampering or degradation.

54. Testing at Lakeshore, an **ISO/IEC 17025 accredited** independent facility, ran from March 5 through March 18, 2024. All 150 units were subjected to four categories of testing: visual and dimensional inspection; chemical composition analysis by optical emission spectrometry; mechanical properties testing under ASTM E8/E8M and ASTM E18; and metallographic and grain-structure analysis under ASTM E112, applying the ASTM B348 Grade 5 criteria on which Veridian's own claim rested.

55. The results were unambiguous. All 150 units met the ASTM B348 Grade 5 chemical composition requirements. All 150 met the mechanical property requirements. One hundred forty-nine of 150 passed visual and dimensional inspection. One hundred forty-seven of 150 exhibited fully conforming alpha-beta microstructure. Dr. Ferren classified **three units of 150 — 2.1%** — as non-conforming, and did so applying, in his words, "a conservative methodology that classifies all borderline units as non-conforming."

56. **2.1% is below the 3% Acceptable Defect Threshold in MSA Section 9.2.** It is consistent with Greystone's contemporaneous QA finding of 1.8%. It is inconsistent with Veridian's claimed 12% at any reasonable level of statistical confidence: Dr. Ferren calculated that the probability of observing only three non-conforming units in a sample of 150 drawn from a population with a true 12% defect rate is "less than 0.001%."

57. Dr. Ferren completed his report on **March 22, 2024** — **twenty-one days** after he received the samples, and fourteen days after the expert report deadline had already passed. He stated: "it was a **physical impossibility** for me to conduct independent re-testing and include the results in my initial expert report."

58. Greystone moved to admit the report on March 28, 2024, expressly grounding the motion on the fact that "the late completion of the report was the direct and unavoidable consequence of Veridian's own 45-day delay in producing the retained samples." Veridian opposed on March 29, 2024.

59. On **April 2, 2024**, the arbitrator denied the motion **without oral argument**, which she "determined to be unnecessary." Her Ruling:

   a. Found the report untimely by reference to the February 15, 2024 deadline, and observed that "Greystone did not request an extension of the expert report deadline at any point prior to February 15, 2024" (Ruling § V.B);

   b. Stated, as to Greystone's justification, that "**Respondent had ample opportunity throughout the discovery period to arrange for independent testing** and did not seek an extension of the expert report deadline at any point prior to its expiration. The Tribunal is not persuaded that the circumstances cited by Respondent justify the degree of delay reflected in the record" (Ruling § V.C);

   c. Found that admission six days before the hearing would prejudice Veridian, which would lack time to retain a rebuttal expert or prepare cross-examination (Ruling § V.D);

   d. **Declined a continuance**, reasoning that the proceedings had "been ongoing for approximately ten months" and that delay "would undermine the efficiency and finality that the arbitration process is intended to provide" (Ruling § V.E);

   e. Concluded that Greystone retained "a meaningful opportunity to contest Claimant's defect rate evidence" through its initial report and through cross-examination of Veridian's expert (Ruling § V.F);

   f. Ordered that the report "shall not be admitted into evidence and shall not be referenced or relied upon by any party during the evidentiary hearing or in any post-hearing briefing."

60. **Nowhere in the Ruling did the arbitrator mention Veridian's forty-five-day violation of her own Procedural Order No. 3.** The Ruling does not name Procedural Order No. 3. It does not mention the January 15, 2024 production deadline. It does not mention the March 1, 2024 production date. It does not mention the February 12, 2024 teleconference at which she declined to sanction that violation. The Ruling's central factual premise — that Greystone "had ample opportunity throughout the discovery period to arrange for independent testing" — is directly contradicted by the arbitrator's own finding, four months earlier, that the samples were "in Claimant's sole possession" and that the January 15 deadline had been "specifically calibrated" to make the very testing at issue possible.

61. **The arbitrator herself later documented these omissions.** Paragraph 43 of the Award states:

> "The Tribunal's ruling addressed the timeliness of the report relative to the February 15, 2024 deadline and the potential prejudice to Veridian from late admission. The ruling **did not address or acknowledge the causal connection** between Veridian's own 45-day delay in producing the retained samples ... **and the necessarily late completion** of the supplemental Ferren report. The ruling **did not address why Greystone should bear the consequences of Veridian's noncompliance** with the Tribunal's discovery order. The ruling **did not discuss or consider any alternatives to exclusion**, such as a brief continuance of the hearing to permit Veridian to prepare a rebuttal to the Ferren report, or the admission of the report with a reduced evidentiary weight, or any other remedy that might have accommodated both parties' interests."

62. Greystone "formally objected to the Tribunal's ruling on the record and expressly preserved its challenge for any subsequent proceedings," stating that "the exclusion of the Ferren report deprived Greystone of its most probative evidence on the central factual dispute in the case and that the exclusion was fundamentally unfair given that the late report was the direct result of Veridian's own discovery noncompliance." Award ¶ 44.

### F. The Award

63. The evidentiary hearing was held over eight days at PAS's Chicago offices: April 8–11 and April 22–25, 2024. Post-hearing briefs closed June 21, 2024; the record closed June 28, 2024. The Award issued **March 14, 2025** — nearly nine months later.

64. **Claim 1 (non-conforming goods, $8,200,000).** The arbitrator credited Veridian's in-house-derived 12% figure over Greystone's contemporaneous QA records, discounting the latter because they "were generated by Greystone's own personnel and are necessarily subject to the concern that they were produced by a party with an inherent interest in the outcome." Award ¶ 75. She contrasted them with "the findings of an independent expert engaged by the purchaser." *Id.* The one genuinely **independent** analysis in existence — testing performed at an accredited third-party laboratory on the actual disputed units — was the report she had excluded. Award ¶ 78. She found a 12% defect rate and awarded the full $8,200,000. Award ¶¶ 79–85.

65. **Claim 2 (lost profits, $5,900,000).** The arbitrator found an eleven-week V-900 program delay caused by the breach found under Claim 1, apportioned 79% to Greystone, and awarded $5,900,000 in **lost profits**. Award ¶¶ 91–98.

66. **Claim 3 (price adjustment overcharges, $1,900,000).** The arbitrator twice acknowledged that the contract text contains no durational requirement:

   - At Award ¶ 10: "The provision contains **no express requirement** that such increase be sustained for any specified duration prior to the supplier's exercise of the adjustment right."
   - At Award ¶ 105: "The Tribunal acknowledges that the text of Section 7.4 **does not expressly include the phrase 'sustained for two consecutive quarters' or any other express durational requirement**."

   She then held, at Award ¶ 107: "**The Tribunal finds an implied requirement that the cost increase be sustained for at least two consecutive quarters** as reflected in the MMI data before Greystone is entitled to invoke the price adjustment mechanism under Section 7.4." Applying that implied term, she held Greystone's invocation premature, found breach, and awarded $1,900,000. Award ¶¶ 108–111.

67. **Claim 4 (defense prime penalties, $2,700,000).** The arbitrator quoted MSA Section 14.1's prohibition and recorded Greystone's objection that the penalties were barred consequential damages. Award ¶ 112. She recited the orthodox Illinois distinction between direct and consequential damages and the rule of *Hadley v. Baxendale*, 9 Ex. 341 (1854): consequential damages "are those that arise from the special circumstances of the non-breaching party and that would not necessarily be incurred by every plaintiff in a similar situation." Award ¶ 118. Penalties imposed under a separate contract between Veridian and a third party, on terms Greystone never saw and to which it was not privy, are the paradigm case.

68. She nonetheless awarded them, relabelled as "direct flow-through costs." Her stated reasoning, at Award ¶ 121, is dispositive of Greystone's Ground Three:

> "Second, the Tribunal **acknowledges the tension between this characterization and the express language of Section 14.1**. A rigid application of the consequential damages exclusion to bar all downstream costs, however, would effectively render a Tier 1 supplier remediless for the most predictable and direct impacts of a sub-supplier's breach. **The Tribunal does not interpret Section 14.1 to produce such an unreasonable result.**"

69. **The counterclaims.** Both of Greystone's counterclaims were denied, and both denials rest directly on the Claim 1 defect-rate finding. Counterclaim 1 (wrongful termination, $14,800,000): "The resolution of Greystone's wrongful termination counterclaim **follows directly from the Tribunal's findings on Veridian's claims**," Award ¶ 131; the 12% finding supplied the material breach justifying Veridian's termination, Award ¶¶ 131–136, and barred Greystone from enforcing Veridian's minimum purchase commitments under the prior-material-breach doctrine, Award ¶ 137. Counterclaim 2 (unpaid invoices, $6,300,000): denied on set-off against "Veridian's claims for damages ... which total $18,700,000," and on Veridian's UCC right to withhold payment for non-conforming goods. Award ¶¶ 144–147.

70. **Costs.** Because "Veridian has prevailed on all four of its affirmative claims" and "Greystone's counterclaims have both been denied in their entirety," the arbitrator allocated 75% of the $550,000 arbitration costs to Greystone ($412,500), with $137,500 in deposit equalization payable to Veridian. Award ¶¶ 151–154.

71. **The single finding on which everything turns.** Every dollar of the $18,700,000 award, and every dollar of the $21,100,000 in denied counterclaims, and the 75% costs allocation, trace back to the 12% defect-rate finding made on a record from which the only independent testing had been excluded.

## VII. GROUNDS FOR VACATUR

*Section 10(a) of the FAA provides a closed list of four grounds, any one of which independently supports vacatur. Greystone addresses each in turn, including the one it does not assert.*

---

### GROUND ONE — EVIDENT PARTIALITY: 9 U.S.C. § 10(a)(2)

#### A. The Legal Standard

79. Section 10(a)(2) authorizes vacatur "where there was evident partiality or corruption in the arbitrators, or either of them."

80. In *Commonwealth Coatings Corp. v. Continental Casualty Co.*, 393 U.S. 145 (1968), the Supreme Court vacated an award where a neutral arbitrator failed to disclose a sporadic business relationship with one of the parties. The Court held that arbitrators "not only must be unbiased but also must avoid even the appearance of bias," *id.* at 150, and "should be held to the highest standards of integrity" precisely because their awards are subject to such limited review, *id.* at 149. The Court's central instruction is a disclosure rule: arbitrators must "disclose to the parties any dealings that might create an impression of possible bias." *Id.* Justice White, concurring, agreed that arbitrators "should err on the side of disclosure," while emphasizing that disclosure — not disqualification — is the ordinary remedy, because a party informed in advance may elect to proceed. *Id.* at 151–52 (White, J., concurring).

81. The Seventh Circuit has cautioned that nondisclosure does not automatically compel vacatur; the undisclosed matter must be **material**. In *Merit Insurance Co. v. Leatherby Insurance Co.*, 714 F.2d 673 (7th Cir. 1983), the court declined to vacate where the arbitrator had failed to disclose an employment relationship with an officer of a party that had ended some fourteen years earlier and had no continuing character. Judge Posner's opinion turns on the triviality and remoteness of the relationship, not on any principle that family or contemporaneous relationships may go undisclosed. *See also Sphere Drake Insurance Ltd. v. All American Life Insurance Co.*, 307 F.3d 617 (7th Cir. 2002) (distinguishing actual bias from nondisclosure and requiring a material undisclosed relationship).

82. Under either the "impression of possible bias" formulation of *Commonwealth Coatings* or the stricter materiality formulation applied in this Circuit, the inquiry is objective and prospective: it asks what a reasonable person, knowing the undisclosed facts, would conclude about the risk that the arbitrator's judgment might be affected. It does not require proof that the arbitrator was in fact biased, that the relationship in fact influenced any ruling, or that the undisclosed relative in fact worked on the matter. Actual bias is a separate and far more demanding showing that Greystone need not, and does not, undertake.

#### B. Application

83. **The relationship is familial, contemporaneous, ongoing, and directly connected to counsel of record.** The arbitrator's son is a Senior Associate at the law firm that represented the claimant in this arbitration. He was employed there before the arbitration began and remained employed there when the Award issued. This is not a stale connection to a party's officer of the kind found immaterial in *Merit*; it is a live employment relationship between the decision-maker's child and the prevailing party's counsel of record, subsisting for every day of the proceeding.

84. **The nexus to lead counsel is specific, not institutional.** Garrett Rourke does not merely work somewhere within a large national firm. He works in the **same office** as Marcus D. Wheaton, in the **same Aerospace & Defense industry group** as Mr. Wheaton, in a group that Mr. Wheaton **co-chairs**, in the very field that was the subject matter of the arbitration. Lead counsel for the party seeking $22,200,000 from Greystone occupied a position of professional seniority over the arbitrator's son within the group in which that son builds his career. A reasonable person assessing that configuration does not need to speculate about mechanisms of influence; the appearance of a stake is intrinsic to it.

85. **The financial dimension is real.** A senior associate's compensation, advancement, and partnership prospects depend on the standing and prosperity of the firm and on the assessment of the partners who lead his practice group. The arbitrator's son had an economic relationship with the entity whose client's $22,200,000 claim she was appointed to decide, and a professional relationship with the partner prosecuting it.

86. **The nondisclosure was not a close judgment call — and the arbitrator's own standards say so.** Her Disclosure Statement invoked the IBA Guidelines on Conflicts of Interest in International Arbitration as her chosen benchmark. The Harwell & Pratt investigation concludes that an arbitrator's close family member's employment at a party's counsel's firm falls within the IBA **Red List** — a category requiring mandatory disclosure and, in its non-waivable form, counselling against service altogether. Greystone does not contend that the IBA Guidelines are binding law in this Court. It contends that the arbitrator adopted them as her own measure of what required disclosure, and that by her own measure the relationship required disclosure. So too under PAS Rule 12(a), which requires disclosure of "any circumstance likely to give rise to justifiable doubts as to the arbitrator's impartiality or independence," expressly including relationships with counsel for any party.

87. **She disclosed far less.** The Disclosure Statement volunteered, "[i]n the interest of full disclosure," a 2019 arbitration involving an unrelated company, unrelated counsel, and unrelated subject matter, disclosed "solely to ensure transparency regarding my experience with disputes in the aerospace components industry." An arbitrator attentive enough to disclose that is an arbitrator who understood the breadth of the obligation. The omission of her son's employment at claimant's counsel's firm cannot be attributed to a narrow reading of the duty.

88. **This was an affirmative misstatement, not merely silence.** Section IV.B did not omit the subject of Ridgeline Chambers; it addressed it and denied any relationship: "I have no prior personal, professional, or financial relationship with Marcus D. Wheaton or **Ridgeline Chambers LLP**." Section VII then certified a "diligent and thorough inquiry." Greystone was entitled to rely on both statements, and did.

89. **The duty was continuing, and it was breached continuously.** Section V of the Disclosure Statement and PAS Rule 12(d) imposed an obligation running "from the date of my appointment through the issuance of any final award." Nineteen months elapsed. The relationship was never disclosed. The arbitrator expressly acknowledged in Section V that "a failure to make such supplemental disclosures may constitute grounds for challenge to my service as arbitrator or **to the validity of any award rendered**."

90. **Greystone did not waive the objection, because it could not.** Waiver requires knowledge. Greystone learned of the relationship on April 17, 2025, thirty-four days after the Award. Any suggestion that Greystone should have investigated the arbitrator's family independently is answered by the structure of the disclosure regime itself: the duty to disclose rests on the arbitrator, who alone possesses the relevant facts, and the entire purpose of a disclosure statement is to relieve parties of the obligation to conduct covert investigations of the neutral. It is answered a second time by the fact that the arbitrator affirmatively represented that no relationship with Ridgeline Chambers existed. A party that reads a sworn certification of "no relationship" and a certification of "diligent and thorough inquiry" is not put on notice to verify it.

91. **The Award's recital of non-objection does not assist Veridian.** Paragraph 32 of the Award states that "neither party challenged the appointment of the arbitrator or raised any objection to the Disclosure Statement within the time period prescribed by the PAS Rules." That is true and irrelevant. A party cannot object to what it has not been told.

92. **Prejudice is not an element, but it is present.** Section 10(a)(2) does not require a showing that the undisclosed relationship altered the outcome. Nonetheless, the nondisclosure deprived Greystone of the concrete procedural rights that disclosure exists to protect: the right to seek the arbitrator's recusal under the PAS Rules before the proceedings began, the right to challenge her appointment, and the right to make an informed decision whether to proceed before her at all. Those rights were extinguished, not exercised. And the proceeding that followed produced a $18,700,000 award against Greystone, the denial of $21,100,000 in counterclaims, a 75% costs allocation against Greystone, and — as Ground Two describes — an evidentiary ruling that excluded Greystone's most probative evidence while imposing no consequence on the opposing party's violation of the arbitrator's own order.

93. **Greystone does not allege actual bias, and does not need to.** Greystone does not contend that Arbitrator Rourke consciously favored her son's employer. The statutory standard does not turn on her subjective state of mind. It turns on whether a relationship existed that a reasonable person would regard as bearing on her impartiality, and which she was obliged to disclose and did not. It did, and she was, and she did not.

94. **Limited discovery is warranted.** Whether Garrett Rourke had any involvement in the Veridian matter — strategy, research, document review, or informal consultation — cannot be established from public sources. Greystone requests leave to take narrowly tailored discovery on that question. Greystone emphasizes that its Section 10(a)(2) ground does not depend on the answer: the duty to disclose arises from the existence of the relationship, not from the family member's participation in the particular case.

95. Ground One independently requires vacatur of the Award in its entirety.

### GROUND TWO — REFUSAL TO HEAR PERTINENT AND MATERIAL EVIDENCE, REFUSAL TO POSTPONE, AND OTHER PREJUDICIAL MISBEHAVIOR: 9 U.S.C. § 10(a)(3)

#### A. The Legal Standard

96. Section 10(a)(3) authorizes vacatur "where the arbitrators were guilty of misconduct in refusing to postpone the hearing, upon sufficient cause shown, or in refusing to hear evidence pertinent and material to the controversy; or of any other misbehavior by which the rights of any party have been prejudiced."

97. Arbitrators possess broad discretion over evidence and procedure, and § 10(a)(3) is not a licence to relitigate evidentiary rulings. The question is not whether the ruling was correct but whether it deprived the party of a **fundamentally fair hearing** — whether the party was given an adequate opportunity to present its evidence and argument on the issues that decided the case. *See Generica Ltd. v. Pharmaceutical Basics, Inc.*, 125 F.3d 1123, 1129–30 (7th Cir. 1997); *United Paperworkers Int'l Union v. Misco, Inc.*, 484 U.S. 29, 40 (1987) (a procedural error warrants vacatur where it denies a party fundamental fairness). Where excluded evidence goes to the dispositive issue and is not cumulative, exclusion may cross that line. *Cf. Tempo Shain Corp. v. Bertek, Inc.*, 120 F.3d 16, 20 (2d Cir. 1997).

98. All three limbs of § 10(a)(3) are engaged here: refusal to hear pertinent and material evidence; refusal to postpone upon sufficient cause shown; and other misbehavior prejudicing Greystone's rights.

#### B. The Excluded Evidence Was Pertinent and Material — as the Arbitrator Herself Found

99. The defect rate of the February 28, 2023 shipment was, in the arbitrator's words, "the central factual dispute in this claim," and its resolution "determines whether the shipment was conforming or non-conforming under MSA Section 9.2, and, accordingly, whether Greystone breached the MSA." Award ¶ 69.

100. Twelve weeks before excluding the evidence, the arbitrator found that access to the physical samples was "plainly relevant and material to the central factual dispute in this arbitration"; that Greystone's "right to present its case — including through the testimony and analysis of its own retained expert — is a fundamental principle of arbitral fairness and due process"; and that denying access "would effectively deprive Respondent of any meaningful ability to rebut Claimant's metallurgical evidence." Procedural Order No. 3, § III. Materiality is therefore not contested; it is a finding of the tribunal.

101. The excluded report was not cumulative. It was the **only** independent, third-party analysis of the actual disputed units in existence — testing performed at an ISO/IEC 17025 accredited laboratory, on 150 units drawn from the disputed batch, applying the same ASTM B348 Grade 5 criteria Veridian invoked, across four testing categories. Its finding of 2.1% fell **below** the contractual 3% threshold and would, if credited, have defeated Claim 1 outright and, by extension, Claims 2 and 4 and the denial of both counterclaims.

102. The exclusion's effect on the arbitrator's own reasoning is documented in the Award. She discounted Greystone's QA records as self-generated by "a party with an inherent interest in the outcome," and weighed them against "the findings of an independent expert engaged by the purchaser." Award ¶ 75. She criticised Greystone's methodology as resting on a "smaller sample size" and being "less rigorous." Award ¶ 76. Every one of those criticisms is answered by the Ferren report — larger sample, accredited independent laboratory, four-category protocol including metallographic examination under ASTM E112. Greystone was excluded from the record and then found wanting for the absence of precisely the evidence it had been prevented from putting in.

#### C. The Stated Ground for Exclusion Is Contradicted by the Arbitrator's Own Record

103. The Ruling of April 2, 2024 rests on the proposition that "Respondent had ample opportunity throughout the discovery period to arrange for independent testing and did not seek an extension of the expert report deadline at any point prior to its expiration." Ruling § V.C.

104. That proposition cannot be reconciled with Procedural Order No. 3. Greystone had **no** opportunity to arrange for independent testing during the discovery period, because the samples were — as the arbitrator found — "in Claimant's sole possession," and Veridian resisted producing them. Greystone did the only thing available to it: it moved to compel, and won. The arbitrator then set a production deadline she described as "**specifically calibrated**" to enable exactly this testing within exactly this schedule, and warned that "**[t]ime is of the essence.**"

105. The premise that Greystone should have sought an extension before February 15, 2024 fares no better. The arbitrator convened a teleconference on **February 12, 2024** — three days before the deadline — for the specific purpose of addressing Veridian's noncompliance, at Greystone's instance. Greystone had "raised Veridian's noncompliance with the Tribunal on multiple occasions during this period, requesting that sanctions be imposed and that Veridian be compelled to produce the samples immediately." Award ¶ 38. The tribunal was squarely on notice, before the deadline, that its calibrated schedule had failed. It declined to sanction, declined to extend, and gave no direction. Greystone cannot fairly be faulted for failing to make a request the tribunal had already, in substance, refused.

106. Dr. Ferren, upon finally receiving the samples on March 1, 2024, delivered his report in **twenty-one days**. There is no suggestion anywhere in the record that Greystone was dilatory after the samples arrived. The entirety of the lateness the arbitrator punished was Veridian's forty-five days.

#### D. Refusal to Postpone Upon Sufficient Cause Shown

107. Section 10(a)(3) separately reaches "misconduct in refusing to postpone the hearing, upon sufficient cause shown."

108. Greystone had shown sufficient cause. Its motion expressly grounded the request on Veridian's forty-five-day violation of a tribunal order that the tribunal had described as time-critical. The arbitrator considered a continuance and refused it, reasoning that the arbitration had "been ongoing for approximately ten months" and that a continuance "would further delay an already lengthy proceeding" and "would undermine the efficiency and finality that the arbitration process is intended to provide." Ruling § V.E.

109. Efficiency is a legitimate arbitral value. It cannot, however, be purchased at the price of the losing party's only evidence on the dispositive issue, where the need for the postponement was created by the opposing party's breach of the tribunal's own order. And the reasoning does not survive contact with what actually happened: having refused a short continuance in April 2024 in the name of expedition, the arbitrator then took **nearly nine months** after the record closed on June 28, 2024 to issue the Award on March 14, 2025. Whatever the length of a continuance sufficient to let Veridian retain a rebuttal expert, it was a fraction of the delay the tribunal itself absorbed without difficulty.

#### E. Other Misbehavior Prejudicing Greystone's Rights

110. The third limb of § 10(a)(3) is engaged by the tribunal's asymmetric treatment of the two parties' compliance with its own orders.

111. Veridian violated Procedural Order No. 3 by forty-five days. Paragraph 6 of that Order expressly contemplated "adverse inferences, preclusion of evidence, cost-shifting, or such other remedies as the Arbitrator deems just and appropriate." Greystone requested sanctions on multiple occasions. **The tribunal imposed none.** Award ¶ 38.

112. Greystone submitted a report thirty-five days after a deadline it could not have met because of that violation. **The tribunal imposed the most severe evidentiary sanction available: total preclusion**, extending not only to admission but to any reference to or reliance on the report "during the evidentiary hearing or in any post-hearing briefing." Ruling ¶ 2.

113. The net effect was to transfer the entire consequence of Veridian's noncompliance onto Greystone. The arbitrator's own Award identifies this precise defect: the Ruling "did not address why Greystone should bear the consequences of Veridian's noncompliance with the Tribunal's discovery order." Award ¶ 43.

114. The Award further records that the Ruling "did not discuss or consider any alternatives to exclusion, such as a brief continuance ..., or the admission of the report with a reduced evidentiary weight, or any other remedy that might have accommodated both parties' interests." Award ¶ 43. Preclusion was imposed without weighing any lesser measure, and without oral argument, which the tribunal "determined to be unnecessary." Ruling § I.

#### F. Prejudice

115. Prejudice here is not speculative; it is arithmetic.

116. **Claim 1 ($8,200,000)** turned entirely on the defect rate. Had the tribunal credited 2.1%, the shipment would have been conforming under Section 9.2 and Claim 1 would have failed in its entirety.

117. **Claims 2 and 4 ($5,900,000 and $2,700,000)** were both premised on the breach found under Claim 1 and on the V-900 delay said to flow from it. Award ¶¶ 91, 119. Neither survives the failure of Claim 1.

118. **Counterclaim 1 ($14,800,000)** was denied because the 12% finding supplied the material breach that justified Veridian's termination, Award ¶¶ 131–136, and because the prior-material-breach doctrine barred Greystone from enforcing Veridian's minimum purchase commitments, Award ¶ 137. Both rationales collapse if the shipment was conforming.

119. **Counterclaim 2 ($6,300,000)** was denied on set-off against damages "which total $18,700,000," Award ¶ 145, and on Veridian's asserted UCC right to withhold payment for non-conforming goods, Award ¶ 146. Both rationales collapse likewise.

120. **Costs ($412,500 plus $137,500 equalization)** were allocated 75% to Greystone expressly because "Veridian has prevailed on all four of its affirmative claims" and "Greystone's counterclaims have both been denied in their entirety." Award ¶ 151.

121. A single excluded report thus determined an outcome swing of $39,800,000 in claims and counterclaims plus $550,000 in costs. The exclusion of evidence that decides everything, imposed as a penalty for delay caused by the party it benefited, is not a discretionary evidentiary ruling entitled to deference. It is a denial of the fair hearing that § 10(a)(3) exists to guarantee.

122. **The objection was preserved.** Greystone objected on the record and "expressly preserved its challenge for any subsequent proceedings." Award ¶ 44. Greystone renewed the point in its post-hearing submissions, and the Award addresses it at ¶¶ 67–68 and 78.

123. Ground Two independently requires vacatur of the Award in its entirety.

### GROUND THREE — THE ARBITRATOR EXCEEDED HER POWERS: 9 U.S.C. § 10(a)(4)

#### A. The Legal Standard

124. Section 10(a)(4) authorizes vacatur "where the arbitrators exceeded their powers, or so imperfectly executed them that a mutual, final, and definite award upon the subject matter submitted was not made."

125. An arbitrator's authority is created by, and bounded by, the parties' agreement. The question under § 10(a)(4) "is whether the arbitrator (even arguably) interpreted the parties' contract, not whether he got its meaning right or wrong." *Oxford Health Plans LLC v. Sutter*, 569 U.S. 564, 569 (2013). That standard is exacting, and Greystone does not understate it: "convincing a court of an arbitrator's error — even his grave error — is not enough." *Id.* at 572.

126. But the standard has a floor. An arbitrator exceeds his powers when he "strays from interpretation and application of the agreement and effectively 'dispense[s] his own brand of industrial justice.'" *Stolt-Nielsen S.A. v. AnimalFeeds International Corp.*, 559 U.S. 662, 671 (2010) (quoting *Major League Baseball Players Ass'n v. Garvey*, 532 U.S. 504, 509 (2001)). In this Circuit, an arbitrator exceeds his authority if he "must have based his award on some body of thought, or feeling, or policy, or law that is outside the contract." *Eljer Manufacturing, Inc. v. Kowin Development Corp.*, 14 F.3d 1250, 1256 (7th Cir. 1994) (quoting *Ethyl Corp. v. United Steelworkers of America*, 768 F.2d 180, 184–85 (7th Cir. 1985)).

127. The distinction is between an arbitrator who construes a clause wrongly and an arbitrator who declines to apply a clause because she considers the result it dictates unreasonable. The first is unreviewable. The second is a refusal to exercise the delegated authority on its own terms, and is reviewable under § 10(a)(4). This distinction has particular force where the clause in question is not merely a substantive term but an **express limitation on the arbitrator's remedial authority** — a carve-out from the grant of power itself. A remedy the parties withheld from the arbitrator is a remedy she has no power to award.

#### B. Sub-Ground 3(a) — The $2,700,000 Consequential-Damages Award (Claim 4)

128. MSA Section 14.1 provides that the arbitrator "shall have the authority to award any remedy or relief that a court of competent jurisdiction could order or grant ... *provided, however, that the arbitrator shall not award consequential, punitive, or speculative damages, it being the express agreement of the Parties that such damages are excluded from any award*." The clause states its own purpose: it "reflects the Parties' mutual intent that neither Party shall be exposed to open-ended consequential or punitive liability," and it is "**a limitation on the arbitrator's remedial authority**."

129. Claim 4 sought $4,600,000 in **penalties imposed on Veridian by its defense prime contractor** under a separate contract to which Greystone was not a party, whose terms Greystone never saw, and whose penalty amounts Greystone did not know. Award ¶ 116. Under the framework the arbitrator herself set out, these are consequential damages by definition: they "arise from the special circumstances of the non-breaching party and ... would not necessarily be incurred by every plaintiff in a similar situation." Award ¶ 118 (citing *Hadley v. Baxendale*, 9 Ex. 341 (1854)).

130. The arbitrator awarded $2,700,000 of them, relabelled as "direct flow-through costs." Award ¶¶ 124, 160.

131. **The Award itself supplies the § 10(a)(4) finding.** At paragraph 121 the arbitrator wrote: "the Tribunal **acknowledges the tension between this characterization and the express language of Section 14.1**. A rigid application of the consequential damages exclusion to bar all downstream costs, however, would effectively render a Tier 1 supplier remediless .... **The Tribunal does not interpret Section 14.1 to produce such an unreasonable result.**"

132. That passage does not report an interpretation of Section 14.1. It reports a decision not to apply Section 14.1 as written because the arbitrator judged the outcome commercially unreasonable. The reasoning is candid and it is dispositive: the operative premise is not the parties' language but the arbitrator's assessment of a fair allocation of supply-chain risk — precisely the "body of thought, or feeling, or policy" that *Eljer* places outside the contract, and precisely the "own brand of industrial justice" that *Stolt-Nielsen* forbids.

133. The relabelling does not cure the defect. As Greystone argued below and as the client has maintained throughout, penalties passed down a contractual chain from a third party are consequential damages whatever they are called; the character of the loss, not the caption applied to it, determines whether Section 14.1 permits the award. And the arbitrator's own rationale confirms that she understood the loss to fall within the clause and awarded it notwithstanding.

134. The parties' allocation of this exact risk was deliberate, and the arbitrator's premise that enforcement would leave Veridian "remediless" is not accurate in any event: Veridian recovered $8,200,000 in replacement costs and $5,900,000 in lost profits. What the clause denied Veridian was open-ended downstream exposure — which is what the parties bargained for.

135. Sub-Ground 3(a) requires, at minimum, vacatur of the $2,700,000 awarded on Claim 4.

#### C. Sub-Ground 3(b) — The $5,900,000 Lost-Profits Award (Claim 2)

136. Section 14.1's remedial limitation is expressly "intended to be read in conjunction with the limitation of liability provisions set forth in Section 11.2." Section 11.2 defines the excluded category by enumeration, and the first item enumerated is "**LOSS OF PROFITS**": "IN NO EVENT SHALL EITHER PARTY BE LIABLE ... FOR ANY INDIRECT, INCIDENTAL, CONSEQUENTIAL, SPECIAL, OR PUNITIVE DAMAGES ..., INCLUDING BUT NOT LIMITED TO LOSS OF PROFITS, LOSS OF REVENUE, LOSS OF USE, OR COST OF PROCUREMENT OF SUBSTITUTE GOODS."

137. Read as Section 14.1 directs, the parties defined the damages withheld from the arbitrator's remedial authority to include lost profits. The arbitrator awarded **$5,900,000 in lost profits** on Claim 2. Award ¶¶ 97, 158.

138. The Award contains no analysis of Section 11.2 in connection with Claim 2 — or anywhere. The arbitrator's recitation of the MSA at Award ¶ 15 refers in passing to "limitation of liability (Section 16)," a provision that does not exist in the executed MSA, in which the limitation of liability appears at Section 11.2 within Article 11. The Award's Claim 2 analysis (¶¶ 91–98) addresses causation, apportionment, and quantum, and does not address the arbitrator's authority to award lost profits at all. Where the agreement withholds a category of remedy and the arbitrator awards it without engaging the withholding provision, she has not construed a limit on her power; she has passed over it.

139. Greystone acknowledges that Veridian may argue that Section 11.2's enumerated items are qualified by the introductory adjectives — that "loss of profits" is excluded only where the lost profits are themselves indirect or consequential — and that the arbitrator's finding of an eleven-week critical-path delay renders these lost profits direct. Greystone submits that this reading is unavailable given that Section 14.1 independently bars "consequential ... or speculative damages" and that the arbitrator expressly acknowledged the lost-profits analysis "necessarily involves some degree of estimation," Award ¶ 92. The Court need not resolve Sub-Ground 3(b), however, if it grants relief on Grounds One or Two, or on Sub-Ground 3(a).

#### D. Sub-Ground 3(c) — The $1,900,000 Award on an Implied Term the Arbitrator Found Absent (Claim 3)

140. Section 7.4 of the MSA permits a price adjustment where raw-material costs increase by more than 8% year-over-year as measured by the MMI. It contains no durational qualifier.

141. The arbitrator said so twice. At Award ¶ 10: "The provision contains **no express requirement** that such increase be sustained for any specified duration prior to the supplier's exercise of the adjustment right." At Award ¶ 105: "the text of Section 7.4 **does not expressly include** the phrase 'sustained for two consecutive quarters' **or any other express durational requirement**."

142. She then supplied one: "The Tribunal finds an **implied requirement** that the cost increase be sustained for **at least two consecutive quarters** as reflected in the MMI data before Greystone is entitled to invoke the price adjustment mechanism under Section 7.4." Award ¶ 107. The stated basis was "the commercial purpose of the MSA and the reasonable expectations of sophisticated commercial parties." *Id.*

143. Applying that manufactured prerequisite, the arbitrator found Greystone's September 12, 2022 invocation premature — notwithstanding an undisputed 31% year-over-year increase, nearly four times the contractual trigger — and awarded Veridian $1,900,000. Award ¶¶ 108–111.

144. Greystone recognises the force of *Oxford Health*: an arbitrator who reads a contract badly has still read it. But there is a meaningful difference between construing ambiguous language and finding, twice and expressly, that the language does not contain a requirement and then imposing that requirement anyway on grounds of commercial purpose. The two-consecutive-quarters condition entered this dispute in Veridian's October 15, 2022 letter, not in the parties' 2020 bargain. Adopting a party's post-hoc gloss as an implied term, after finding it absent from the text, is the addition of a term rather than the interpretation of one — and it added a term that reallocated $1,900,000.

145. Greystone presents Sub-Ground 3(c) as an alternative and subsidiary basis for partial relief, and does not rest its Petition on it.

#### E. Relief Under Ground Three

In the alternative, the Court may modify the Award under 9 U.S.C. § 11(b), which permits modification "where the arbitrators have awarded upon a matter not submitted to them," by striking those components. Because Grounds One and Two require vacatur of the Award in its entirety, however, Greystone's primary request is complete vacatur, with Ground Three offered as an independent and alternative basis for relief should the Court decline complete vacatur.

## VIII. THE REMEDY REQUESTED

150. **Vacatur in its entirety is the appropriate remedy.** Grounds One and Two are not confined to discrete components of the Award. Evident partiality infects every ruling the arbitrator made, from Procedural Order No. 3 through the allocation of costs; there is no severable portion of an award that a partial decision-maker may be said to have decided impartially. The § 10(a)(3) violation is similarly pervasive: the excluded evidence determined the defect-rate finding, and the defect-rate finding determined all four claims, both counterclaims, and the costs allocation. *See* ¶¶ 116–121 above.

151. **Rehearing before a new arbitrator.** Section 10(b) provides that "[i]f an award is vacated and the time within which the agreement required the award to be made has not expired, the court may, in its discretion, direct a rehearing by the arbitrators." The MSA prescribes no deadline for the making of an award, and the parties' arbitration agreement in Section 14.1 remains valid and enforceable. Greystone does not seek to escape arbitration; it seeks an arbitration before a neutral. Greystone therefore requests that the Court direct a rehearing before a **newly appointed sole arbitrator**, and remit the matter to PAS for appointment in accordance with Rule 13 of the PAS Commercial Arbitration Rules (2022 Edition), with Arbitrator Rourke disqualified from further service in this matter. Where vacatur rests on evident partiality, remitting to the same arbitrator would defeat the purpose of the vacatur.

152. **Stay of enforcement.** Section 12 provides that "any judge who might make an order to stay the proceedings in an action brought in the same court may make an order, to be served with the notice of motion, staying the proceedings of the adverse party to enforce the award." 9 U.S.C. § 12. Greystone requests an order staying any proceeding by Veridian to confirm or enforce the Award pending determination of this Petition. Post-award interest accrues at 5% per annum on $18,700,000 — approximately $935,000 annually — and Greystone's annual revenue is approximately $285,000,000, such that an award of this magnitude is material to its financial position and operations.

153. **Denial of confirmation.** Should Veridian cross-petition to confirm the Award under 9 U.S.C. § 9, Greystone requests that the cross-petition be denied. Section 9 directs confirmation "unless the award is vacated, modified, or corrected as prescribed in sections 10 and 11." Because grounds for vacatur are established, confirmation must be refused.

154. **Limited discovery.** Greystone requests leave to take narrowly tailored discovery directed to (i) the nature and extent of Garrett Rourke's involvement, if any, in Ridgeline Chambers LLP's representation of Veridian in PAS Case No. 2023-ARB-04871; (ii) the professional and supervisory relationship between Garrett Rourke and Marcus D. Wheaton; and (iii) any communications between Arbitrator Rourke and any person at Ridgeline Chambers LLP concerning this matter. Greystone reiterates that Ground One does not depend on the results of such discovery.

155. **Alternative partial relief.** In the alternative, and only if the Court declines complete vacatur, Greystone requests partial vacatur under § 10(a)(4) of the $2,700,000 awarded on Claim 4, the $5,900,000 awarded on Claim 2, and the $1,900,000 awarded on Claim 3, or modification of the Award under 9 U.S.C. § 11(b) to strike those components, together with a corresponding reallocation of arbitration costs.
