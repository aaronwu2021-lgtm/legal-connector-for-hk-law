## I. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

1. Horizon claims USD 87,600,000 across five heads of loss on two causes of action: breach of the Schedule 4 warranties, and the tort of deceit. The contractual claim is capped at USD 33,600,000 by Clause 8.3.1. **The entire USD 54,000,000 of claimed exposure above the cap depends on the fraud allegation succeeding — and the fraud allegation is, on the present record, unsustainable.** It is also, on the Claimant's own contract, only partially cap-breaking: Clause 8.3.4 apportions, so even a successful fraud finding on one vessel leaves the balance capped.

2. The single most consequential feature of this file is that **the Claimant's principal item of "fraud" evidence proves the opposite of what it is pleaded to prove.** Exhibit C-9 is quoted in the Statement of Claim as a fifteen-word fragment: *"The Pioneer's survey results aren't great — let's make sure the VDR upload covers us."* The document itself continues: *"Can you coordinate with James on getting the full BV report and any related maintenance docs into the data room before the next buyer access window opens on the 24th? If Janssen's team has a maintenance plan or timeline for the corrosion work, let's get that in there too so the picture is complete."* The email chain also contains Ms. Sunderland's antecedent message of 16 May 2022 recording that the vessel *"passed"* the survey and that the Northsea technical team characterised the findings as *"manageable within the next scheduled maintenance cycle."* An instruction to upload the full report, the maintenance records and the remediation plan "so the picture is complete" is the antithesis of concealment. The Claimant's pleading at ¶¶110–112 — that the phrase "covers us" reveals a strategy "to create a veneer of disclosure while obscuring the true condition of the vessel" — is contradicted by the next sentence of the very document relied on.

3. Beyond that, the Statement of Claim and the Expert Report of Dr. Richter contradict each other on matters that ought to be beyond argument: **which company owned each vessel, what type each vessel is, in which ocean each vessel traded, which classification society classed each vessel, who prepared the repair estimates, when the Respondent acquired the Target, and whether the North Sea charter was ever concluded at all.** These are not stylistic divergences. The day rates driving USD 29.8 million of claimed loss are attached to the wrong vessels in the wrong markets.

4. The lost-revenue head does not add up. The three components stated in both the Statement of Claim (¶¶139–141) and the Expert Report (¶¶57, 60, 63 and the Head-of-Loss-2 table) are USD 7,980,000, USD 6,737,500 and USD 9,020,000. **They total USD 23,737,500. The claim is USD 29,800,000. USD 6,062,500 — 25.5% of the head — is unexplained arithmetic.**

5. Most fundamentally, **the Claimant's own expert values the fleet it received at more than the Claimant paid for it.** The DCF at ¶71 puts the warranted-condition fleet at USD 134,500,000 and the actual post-discovery fleet at USD 115,600,000. The purchase price was USD 112,000,000. On the correct English measure for a share sale — the difference between the value of what was bought as warranted and as it in fact was, measured against the price paid — Horizon received more than it paid for and has suffered no transactional loss at all.

6. The Statement of Claim was also filed **without a single witness statement**, in breach of PO No. 1, ¶¶21 and 26. The Janssen evidence — the sole support for the fraud allegation on the *Nordic Sovereign* — exists only as a description of what a witness "will testify." Under PO No. 1, ¶26, no further witness statement may be filed without leave. There is likewise no statement from Mr. Brynstad or Ms. Devaux, the two individuals named in Clause 7.4 as responsible for the transaction, which means **there is presently no evidence of reliance at all.**

7. Recommended posture: deny in full; plead the "Fairly Disclosed" defence, the Clause 7.5 and 11.3.2 non-reliance regime (with an affirmative reasonableness case under Misrepresentation Act 1967 s.3 / UCTA 1977 s.11), the Clause 8.3 limitations with express reliance on the Clause 8.3.4 apportionment, the Clause 8.4 time bar as against every warranty not specified in the 15 February 2024 notice, the Clause 8.5 mitigation and insurance-recovery obligations, and Clause 8.6 no-double-recovery; **counterclaim for the Second Deferred Payment of USD 8,500,000** which fell due on 14 October 2024 and which Clause 3.2 forbids Horizon from setting off; and apply for early determination of the fraud allegation and the Clause 8.3.1 cap.

## III. THE GOVERNING LEGAL FRAMEWORK

### A. The contractual claim

10. The First Cause of Action is a claim in contract for breach of the Schedule 4 Warranties, sounding in damages under Clause 8.1.1 and subject to the whole of Clause 8. Clause 8.7.1 makes a Clause 8 damages claim the **sole and exclusive remedy** for warranty breach and expressly removes any right to rescind or terminate. The contractual measure is therefore the orthodox English measure for breach of warranty on a sale of shares: the difference between the value of the shares as warranted and the value of the shares as they in fact were, at the warranty date. It is not the cost of repairing the underlying assets, and it is not the target group's lost trading revenue.

### B. The tort claim — structure of the test

11. The Second Cause of Action is the tort of deceit, pleaded in the alternative as negligent misrepresentation under s.2(1) of the Misrepresentation Act 1967 (SoC, ¶106). **This is a conjunctive test: every element must be established. Failure on any single element defeats the claim entirely, regardless of how strong the others are.** The elements, and the sub-tests within them, are:

| El. | Element | Sub-tests engaged on this record |
|---|---|---|
| **E1** | False statement of existing fact | E1a fact v. opinion; E1b half-truth; E1c continuing representation; **E1d representation by conduct — mere silence is not actionable; there is no general duty of disclosure** |
| **E2** | Addressed to the representee | E2a channel — the representation must be made to, or intended to reach, the claimant |
| **E3** | Inducement / reliance | E3a awareness; E3b actual reliance; E3c presumption of inducement; E3d doubt does not defeat reliance; E3e no duty to investigate |
| **E4** | State of mind (variant selector) | **E4a fraudulent** — knowingly false, without belief in truth, or reckless (*Derry v Peek*); an honest but negligent belief is insufficient and **"ought to have known" does not establish fraud**. **E4b negligent statutory** — no proof of fraud needed, but the **burden is reversed**: the representor must prove objectively reasonable ground to believe *and* actual belief up to the time of contract |
| **E5** | Remedies | E5a rescission and its bars (affirmation, lapse of time, impossibility of *restitutio*, third-party rights); E5b deceit measure — all loss directly flowing, not limited by foreseeability; E5c statutory measure |

12. Two defences are directly in issue:

- **D1 — Non-reliance / entire agreement.** Contractual estoppel: parties may agree that no representations were made or relied on, barring non-fraudulent claims. Fraud can never be excluded. Statutory reasonableness control applies where the jurisdiction provides it.
- **D2 — Own investigation.** The representee relied solely on its own independent investigation rather than on the statement.

13. **The state of the English authority matters, and it has moved.** Three doctrinal timelines bear on this dispute, and the standing English rule in each is as follows:

- **Non-reliance clauses (D1).** The *Peekay* (2006) / *Springwell* (2010) line established and broadened contractual estoppel between sophisticated commercial parties. But since **2018 (*First Tower Trustees v CDS*) the standing English rule is that basis clauses are exclusions in substance and must therefore pass the reasonableness test under Misrepresentation Act 1967 s.3 / UCTA 1977 s.11(1).** Practical consequence for us: Clauses 7.5 and 11.3.2 are not self-executing. We must plead and prove reasonableness affirmatively, and under UCTA s.11(5) the burden sits on the party relying on the clause — that is, on Bellwood. This is a positive pleading obligation, not a mere denial, and it must go into the Statement of Defence.
- **Presumption of inducement (E3c).** The English line has strengthened, not weakened. *Hayward v Zurich* (2016): belief in the truth of the statement is not required, and suspicion of falsity does not defeat reliance. **The standing English rule since 2019 (*BV Nederlandse v Rembrandt*) is that in fraud the presumption of inducement is very difficult to rebut and the test is whether the representation was "a" cause, not "the" cause.** Practical consequence: we should not expect to win at E3 once a material representation calculated to induce has been established. Our effort must be concentrated at **E1, E2 and E4**, where the Claimant has no case, rather than at E3, where the law now runs against us. The Singapore line (*Wee Chiaw Sek Anna*, 2013 — inference of fact only, burden staying on the representee) and the Hong Kong line (*Shine Grace v Citibank*, 2018 — no inducement where the representee would have acted anyway) are more favourable to a respondent but are not the law of the seat and should not be relied on as if they were.
- **The s.2(1) damages measure (E5c).** *Royscot Trust v Rogerson* (1991) applied the deceit measure to s.2(1) via the "fiction of fraud"; the House of Lords in *Smith New Court* (1996) expressly left its correctness open, and it has been doubted since. The Claimant's alternative s.2(1) plea (SoC, ¶106) simply assumes the deceit measure without addressing the point.

14. **What this framework changes about the analysis.** Three things. First, it forecloses the intuitively attractive but legally wrong defence that Horizon should have found the problems for itself: an opportunity to discover the truth is no defence to misrepresentation (E3e). The "14,200 documents" point is a *contractual* answer under the Fairly Disclosed definition and a *D2* answer if Horizon relied solely on its own investigation — it is not a reliance answer in tort, and pleading it as one would be an error. Second, it identifies where the Claimant's case actually fails: not at reliance, where English law now heavily favours a claimant, but at E1/E2 (there is no identified representation, and nothing addressed to Horizon) and E4a (the pleaded mental element is "knew or ought to have known", which is not fraud). Third, it tells us that our non-reliance clauses need an affirmative reasonableness case rather than bare invocation.

## IV. PART A — THRESHOLD AND PROCEDURAL DEFICIENCIES

### Issue 1 — The Statement of Claim was filed without any witness statement, and the fraud case cannot be proved without one

**Standard.** PO No. 1, ¶21: *"The Statement of Claim shall be accompanied by all supporting documents, witness statements, and expert reports on which the Claimant intends to rely at this stage of the proceedings... Witness statements shall be identified as CWS-1, CWS-2, etc."* PO No. 1, ¶26: *"First-round witness statements shall be submitted together with the Statement of Claim... **No additional witness statements shall be filed without leave of the Tribunal.**"* PO No. 1, ¶36: witness statements stand as evidence-in-chief; no examination-in-chief without leave.

**The shortfall.** The Claimant's exhibit list (SoC, §XII) runs C-1 to C-22 and contains no CWS at all. The Claimant nonetheless pleads at ¶¶60, 113–116 that Mr. Henrik Janssen *"will testify"* to matters that constitute the entire evidential basis for the fraud allegation on the *Nordic Sovereign*. The same applies to Mr. Torbjørn Halvorsen, whose validation of the downtime estimates is relied on at ¶144 and ER ¶54, and — critically — to Mr. Lars-Erik Brynstad and Ms. Marguerite Devaux, the two individuals identified in SPA Clause 7.4 as principally responsible for the negotiation and execution of the SPA on Horizon's behalf, and therefore the only people who can speak to reliance.

**Consequence.** As matters stand, the Claimant has no admissible evidence of (i) Bellwood's knowledge of the *Sovereign*'s hull condition, (ii) any communication of that condition to anyone at Bellwood, (iii) Horizon's awareness of any representation, or (iv) Horizon's reliance on any representation. The Claimant must apply for leave to cure, and the Tribunal should be invited to refuse or to condition leave on costs, given PO No. 1, ¶40 (*"extensions of time will not be granted absent exceptional circumstances"*).

**Strength: Very strong.** Objective non-compliance with an express order, going to the heart of both causes of action.

### Issue 2 — The exhibit apparatus has collapsed; the Tribunal cannot verify a single expert source

**Standard.** PO No. 1, ¶21 (sequential C-numbering; expert reports as CER-1, CER-2) and ¶35 (*"Each exhibit shall be clearly numbered and identified... A cumulative exhibit index shall be maintained and updated with each round of submissions"*).

**The shortfall.** The Expert Report bears on its own cover page the legend **"Expert Report — Exhibit C-22"**, while the Statement of Claim lists the Expert Report as **C-16** and lists C-22 as correspondence with the West African charter counterparty. Two different documents therefore claim the same exhibit number, and the Expert Report is not numbered CER-1 as PO No. 1, ¶21 requires. Worse, virtually every internal citation in the Expert Report points to a different document from the one the Statement of Claim says bears that number:

| Expert Report citation | What the Expert says it is | What SoC §XII says C-number is |
|---|---|---|
| C-5 (ER ¶9(d), ¶38) | Bureau Veritas Pioneer survey, March 2022 | Request for Arbitration, 1 May 2024 |
| C-6 (ER ¶9(e)) | *Endurance* maintenance logs, Jan 2020 – Nov 2022 | Procedural Order No. 1 |
| C-10 (ER ¶9(f)) | Horizon fleet-wide technical audit, Jan 2023 | *Sovereign* hull inspection report, 22 Mar 2023 |
| C-11, C-12, C-13 (ER ¶¶9(g), 39, 43, 47) | Strandvik engineering assessments (Pioneer, Endurance, Sovereign) | Repair estimate (Havyard); BV survey report; Pioneer repair estimate |
| C-14, C-15, C-16 (ER ¶¶9(h), 40, 44, 48) | Strandvik repair cost estimates | *Endurance* engineering report; *Endurance* repair estimate; Expert Report itself |
| C-17, C-18 (ER ¶¶9(i), 85(a)) | Charter-loss correspondence | Charter *rate* evidence (Pioneer; Endurance) |

**Consequence.** No opinion in the Expert Report can be traced to a document actually before the Tribunal. This is not a clerical irritation: PO No. 1, ¶35 places the burden of establishing authenticity on the producing party where authenticity is challenged, and IBA Rules Art. 5.2 requires an expert report to state the documents on which it relies. The Tribunal should be asked to direct a reconciled cumulative index before the Statement of Defence, failing which the Expert Report should be given no weight.

**Strength: Strong.** Objective, verifiable from the face of the two documents.

### Issue 3 — The Statement of Claim and the Expert Report irreconcilably contradict each other on who prepared the repair estimates

**The shortfall.** The Statement of Claim represents that the estimates come from three separate independent sources: the *Sovereign* estimate at C-11 was *"prepared by Havyard Ship Technology AS, a recognized Norwegian shipyard"* (SoC, ¶¶59, 133); the *Pioneer* estimate at C-13 was *"prepared by an independent repair yard"* (SoC, ¶¶67, 131); and the *Endurance* estimate at C-15 was *"prepared by an independent marine engineering consultancy"* (SoC, ¶¶78, 132). The Expert Report states that **all three engineering assessments and all three repair cost estimates were produced by a single firm, Strandvik Marine Engineering Ltd.** (ER, ¶¶9(g)–(h), 31(d), 34, 39, 43, 47, 51, Appendix D).

**Consequence.** Either the Statement of Claim misdescribes the provenance of USD 23,400,000 of exhibits, or the expert does. On the expert's version, the "independent" cross-check that the Statement of Claim advertises does not exist: the firm that diagnosed the defects also priced their remedy, and the expert *"ha[s] not independently verified the accuracy of the repair cost estimates"* (ER, ¶34). This also renders unsupportable SoC ¶135's assertion that the estimates are *"supported by the independent shipyard and engineering consultancy estimates referenced above"* — on the expert's account there was one firm, not several.

**Strength: Very strong.**

### Issue 4 — Selective quotation of Exhibit C-9

**Standard.** A party and its counsel owe the Tribunal a duty of candour; a document put before a tribunal as evidence of a state of mind must not be truncated so as to reverse its sense. PO No. 1, ¶41 gives the Tribunal full discretion as to weight; Clause 14.4 and ICC Rules Art. 38 permit costs consequences having regard to *"the conduct of the parties."*

**The shortfall.** Set out in full at ¶2 above. The Statement of Claim reproduces fifteen words of a document that continues for a further three sentences in the opposite direction, and does not disclose the antecedent message in the same chain. The Claimant then builds four paragraphs of inference on the truncation (SoC, ¶¶109–112), including the assertion at ¶112 that Bellwood *"ensured that a technical 'paper trail' existed in the VDR... while simultaneously failing to disclose the true severity of the deficiencies."* Mr. Colton's actual instruction was to place *"the full BV report and any related maintenance docs"* and *"a maintenance plan or timeline for the corrosion work"* into the data room *"so the picture is complete."*

**Consequence.** Three. First, the pleaded inference of fraudulent intent collapses (see Issue 18). Second, the truncated quotation should be put to Mr. Colton in cross-examination alongside the full text and made the centrepiece of the credibility case. Third, we should reserve a costs application under Clause 14.4 and ICC Art. 38(5) on the conduct of the Claimant in advancing an allegation of fraud on a document so presented.

**Strength: Very strong.**

### Issue 5 — The Statement of Claim misstates the constitution of the Tribunal, contradicting its own exhibit

**The shortfall.** SoC, ¶26: *"As of the date of this Statement of Claim, the Tribunal is not yet fully constituted. Each party has nominated its party-appointed arbitrator, and the process for appointment of the presiding arbitrator is ongoing pursuant to the ICC Rules."* The Statement of Claim is dated 7 October 2024. Its own Exhibit C-6 — Procedural Order No. 1, on which ¶1 expressly relies — records at ¶5 that *"[t]he Arbitral Tribunal... was constituted on July 22, 2024"* and at ¶8 identifies Sir Malcolm Fairhurst KC as Presiding Arbitrator, appointed by the ICC Court under Art. 13(3) after the parties failed to agree, with Professor Elena Vasquez and Mr. Desmond Kwan as co-arbitrators. PO No. 1 is signed by all three arbitrators. SoC ¶1 simultaneously asserts that PO No. 1 was *"issued by the Tribunal constituted in these proceedings"* — an internal contradiction with ¶26 within the same document.

**Consequence.** No substantive consequence, but a clean and unanswerable illustration of the standard of care applied to this pleading. Deploy it early, in the introductory section of the Statement of Defence, as the frame through which the Tribunal should read the remainder.

**Strength: Moderate** (credibility only).

## V. PART B — DEFICIENCIES IN THE PLEADING OF THE CONTRACT

### Issue 6 — Clause 7.1 is misquoted; the materiality qualifier is dropped

**The shortfall.** SoC, ¶35 quotes Clause 7 as warranting that the Schedule 4 Warranties were *"true and accurate as of the date of the SPA and as of Closing, except as Fairly Disclosed in the Disclosure Letter."* No such sentence exists in the SPA. Clause 7.1 warrants that each Warranty is *"true, accurate, and **not misleading in any material respect** as at the date of this Agreement and as at the Closing Date."* The Fairly Disclosed qualification is in a separate provision, Clause 7.2. The Claimant has fused two clauses into one fabricated quotation and, in doing so, **deleted the materiality qualifier that governs every warranty in Schedule 4.**

**Consequence.** Materiality is a threshold on the warranty itself, sitting above and independent of the Clause 8.3.2 de minimis and Clause 8.3.3 basket. It must be pleaded and it must be proved vessel by vessel, deficiency by deficiency, against a twelve-vessel fleet acquired for USD 112,000,000.

**Strength: Strong.**

### Issue 7 — Schedule 4 ¶12 is misquoted, and ¶¶12.2–12.4 are suppressed entirely

**The shortfall.** SoC, ¶¶36, 83 and 128 (and ER, ¶19, ¶66) all quote the Condition of Assets Warranty as: *"Each material asset of the Group is in good working condition consistent with its age and has been maintained in accordance with good industry practice and all applicable classification society requirements."* The actual Paragraph 12.1 reads:

> *"Each material asset owned or used by any Group Company (including, without limitation, each vessel in the fleet operated by the Group) is in good working condition **and repair, consistent with its age and normal wear and tear**, and has been maintained in accordance with: (a) good industry practice for assets of a similar type, age, and classification...; (b) all applicable requirements of the relevant classification society...; and (c) all manufacturer's recommended maintenance schedules, service bulletins, and technical instructions..."*

The Claimant has excised **"and normal wear and tear."** For a 19-year-old vessel (the *Sovereign*) and a 14-year-old vessel (the *Pioneer*), that phrase is the difference between a warranty of condition and a warranty of near-newness.

**The suppression.** Paragraphs 12.2, 12.3 and 12.4 are not pleaded at all, and are the paragraphs that actually address the matters the Claimant complains of:

- **¶12.2** warrants that required surveys have been carried out timely and that **"[n]o vessel in the fleet is currently subject to any outstanding condition of class"** except as Fairly Disclosed. It does *not* warrant that a vessel will pass a future survey. SoC ¶86's central proposition — *"A vessel that fails its next scheduled special survey within seven months of the warranty date was manifestly not in good working condition at the warranty date"* — is a non-sequitur against the actual warranty. DL §3.1 records that as at the Closing Date no conditions of class had been imposed on the *Pioneer* and its class remained current and in good standing.
- **¶12.3** warrants that no Group Company has received any notice, report or survey from a classification society, insurer, P&I club, port state or flag state indicating that a vessel *"is or may be in a condition that is unsafe, unseaworthy, or otherwise non-compliant"*, except as Fairly Disclosed. This is the warranty to which the Janssen allegation and the March 2022 BV report are actually directed — and it is not pleaded.
- **¶12.4** warrants that maintenance records and planned-maintenance-system records are complete and accurate and that true and complete copies *"have been made available to the Buyer in the VDR."* The Claimant nowhere pleads that any vessel's records were incomplete or unavailable.

**Consequence.** Two. First, the pleaded warranty is not the contractual warranty, and the damages model is built on the wrong counterfactual (see Issue 32). Second, and more seriously for the Claimant, **any claim under ¶¶12.2, 12.3 or 12.4 is now time-barred** if it was not specified in the 15 February 2024 notice, because the Clause 8.4.2 Warranty Claim Deadline of 30 May 2024 has passed (see Issue 43).

**Strength: Very strong.**

### Issue 8 — Schedule 4 ¶15 is misquoted and misapplied

**The shortfall.** SoC, ¶¶37 and 93 quote the Compliance with Law Warranty as *"The Group has complied in all material respects with all applicable laws, including environmental and maritime safety regulations."* Paragraph 15.1 actually provides that *"[e]ach Group Company has **at all times during the period of three (3) years prior to the date of this Agreement conducted its business and operations** in all material respects in compliance with all applicable laws..."*. The Claimant has deleted (i) the **three-year look-back**, which confines the warranty to conduct on and after 14 October 2019, and (ii) the **"conducted its business and operations"** framing, which directs the warranty at the manner in which the business was carried on, not at the physical condition of individual assets.

**Consequence.** The Second Cause of Action's Compliance-with-Law limb (SoC, ¶¶93–97) is an attempt to take a second bite at asset condition through a warranty that does not address it. Where Schedule 4 contains a specific warranty on the condition of assets (¶12) and a general warranty on compliance (¶15), the specific governs; Clause 7.3's "separate and independent" provision does not convert a compliance warranty into a condition warranty. SoC ¶96's assertions that *"[a] vessel with undetected or unremediated hull cracks... is not in compliance with DNV GL class rules"* and that engine wear breaches *"Lloyd's Register requirements for propulsion machinery maintenance"* are bare assertions of foreign regulatory content, unsupported by any expert evidence of class rules — and the classification societies named are, on the expert's evidence, the wrong ones (see Issue 27).

**Strength: Strong.**

### Issue 9 — The definition of "Losses" is misdescribed

**The shortfall.** SoC, ¶41: *"The definition of 'Losses' in the SPA is broad and encompasses direct losses, liabilities, costs, expenses, damages, and claims of any nature whatsoever, **including consequential losses to the extent that they are the direct result of the breach**."* The SPA Clause 1 definition contains no reference to consequential loss at all: *"'Losses' means all losses, liabilities, damages, costs, charges, expenses (including reasonable legal and professional fees and disbursements), actions, proceedings, claims, and demands of every nature and description, whether or not involving a Third Party Claim."*

**Consequence.** The Claimant has invented a contractual gateway for Heads of Loss 2, 3 and 5 that the contract does not contain. Recoverability of those heads falls to be determined by ordinary English remoteness principles applied to Clause 8.1.1's *"arising from or in connection with any breach of the Warranties"* — not by a bespoke consequential-loss provision.

**Strength: Strong.**

### Issue 10 — Clause 7.5 is not mentioned anywhere in the Statement of Claim

**The shortfall.** The Statement of Claim engages only with Clause 11.3 (SoC, ¶¶46, 119–122). It is silent on **Clause 7.5**, which is the provision most damaging to its case:

> *"The Buyer acknowledges that, in entering into this Agreement, it has not relied on, and shall have no right or remedy (whether by way of rescission, damages, or otherwise) in respect of, any representation, warranty, assurance, or undertaking (whether made innocently or negligently) that is not expressly set out in this Agreement or the Disclosure Letter. The Buyer acknowledges that, save as expressly provided in this Agreement, the Seller makes no representation or warranty, express or implied, as to the accuracy or completeness of any information provided to the Buyer in the course of negotiations leading to this Agreement or during any due diligence investigation, and **the Buyer has satisfied itself, through its own investigations and inquiries, as to the condition and prospects of the Group.** Save that nothing in this Clause 7.5 shall exclude or limit any liability for, or any remedy in respect of, fraud."*

**Consequence.** Clause 7.5 does three things the Claimant does not confront. It records a contractual acknowledgment of non-reliance (D1). It records that the Buyer satisfied itself **through its own investigations** — the contractual embodiment of defence D2. And it expressly disclaims any representation as to the *accuracy or completeness of any information provided... during any due diligence investigation*, which is precisely the complaint made about the VDR at SoC ¶¶40 and 72. It must be pleaded in full in the Statement of Defence alongside Clause 11.3.2, with an affirmative reasonableness case (Issue 22).

**Strength: Strong.**

### Issue 11 — Clauses 8.4.4, 8.5.2(c), 8.6.2, 9 and 3.2 are omitted from the pleading

**The shortfall.** The Statement of Claim's account of Clause 8 (SoC, ¶¶41–45) and its compliance section (SoC, §X) omit every provision that operates against it:

- **Clause 8.4.4** — a Warranty Claim notified but not pursued by commencement of arbitration within **twelve months of notification** is *"deemed to have been irrevocably withdrawn... and shall be forever barred."* Not mentioned. (On the pleaded dates the Claimant is within it, but the provision governs the scope of what was validly notified and must be addressed.)
- **Clause 8.5.2(c)** — the Buyer must *"pursue all available claims against third parties (including, without limitation, under insurance policies, manufacturer's or shipyard warranties, and contractual indemnities from charterers or other counterparties)... and apply the proceeds of any such recovery in reduction of the claim."* Not mentioned, despite DL §3.3 disclosing hull and machinery and P&I cover through Tidewater Assurance Group (London) for the policy year 1 April 2022 – 31 March 2023.
- **Clause 8.6.2** — mandatory credit for insurance proceeds, third-party recoveries and tax relief. Not mentioned. SoC ¶45 acknowledges only the *"no double recovery"* headline of Clause 8.6 without engaging with the credit mechanism.
- **Clause 9 (Conduct of Claims)** — omitted in its entirety, including the obligation at 9.1(a) to notify a Third Party Claim within **twenty Business Days**, and the prohibition at 9.1(b) on *"any admission of liability, agreement, settlement, or compromise"* without the Seller's prior written consent. "Third Party Claim" is expressly defined to include claims by *"any... classification society, insurer, charterer, or other counterparty."*
- **Clause 3.2** — the Purchase Price *"shall not be subject to any set-off, deduction, counterclaim, or withholding whatsoever."* Not mentioned, notwithstanding SoC ¶34's statement that Horizon *"reserves all rights in respect of"* the Second Deferred Payment (see Issue 49).

**Strength: Strong** as to each; cumulatively decisive on quantum and on the counterclaim.

## VI. PART C — THE "FAIRLY DISCLOSED" DEFENCE, VESSEL BY VESSEL

15. **The standard.** Clause 7.2: *"No claim shall lie against the Seller in respect of any breach of the Warranties to the extent that the facts, matters, or circumstances giving rise to such claim have been Fairly Disclosed in the Disclosure Letter... references... include references to the Disclosure Bundle and, subject to the definition of Fairly Disclosed, to the Data Room Documents forming part thereof."* Clause 1: *"'Fairly Disclosed' means disclosed in sufficient detail to enable a reasonable buyer, **acting with the benefit of professional advisers experienced in transactions of the nature contemplated by this Agreement**, to identify the **nature and scope** of the matter disclosed."*

16. Three features of that definition dispose of the Claimant's disclosure case in large part. It is **objective**: the test is what a reasonable, professionally advised buyer could identify, not what Horizon's team in fact noticed. It requires identification of the **nature and scope of the matter disclosed** — not of the matter's ultimate financial consequence, and not a warranty that the disclosed surveyor's prognosis will prove correct. And it is a **contractual** test, entirely distinct from reliance in tort; the two must not be run together.

### Issue 12 — The "14,200 documents were too many" argument is legally misdirected

**The shortfall.** SoC ¶40 and ¶72 argue that *"[t]he sheer volume of documents in the VDR, many of which were irrelevant... rendered the VDR inadequate as a disclosure mechanism."* This is not an argument the definition of Fairly Disclosed admits of. Volume is irrelevant to whether a specifically identified document, in a specifically identified folder, under a specific VDR reference number, enables a professionally advised buyer to identify the nature and scope of a matter. The Claimant reinforces the error by conflating this with knowledge: Clause 7.4 expressly provides that *"[n]o knowledge shall be imputed to the Buyer by reason of anything contained in the Data Room Documents"* — a provision which cuts **against** any argument that the VDR alone discharges disclosure, but equally establishes that the VDR's volume has nothing to do with the Fairly Disclosed analysis. The two provisions do different work and the Claimant has run them together.

**Strength: Very strong.**

### Issue 13 — *MV Nordic Pioneer*: the matter was fairly disclosed with precision

**The record.** DL §3.1, expressly *"Disclosed against: Schedule 4, Paragraph 12 (Condition of Assets) and Paragraph 15 (Compliance with Law)"*, discloses:

- a Bureau Veritas class survey report dated **18 March 2022**;
- located at **VDR/Vessels/Nordic_Pioneer/Surveys/2022/**, document reference **VDR-04731**;
- identifying corrosion **in ballast tanks Nos. 2, 4 and 5 on the port side**;
- with the surveyor's recommendation that those areas be subject to **enhanced monitoring and follow-up inspection at the next scheduled intermediate survey**;
- recording that the vessel **remained in class**, that **no conditions of class had been imposed**, and that the corrosion was flagged as *"areas of concern requiring monitoring"*;
- and confirming that classification **remained current and in good standing as of the Closing Date**.

**Application.** Nature: ballast tank corrosion. Scope: three identified tanks, port side, requiring monitoring and follow-up at the next survey. Source: an identified BV report, at an identified reference, in an identified folder. A buyer with advisers experienced in offshore vessel M&A could not fail to identify the nature and scope of that matter. The disclosure is also deemed by **DL §1.3** to run against every other Warranty to which it is relevant.

**Answering the Claimant's case.** SoC ¶¶70–71 argue that the disclosure *"significantly understated the true severity"* because the vessel failed its special survey fifteen months later. That is an argument that the surveyor's professional recommendation turned out to be optimistic — not an argument that the matter was not disclosed. The Claimant does not, and cannot, say that Bellwood knew the corrosion was worse than BV reported it to be. And the Claimant's own pleading (SoC, ¶65) concedes that the June 2023 survey was the **scheduled five-yearly special survey**: a professionally advised buyer, told in the Disclosure Letter of monitored ballast-tank corrosion in a 2008-built vessel whose 15-year special survey fell due within seven months of closing, was on precise notice of exactly the risk that eventuated.

**Strength: Very strong.**

### Issue 14 — *MV Nordic Endurance*: the deferral and its scope were disclosed and class-approved

**The record.** DL §3.2, disclosed against Schedule 4 ¶12, discloses the deferral of the scheduled dry-docking from Q3 2022 to Q1 2023; that the deferral was **requested by letter dated 8 July 2022** and **approved by the vessel's classification society by letter dated 29 July 2022** (VDR-06218); that approval was conditional on interim inspections which *"were duly carried out"*; that the deferral *"does not affect the vessel's class status as of the Closing Date"*; and — critically — that the dry-docking *"is anticipated to include routine hull inspection, **propulsion system servicing**, underwater survey works, and **any repair works identified during the dry-docking survey process**."*

**Answering the Claimant's case.** SoC ¶79 concedes the deferral was disclosed but argues *"the underlying engine condition was not."* The disclosure expressly identified propulsion system servicing and open-ended repair works arising from the survey as within the deferred scope. A professionally advised buyer told that a propulsion-inclusive dry-docking had been pushed six months to the right, on class approval, with the results of that dry-docking unknown, has been told the nature and scope of the matter. DL §1.3's cross-disclosure provision applies. The Claimant's further contention (SoC, ¶¶79, 118) that the deferral was a stratagem *"to avoid revealing the extent of the engine degradation prior to closing"* is answered by the fact that the deferral was sought from, and granted by, the classification society in writing, on conditions, five months before closing, and then disclosed.

**Strength: Strong.**

### Issue 15 — *MV Nordic Sovereign*: no specific disclosure — our weakest ground, and the Claimant's best point

**Candid assessment.** DL §§3.1–3.4 make no specific disclosure in respect of the *Sovereign*. SoC ¶62 is correct that the vessel is not mentioned in the schedule of specific disclosures. Only the general VDR disclosure at DL §2.1 and the matters-known-to-the-Buyer disclosure at DL §2.3 apply. Clause 7.2 subjects the Data Room Documents to the Fairly Disclosed standard, so a blanket VDR disclosure will not by itself discharge the warranty in respect of a matter the VDR did not squarely address.

**What nonetheless defeats the claim on this vessel.**

- **The warranty is qualified.** Schedule 4 ¶12.1 warrants condition *"consistent with its age **and normal wear and tear**"* — the phrase the Claimant deleted. The *Sovereign* was, on the Claimant's own pleading (SoC, ¶56), **19 years old and the oldest vessel in the fleet**, with (on the Claimant's expert's evidence, ER ¶24) *"an expected remaining useful life of approximately 6 to 8 years."*
- **Causal attribution runs against the Claimant.** The Claimant's own expert attributes the cracking to *"a combination of fatigue, corrosion, and inadequate maintenance of the hull structure **over an extended period**"* (ER, ¶47). Bellwood's ownership was three years on the Claimant's pleading (SoC, ¶17) or five on the expert's (ER, ¶14) — see Issue 26. On either version, an "extended period" of fatigue in a 19-year-old hull substantially predates Bellwood.
- **Discovery post-dates closing by four months and arose in Horizon's own operation.** SoC ¶57 pleads that the cracks were found in **March 2023** during an *"unscheduled inspection prompted by anomalous vibration readings"* flagged by the vessel's master during routine operations under Horizon's management.
- **Records were warranted available and are not complained of.** Schedule 4 ¶12.4 warrants that maintenance records, logs and PMS records were made available in the VDR; the Claimant does not plead that the *Sovereign*'s records were incomplete or withheld.
- **There is no admissible evidence of knowledge** (Issue 20).

**Recommended handling.** Do not overreach on disclosure for this vessel. Plead the qualified warranty, materiality under Clause 7.1, age and wear-and-tear, causal attribution to a period preceding Bellwood's ownership, and the absence of evidence of knowledge. Reserve the disclosure argument to DL §2.3 (matters within the Buyer's actual knowledge from site visits, management presentations and the June–October 2022 written Q&A) pending production of the Q&A log and Horizon's technical due diligence report.

**Strength: Moderate for us** — the *Sovereign* is where the contractual claim, within the cap, is most likely to have some traction.

## VII. PART D — THE FRAUD CLAIM, ELEMENT BY ELEMENT

17. The test is conjunctive. The Claimant fails at E1, at E2 and at E4a independently. Each failure is sufficient on its own.

### Issue 16 — E1: no false statement of existing fact is identified; the case is pleaded as silence

**Standard (E1d).** Conduct can constitute a representation, but **mere silence generally cannot — there is no general duty of disclosure.** A claimant in deceit must identify the representation: what was said, by whom, to whom, when, and in what terms.

**The shortfall.** The Statement of Claim pleads concealment, not misstatement, throughout. The operative language is *"deliberately concealed them"* (¶5); *"Bellwood's failure to disclose the Sovereign's hull condition was deliberate and constitutes fraudulent concealment"* (¶61); *"contained no specific disclosure"* (¶62); *"the underlying engine condition was not [disclosed]"* (¶79); *"Bellwood made no disclosure whatsoever"* (¶116); *"a deliberate strategy of non-disclosure"* (¶117). Section VII.B of the Statement of Claim is headed *"Evidence of Bellwood's Knowledge and Fraudulent Intent"* and identifies *"two principal bases"* (¶107) — the Colton email and the Janssen testimony. **Neither is a statement made by Bellwood to Horizon.** Nowhere in 181 paragraphs does the Statement of Claim plead a single pre-contractual representation: no date, no speaker, no recipient, no words.

**The available theories the Claimant has not pleaded.** A half-truth theory (E1b) — that disclosing the March 2022 report while knowing more rendered the disclosure misleading — is the Claimant's best available route on the *Pioneer*, and it is not pleaded. Nor is a continuing-representation theory (E1c) that the warranty, true when given on 14 October 2022, was falsified before its repetition at Closing on 30 November 2022 under Clause 7.1. The Claimant should not be permitted to reformulate its fraud case in Reply.

**Strength: Very strong.**

### Issue 17 — E2: nothing relied on was ever addressed to Horizon

**Standard (E2a).** The representation must be addressed to the claimant or intended to reach it.

**The shortfall.** Exhibit C-9 is an internal Bellwood email from `dcolton@bellwoodcapital.com` to `rsunderland@bellwoodcapital.com`, sent 17 May 2022 at 14:23:08 -0400, in a thread begun by Ms. Sunderland on 16 May 2022. It was never sent to Horizon and was never intended to reach Horizon. The Janssen material runs the other way: on the Claimant's own pleading (SoC, ¶¶60, 114–115), Northsea's own Technical Manager raised concerns **with Bellwood** — verbally, at a meeting at Bellwood's New York offices, and by telephone. That is an inbound communication to the alleged representor, not an outbound representation to the representee.

**Strength: Very strong.**

### Issue 18 — E4a: the pleaded state of mind is "knew or ought to have known", which is not fraud

**Standard.** Fraud requires that the statement be made knowingly, without belief in its truth, or recklessly careless whether it be true or false (*Derry v Peek*). **An honest but negligent belief is insufficient: "ought to have known" does not establish fraud.** Fraud must be distinctly alleged and distinctly proved, and pleaded with particulars of primary facts that are more consistent with fraud than with innocence (*Three Rivers DC v Bank of England (No 3)*).

**The shortfall.** The Statement of Claim never commits to a fraudulent state of mind:

- ¶5: *"Bellwood **knew or ought to have known** of these deficiencies prior to closing"*
- ¶79: *"Bellwood **was or should have been** aware of the engine degradation"*
- ¶82: *"deficiencies... **should have been**, and in the case of the Pioneer and Sovereign were, known to Bellwood"*
- ¶106: *"Bellwood **knew or ought to have known** that the warranties... were false when given... **In the alternative**, Claimant pleads that Bellwood's conduct constitutes negligent misrepresentation"*

A pleading that alleges knowledge and constructive knowledge in the same breath does not allege fraud. It is precisely the formulation the authorities exclude.

**The evidence affirmatively negates fraud.** Set against the full text of Exhibit C-9:

| Pleaded inference | What the document says |
|---|---|
| Bellwood knew the condition was "problematic" (SoC, ¶69) | Sunderland, 16 May 2022: *"Overall the vessel **passed**, but the report does flag areas of concern regarding ballast tank corrosion requiring monitoring."* |
| Bellwood's concern was self-protective, not disclosure (SoC, ¶110) | Colton: *"Can you coordinate with James on getting **the full BV report and any related maintenance docs** into the data room before the next buyer access window opens on the 24th?"* |
| Bellwood created *"a veneer of disclosure while obscuring the true condition"* (SoC, ¶111) | Colton: *"If Janssen's team has a maintenance plan or timeline for the corrosion work, let's get that in there too **so the picture is complete**."* |
| Bellwood knew the deficiencies were severe | Sunderland: the Northsea technical team *"characterized the findings as **manageable within the next scheduled maintenance cycle**."* |
| Bellwood sought to avoid scrutiny | Sunderland: *"Should we **loop in James Okoro** on this before we do anything?"* — the General Counsel, who in fact executed the Disclosure Letter |

**Consequence.** The email establishes honest belief. On the *Pioneer* the disclosure that followed matched the instruction: DL §3.1 discloses the report with the tanks identified and the VDR reference. The Claimant's fraud case on its own best document is not merely unproven; it is contradicted.

**Strength: Very strong.**

### Issue 19 — The *Endurance* fraud allegation is inference alone, from a disclosed and class-approved commercial decision

**The shortfall.** SoC ¶118 pleads that *"Bellwood's awareness of the engine degradation **can be inferred from the circumstances** surrounding its decision to defer the vessel's dry-docking"*, and that the Claimant *"contends that the deferral was motivated, at least in part, by a desire to avoid revealing the extent of the engine degradation prior to closing."* There is no document, no witness and no admission. The deferral was requested in writing on 8 July 2022, approved in writing by the classification society on 29 July 2022 subject to interim inspections which were carried out, and disclosed at DL §3.2 with the approval letter identified at VDR-06218. A commercial decision that is sought from, conditioned by and granted by an independent classification society, and then disclosed, cannot found an inference of fraud.

**Strength: Very strong.**

### Issue 20 — The *Sovereign* fraud allegation rests entirely on unparticularised anticipated oral testimony

**The shortfall.** SoC ¶¶60, 113–116 plead that Mr. Janssen *"will testify"* that in **"July 2022"** he raised hull concerns **"directly with members of Bellwood's deal team"**, *"verbally during a meeting at Bellwood's offices in New York and subsequently followed up by telephone with the same individuals."* There is:

- no witness statement (Issue 1);
- **no identification of any individual** — "members of Bellwood's deal team" is the entirety of the particularity offered, three times over;
- **no date** beyond a calendar month;
- **no contemporaneous document** of any kind — no email, no note, no report, no minute;
- **no explanation** of why a Technical Manager's formal concerns about the structural integrity of a vessel would be raised only orally.

Mr. Janssen departed in **August 2022** — two months before signature and four months before closing (SoC, ¶¶60, 113). He was an employee of the Target, not of Bellwood; his knowledge is in the first instance the Group's, and Schedule 4 ¶12.4 warrants that the Group's maintenance records were made available in the VDR.

**Consequence.** This does not meet the particularity that an allegation of fraud requires. It should be met with (i) a request for immediate particulars of the individuals, dates and any documents; (ii) a document production request for all communications between Horizon (and its advisers) and Mr. Janssen, and for any engagement, consultancy, employment or payment arrangement between them; and (iii) an invitation to the Tribunal to draw adverse inferences under PO No. 1, ¶41 if none is produced.

**A latent risk that cuts the other way.** If Mr. Janssen communicated his concerns to Horizon **before 14 October 2022**, Clause 7.4 excludes liability to the extent of Mr. Brynstad's or Ms. Devaux's actual knowledge at that date, and reliance fails at E3a. This is a priority production target.

**Strength: Very strong** as a pleading defect; **high value** as a production target.

### Issue 21 — E3: no awareness, no pleaded reliance — but do not expect to win the presumption

**Standard.** E3a: the representee must have been aware of the representation; a concealment never seen cannot have operated on the mind. E3b: the representation must be a real and substantial cause of entering the contract. E3c: **the standing English rule (2019) is that in fraud the presumption of inducement is very difficult to rebut and "a" cause suffices.** E3d: belief in the truth of the statement is not required; suspicion does not defeat reliance (2016). E3e: **an opportunity to discover the truth is no defence; sole reliance on the representee's own independent investigation is.**

**The shortfall.** Horizon never saw the Colton email; it never heard Mr. Janssen's concerns. On the Claimant's own case, the matters said to have been concealed were by definition never before Horizon's decision-makers. Reliance is pleaded only in the abstract, at SoC ¶¶38 and 84 (*"Horizon relied on these representations in entering into the SPA"*), with no statement from Mr. Brynstad or Ms. Devaux and no identification of what document either of them read, when, or what they would have done differently.

**Candid assessment of where we cannot win.** If the Claimant repairs its case at E1 and E2 — by pleading a half-truth on the *Pioneer*, for example — the presumption of inducement will do a great deal of work for it. We should not build the defence around E3c or E3d, and we must not run the argument that Horizon had 14,200 documents and should have found the problem: that argument is squarely excluded by E3e. The two reliance arguments that remain open are:

- **D2 / own investigation.** Clause 7.5 records that *"the Buyer has satisfied itself, through its own investigations and inquiries, as to the condition and prospects of the Group."* SoC ¶38 concedes Horizon conducted technical due diligence. DL §2.3 discloses site visits to vessels, management presentations and written Q&A conducted via the VDR between June and October 2022.
- **Would have acted anyway, on the *Pioneer*.** The very matter said to be concealed was disclosed at DL §3.1 with the tanks identified, and Horizon contracted anyway.

**Strength: Very strong** as against the case as presently pleaded; **Moderate** if the Claimant repleads.

### Issue 22 — Contractual warranties are not representations, and the non-reliance regime bars the non-fraud limbs

**The shortfall.** The Claimant's only candidate "representations" are the Schedule 4 Warranties themselves: SoC ¶121 asserts that *"[t]he warranties given by Bellwood in Schedule 4 to the SPA constitute representations of fact."* That proposition is contentious as a matter of English law: a contractual warranty is a promise as to a state of affairs, not a pre-contractual representation, and giving a warranty in an executed SPA is not, without more, making a representation for the purposes of the Misrepresentation Act 1967 or the tort of deceit. If the warranties are not representations and no extra-contractual representation is pleaded, **there is no representation in the case at all.**

**The non-reliance regime.** If the Claimant instead relies on extra-contractual statements made during the negotiation or due diligence, it runs into three provisions it has under-pleaded or not pleaded:

- **Clause 7.5** — no reliance on any representation *"not expressly set out in this Agreement or the Disclosure Letter"*, and no representation as to the *"accuracy or completeness of any information provided... during any due diligence investigation."*
- **Clause 11.3.1** — the Agreement *"supersedes and extinguishes all prior... representations"*, and *"[n]o party shall have any claim or remedy in respect of"* them.
- **Clause 11.3.2** — non-reliance, plus the express agreement that *"it shall have no claim for **innocent or negligent** misrepresentation based on any statement... not expressly contained in this Agreement or the Disclosure Letter."* This provision is directly fatal to the Claimant's s.2(1) alternative at SoC ¶106.

**The reasonableness gateway — a burden we carry.** Clauses 7.5 and 11.3.2 are basis clauses. **Under the standing English rule since 2018, basis clauses are exclusions in substance and must pass the reasonableness requirement of Misrepresentation Act 1967 s.3 and UCTA 1977 s.11(1); under UCTA s.11(5) the burden of establishing reasonableness lies on the party relying on the clause.** We must therefore plead reasonableness affirmatively and evidence it. The material is strong:

- two substantial commercial parties of comparable sophistication (a EUR 310m-revenue fleet operator; a USD 2.4bn AUM fund);
- each separately and expertly advised, with the Seller advised by Chadbourne & Whitfield LLP (SPA, cl. 5.1) and the Buyer by advisers whose experience is written into the Fairly Disclosed definition itself;
- a negotiation of approximately four months (SoC, ¶19) with a June–October 2022 due diligence process, site visits, management presentations and a written Q&A process (DL, §2.3);
- a **bespoke, negotiated risk allocation** — a full Schedule 4 warranty suite, a Fairly Disclosed qualification, a 30% cap, a de minimis, a tipping basket, an 18-month claims window — which the clause does not displace but complements;
- **express fraud carve-outs in three separate places** (cl. 7.5 final sentence, cl. 8.3.4, cl. 11.3.3), so the clause takes nothing from the Buyer that the law would not permit it to give up.

The Claimant's answer occupies a single unparticularised paragraph (SoC, ¶122).

**Consequence.** If the clauses are reasonable, they defeat **every** limb of the Second Cause of Action other than fraud — including the s.2(1) alternative — and they do so without the Tribunal needing to decide whether the warranties are representations.

**Strength: Strong**, subject to discharging the reasonableness burden.

### Issue 23 — The cap escape is overstated: Clause 8.3.4 apportions

**The shortfall.** SoC ¶100 asserts that *"Clause 8.3 expressly excludes from the cap any claims arising from fraud or willful misconduct, and **the fraud exception applies to the entirety of Horizon's claims**."* Clause 8.3.4 says the opposite in its second sentence:

> *"In the event that a claim is based **in part** on fraud or willful misconduct and **in part on other grounds**, the limitations in this Clause 8.3 shall apply **only to the portion of the claim that is not attributable to fraud or willful misconduct**."*

**Consequence.** Even on the Claimant's best day, the cap survives for every part of the claim not attributable to fraud. The *Endurance* claim is pleaded on inference alone (Issue 19) and is on any view a non-fraud claim; so is the whole of the Compliance with Law limb; so is any part of the *Pioneer* and *Sovereign* claims that fails the fraud test. The Claimant has pleaded no apportionment at all, and its expert *"do[es] not address the applicability of any contractual limitations on recovery, such as caps, baskets, de minimis thresholds, or time bars"* (ER, ¶36) and presents everything *"on a gross basis"* (ER, ¶90). **There is therefore no evidence before the Tribunal capable of supporting any allocation of the USD 87.6m between capped and uncapped claims** — a gap in the Claimant's case, and one it will struggle to fill in Reply given PO No. 1, ¶27.

**Strength: Very strong.**

### Issue 24 — Affirmation: Horizon paid USD 8,500,000 with full knowledge

**Standard (E5a).** Rescission is barred by affirmation after knowledge, by lapse of time, by impossibility of *restitutio in integrum*, and by third-party rights.

**The record.** Horizon knew of the *Sovereign* cracks in **March 2023** (SoC, ¶57), of the *Pioneer*'s failed special survey in **June 2023** (SoC, ¶66), and of the *Endurance*'s engine degradation by the engineering report of **28 August 2023** (SoC, ¶76). Its own counsel wrote to Bellwood identifying the vessel condition issues on **12 October 2023** (ER, ¶26(f)). **Two days later, on 14 October 2023, Horizon paid the First Deferred Payment of USD 8,500,000** (SoC, ¶34).

**Consequence.** Horizon does not seek rescission, and Clause 8.7.1 would bar it on the contractual claim in any event. But the payment matters beyond rescission: it is an unequivocal affirmation of the bargain with full knowledge, and it is powerful evidence against the counterfactual on which the entire deceit measure rests — that Horizon *"would have been in [a different position] had... Horizon not entered into the SPA"* (SoC, ¶123). A buyer that voluntarily pays a further USD 8.5m of purchase price after discovering all three alleged frauds is not a buyer that would have walked away. Add impossibility of *restitutio*: the fleet has been integrated into Horizon's 38-vessel operation since November 2022 and the vessels have been substantially rebuilt.

**Strength: Strong.**

### Issue 28 — The Claimant and its expert disagree on how long Bellwood owned the Target

**Consequence.** The entire liability theory is that the deficiencies *"could not have arisen without a prolonged period of inadequate maintenance and operational neglect **during Bellwood's period of ownership**"* (SoC, ¶50). The Claimant cannot establish the length of that period. The point also bears on Schedule 4 ¶15.1's three-year look-back to 14 October 2019: on the Claimant's own pleaded acquisition date, Bellwood's ownership and the warranty period are nearly coextensive, leaving no scope for the "prolonged" neglect alleged, while on the expert's date the expert's own attribution of the *Sovereign*'s cracking to fatigue *"over an extended period"* (ER, ¶47) reaches back beyond any warranted period.

**Strength: Strong.**

### Issue 29 — Head of Loss 2 claims gross revenue, contradicted by the expert's own model

**Standard.** Damages for lost use of an income-producing asset are lost **net profit**, not lost turnover. Costs avoided during off-hire — crew wages and victualling, fuel, lubricants, port and agency charges, routine maintenance, consumables and the operating share of insurance — must be deducted.

**The shortfall.** Head of Loss 2 is calculated as `days × day rate` with no deduction whatever (SoC, ¶¶139–141; ER, ¶¶53, 57, 60, 63). The expert's own DCF model, in the same report, assumes an *"Operating cost base (per vessel, annual)"* of **USD 3.2 million – USD 5.8 million** (ER, Appendix B). That is approximately **USD 8,767 to USD 15,890 per vessel per day** on the expert's own inputs. Across the 585 claimed downtime days, the avoided operating cost the expert has failed to deduct is on his own figures of the order of **USD 5.1 million to USD 9.3 million**.

**Consequence.** The internal contradiction is fatal to the head as formulated. The Head 2 methodology cannot be reconciled with Appendix B of the same report.

**Strength: Very strong.**

### Issue 30 — Head of Loss 2 assumes 100% utilisation, contradicted by the expert's own model

**The shortfall.** Head 2 assumes every claimed downtime day would have been an earning day. The expert's DCF assumes *"Fleet utilization rate | **85 – 90%** (consistent with pre-acquisition performance)"* (ER, Appendix B; ER, ¶70(b)). Applying the expert's own utilisation range to Head 2 removes 10–15% — roughly **USD 2.4 million to USD 3.6 million** of the arithmetically supportable USD 23.7 million — before any other adjustment.

**Strength: Very strong.**

### Issue 31 — Head of Loss 2 includes downtime that would have been incurred in any event, and downtime that has not yet happened

**Downtime that would have occurred anyway.** The *Pioneer*'s claimed downtime begins with the **scheduled five-yearly special survey** of June 2023 (SoC, ¶65: *"a comprehensive periodic inspection required by the classification society... at five-year intervals"*). The vessel would have been off-hire in dry dock for that survey whether or not it passed. The *Endurance*'s claimed 175 days expressly *"accounts for the deferred dry-docking period"* (ER, ¶58) — a dry-docking that was scheduled, class-approved and disclosed (DL, §3.2), and whose off-hire would have been incurred in Q3 2022 or Q1 2023 regardless.

**Downtime not yet incurred.** The expert confirms the estimates include *"projected future off-hire periods for repair works that remain in progress or scheduled as of the date of this report"* (ER, ¶54; ¶32(b)). SoC ¶144 concedes the same. The Claimant is therefore claiming, as accrued loss, revenue that may never be lost, on repairs that may never be carried out, at rates fixed to a Q1 2023 trailing average.

**Rate/period mismatch.** Day rates are *"trailing 12-month averages as of Q1 2023"* (ER, ¶32(a), Appendix C) applied to downtime running from March 2023 into 2024 and beyond. SoC ¶143's assertion that *"[w]here the actual charter rates under existing contracts differed from the spot market rates, the Expert Report has used the lower of the two figures to ensure a conservative estimate"* is **not supported anywhere in the Expert Report**, which for the *Pioneer* used *"Horizon's existing term charter for a comparable DP2 platform supply vessel"* (ER, ¶56) and for the other two used market data.

**Strength: Strong** on each limb.

### Issue 32 — Betterment: 14- and 19-year-old assets replaced new, with no deduction

**The shortfall.** The repair scopes are wholesale renewals: *"removal and replacement of corroded steel plating and structural members"* and *"application of approved epoxy coatings"* (ER, ¶40); *"complete overhaul of both main engines, replacement of bearings, cylinder liners, piston assemblies, and turbocharger units"* (ER, ¶44); *"removal of cracked and fatigued steel sections, replacement with new steel plating"* (ER, ¶48). The expert states the works are designed *"to restore the vessel to full class compliance and operational capability **for the remainder of its expected service life**"* (ER, ¶41; and see ¶49). No new-for-old deduction is made anywhere.

**Consequence.** The warranted standard was condition *"consistent with its age and normal wear and tear"* (Sch. 4 ¶12.1) — not new steel and new engines. On a 19-year-old vessel with 6–8 years of life remaining (ER, ¶24), the betterment element of a USD 5.7 million hull renewal is substantial. This head requires a respondent's marine engineering expert.

**Strength: Strong.**

### Issue 33 — The wrong measure has been applied, and on the correct measure there is no loss

**Standard.** Horizon bought **shares in a Dutch holding company**, not vessels. Clause 8.7.1 makes a Clause 8 damages claim the exclusive remedy. The measure is the difference between the value of what was acquired as warranted and its value as it in fact was. For the deceit claim (E5b), the measure is transaction-based: what Horizon paid, less the true value of what it received.

**The shortfall — and the knockout.** The Claimant's own expert supplies the numbers (ER, ¶71):

| | USD |
|---|---:|
| Warranted-condition fleet value | **134,500,000** |
| Post-discovery fleet value (actual condition) | **115,600,000** |
| Price actually paid | **112,000,000** |

**Horizon paid USD 112,000,000 for a fleet its own expert values, in its actual defective condition, at USD 115,600,000.** On the correct measure for either cause of action, Horizon received USD 3,600,000 **more** than it paid and has suffered no transactional loss. The USD 22,500,000 gap between the warranted value and the price is the buyer's bargain, not the seller's liability — and it far exceeds the aggregate repair cost of USD 23,400,000 less nothing.

**The compounding defect.** There is no bridge from *fleet* value to *share* value. The DCF values twelve vessels; Horizon bought the equity of Northsea Fleet Holdings B.V. No adjustment is made for the Group's debt, cash, working capital, charter obligations, tax, or the value of the two operating companies as going concerns. Enterprise value has been treated as equity value without a word of explanation.

**Strength: Very strong.** This should be the centrepiece of the quantum defence and of any bifurcation application.

### Issue 34 — Double recovery, contrary to Clause 8.6.1

**Standard.** Clause 8.6.1: *"The Buyer shall not be entitled to recover damages... more than once in respect of the same Loss, regardless of whether such Loss gives rise to more than one claim."*

**The overlaps.**

1. **Head 1 (repair cost) against Head 3 (diminution).** Cost of cure and diminution in value are alternative measures of the same loss. Head 1 restores the vessels to warranted condition; Head 3 measures the shortfall from warranted condition. The expert asserts they are distinct (ER, ¶¶30, 77) but his own methodology defeats him: Head 3 is calculated at ER ¶66 as *"the difference between (a) the projected aggregate fair market value of the Northsea fleet assuming all twelve vessels were in the condition warranted... and (b) the actual aggregate fair market value... following the discovery"* — which is the **full** diminution measure, not a residual after repair. The DCF *"does not incorporate any adjustment for the specific timing of repair expenditures"* (ER, Appendix B) — i.e., the model does not credit the repairs it is said to sit on top of.
2. **Head 3 against Head 5.** Head 3's *"fleet contagion"* discount is expressly *"reflecting the broader **reputational** impact on the fleet as a whole"* (ER, ¶72), and Head 3 is described as capturing *"market perception"* effects (ER, ¶¶30, 77). Head 5 is *"Reputational Harm"* (SoC, §VIII.F). The same reputational injury is monetised twice.
3. **Head 2 against Head 5.** Head 2 claims lost revenue from vessels being off-hire; Head 5 claims lost charter revenue attributed to the same downtime (*"the charterer informed Horizon that it had elected to award the contract to an alternative vessel provider"*, ER ¶82(a); SoC ¶157: *"Horizon was unable to redeploy substitute vessels at short notice to cover the charter commitments that were disrupted"*). The same vessel-days are claimed twice.
4. **Head 4 against the costs claim.** See Issue 38.

**Strength: Very strong.**

### Issue 35 — The "fleet contagion" discount charges Bellwood for nine vessels it is not alleged to have misdescribed

**The shortfall.** ER ¶72 applies *"a smaller 'fleet contagion' discount of approximately 3–5%... to the projected cash flows of the **remaining nine vessels**."* SoC ¶148 rationalises this as *"the market's perception of increased risk"* and *"a heightened risk that other vessels in the fleet may also suffer from deferred maintenance."*

**Consequence.** No warranty is alleged to have been breached in respect of any of the nine. This head charges Bellwood for the market's speculation about vessels whose condition is not in issue. It is loss caused, if by anything, by the discovery and publicity of the three vessels' condition — not by the breach — and it is irrecoverable as too remote and as unproven speculation. On the expert's own figures a 3–5% discount on nine of twelve vessels is a material slice of the USD 18.9 million.

**Strength: Very strong.**

### Issue 36 — The Head 3 comparables are undisclosed and the expert cannot say how many there were

**Standard.** PO No. 1, ¶37: expert reports must comply with the IBA Rules and *"[e]ach expert appointed by a Party owes a duty to the Tribunal, in addition to any obligation owed to the appointing Party"*, and must describe the instructions received. IBA Rules Art. 5 requires a statement of the facts, methods, evidence and information on which the opinion is based.

**The shortfall.** ER ¶74: *"I have identified **two to three** comparable transactions."* The expert does not know how many transactions underpin his cross-check. ER ¶75: *"Due to confidentiality constraints, I am not at liberty to disclose the specific identities of the parties to these comparable transactions or the precise terms thereof."* The "stigma discount" of 12–15% and the "fleet contagion discount" of 3–5% are sourced to *"my assessment of market conditions, industry experience with comparable situations, and the comparable transaction data"* (ER, ¶72) — i.e., to undisclosed data and the expert's own say-so. The detailed DCF outputs are likewise withheld: *"[t]he detailed spreadsheet outputs are **available upon request**"* (ER, Appendix B).

**Consequence.** The sole cross-check on a USD 18.9 million head cannot be tested. Request the model and the comparables; if they are not produced, invite the Tribunal to give the head no weight and to draw an adverse inference under PO No. 1, ¶41.

**Strength: Strong.**

### Issue 37 — Head of Loss 5 is irrecoverable reputational loss, and the two "lost charters" are not what the pleading says

**Standard.** Damages for injury to commercial reputation are not generally recoverable in contract; a lost opportunity to bid is not the loss of a contract; and a non-renewal of an expiring arrangement is the loss of a chance at best, since there is no legal right to renewal. Consequential loss must in any event satisfy remoteness.

**The shortfall — Charter A.** The two documents describe entirely different events. **SoC ¶156(a):** *"A North Sea charter contract with a major offshore operator, with a value of approximately USD 7,400,000 **over the remaining contract term**. The charter counterparty **terminated the contract** citing Horizon's failure to maintain vessel availability commitments."* **ER ¶82(a):** *"This contract was the subject of **advanced commercial negotiations** in Q3–Q4 2023, and Horizon **had been identified as the preferred bidder**. In Q4 2023, the charterer informed Horizon that **it had elected to award the contract to an alternative vessel provider**."* On the expert's account **there was never a contract at all** — Horizon lost a competitive tender. On the Statement of Claim's account there was a contract, and it was terminated **for Horizon's own failure to maintain availability**, which is Horizon's breach of its own charter and a novus actus, not Bellwood's loss.

**The shortfall — Charter B.** SoC ¶156(b) pleads a counterparty that *"declined to renew the contract upon its expiration"*; ER ¶82(b) describes *"[a] 6-month spot charter extension... expected to be a routine **renewal**"* which the charterer *"opted not to extend."* A hoped-for spot extension is not a contractual entitlement. It is at highest a lost chance, requiring proof of the probability of renewal — which is not attempted.

**Remoteness.** The loss is to **Horizon's own** market reputation as a 38-vessel fleet operator (ER, ¶81), arising from Horizon's post-closing operational choices in managing three vessels within its own fleet. SoC ¶159's assertion that this was *"within the reasonable contemplation of the parties"* is bare. Nothing in the SPA allocates to the Seller the risk of damage to the Buyer's separate commercial standing.

**Vessel confusion again.** ER ¶82(b) identifies the Charter B vessel as *"a multi-purpose supply vessel"* in West Africa — which on the expert's typology is the *Sovereign*, but which on the Statement of Claim's typology is a North Sea platform supply vessel (Issue 26).

**Third Party Claim never notified.** If the SoC's version is right and a charterer terminated a contract, that is a Third Party Claim within SPA Clause 9.1 (expressly including *"charterer[s]"*), requiring notice within twenty Business Days and prohibiting settlement or compromise without Bellwood's prior written consent under Clause 9.1(b). Nothing in the record suggests any such notice was given.

**Strength: Very strong.**

### Issue 38 — Head of Loss 4 is arbitration costs dressed as damages, plus unproven management time

**The shortfall.** Of the USD 3,200,000 claimed (ER, ¶79):

- **USD 900,000** in *"[i]ndependent marine surveyor fees... charged by Strandvik Marine Engineering Ltd. for the preparation of engineering assessments and repair cost estimates"* — i.e., the fees of producing the very exhibits filed with the Statement of Claim.
- **USD 1,100,000** in *"[l]egal advisory fees (pre-arbitration)... in connection with the assessment and formulation of its claims against Bellwood... including the preparation of the formal notice of claim under Clause 8 of the SPA and the engagement of counsel."*
- **USD 700,000** for *"specialist technical consultants and naval architects engaged by Horizon **to review and validate the Strandvik findings**."*
- **USD 500,000** of *"internal management time and travel costs... based on **internal cost allocations** provided by Horizon's finance team."*

**Consequences.** *First*, the costs of investigating and formulating a claim, and of instructing counsel and experts for it, are costs of the arbitration recoverable, if at all, under Clause 14.4 and ICC Rules Art. 38 (and PO No. 1, ¶¶42–44) — not damages. Claiming USD 2,000,000 of them as damages while separately seeking costs at Prayer (g) is double recovery contrary to Clause 8.6.1. *Second*, the USD 700,000 of "review and validate" work is a tacit admission that the Strandvik estimates required independent checking — and those validation reports **have not been produced**, though they must exist and are directly relevant to the reliability of USD 23.4 million of repair cost. Request them; invite an adverse inference if withheld. *Third*, internal management time requires proof of significant disruption and diversion from revenue-generating activity, supported by records; "internal cost allocations" prepared by the claimant's own finance team is not that, and SoC ¶153's hedge — *"time records **where applicable**"* — concedes that records do not exist for all of it.

**Strength: Strong.**

### Issue 39 — No credit for insurance, in breach of Clauses 8.5.2(c) and 8.6.2

**Standard.** Clause 8.5.2(c) obliges the Buyer to *"pursue all available claims against third parties (including... under insurance policies, manufacturer's or shipyard warranties, and contractual indemnities from charterers...)... and apply the proceeds of any such recovery in reduction of the claim."* Clause 8.6.2 requires credit for any payment or benefit *"directly or indirectly referable to the facts, matters, or circumstances giving rise to a claim"*, including insurance proceeds and tax relief, with repayment within thirty days if received after payment by the Seller. Clause 8.6.3 requires the Buyer to keep the Seller informed of insurance claims and recoveries.

**The shortfall.** DL §3.3 discloses that the fleet's **hull and machinery and P&I cover was placed with Tidewater Assurance Group (London)** for the policy year 1 April 2022 – 31 March 2023, with policy schedules, coverage limits, deductibles and claims history in the VDR at `VDR/Insurance/`. The expert confirms he reviewed *"P&I and hull insurance policy summaries for the Northsea fleet"* (ER, ¶9(l)). **Neither the Statement of Claim nor the Expert Report contains a single word about insurance recovery, deductions, or claims made.** Hull structural cracking and main-engine damage are paradigm hull and machinery claims. The *Sovereign*'s cracks were discovered in March 2023, within the disclosed policy period.

**Consequence.** Two distinct defences: (i) a Clause 8.6.2 credit, which reduces the claim pound for pound; and (ii) a Clause 8.5 mitigation defence, since a failure to pursue available insurance and shipyard warranties is a failure to mitigate that reduces the Seller's liability under Clause 8.5.3. The Claimant's silence also breaches its Clause 8.6.3 information obligation.

**Strength: Very strong.** Priority production request.

### Issue 40 — The losses claimed are, in large part, not the Claimant's losses

**The shortfall.** Horizon owns shares in Northsea Fleet Holdings B.V. The vessels are owned and operated by **Northsea Offshore Operations Ltd** (England and Wales, company number 08734219, eight vessels) and **Northsea West Africa Ltd** (Ghana, registration CS-041827-2018, four vessels) — SPA Clause 1; ER ¶15. The repair expenditure (Head 1) is incurred by those companies on their own assets. The charter hire foregone (Head 2) is revenue those companies would have earned. The fleet whose value is said to have diminished (Head 3) is those companies' fleet. Yet the Statement of Claim pleads throughout that *"Horizon has suffered"* these losses and claims them as Horizon's own, without pleading that it sues on behalf of the Group Companies, without any assignment, and without identifying which entity bore which cost.

**The contractual answer, and its limits.** Clause 8.1.1 indemnifies *"the Buyer **and each Group Company**"* against Losses *"suffered or incurred by the Buyer or any Group Company"*, which may be sufficient to overcome the reflective-loss objection **if the claim is properly framed**. It has not been. The Group Companies are not parties to the arbitration agreement in Clause 14.2, and the third-party rights provisions of the SPA (Clauses 11.7–11.12) have not been reproduced. The Claimant must be required to identify, head by head and vessel by vessel, the entity that suffered the loss and the basis on which Horizon claims it. Without that, Heads 1, 2 and 3 are, on their face, the losses of non-parties.

**Strength: Strong**, and in any event a valuable requirement for particulars that will expose the double-counting in Issue 34.

### Issue 41 — The expert's independence and compliance with his duty to the Tribunal

**Standard.** PO No. 1, ¶37: an expert *"owes a duty to the Tribunal, in addition to any obligation owed to the appointing Party"*, and the report must contain *"a description of the instructions received from the appointing Party."* IBA Rules Art. 5 applies.

**The shortfall.** ER ¶22: *"**I understand from Horizon's counsel** that the Disclosure Letter did not specifically reference these concerns as a qualification to the condition-of-assets warranty."* That instruction is factually wrong: DL §3.1 discloses the *Pioneer* Survey Report expressly *"Disclosed against: Schedule 4, Paragraph 12 (Condition of Assets) and Paragraph 15 (Compliance with Law)."* The expert lists the Disclosure Letter among the documents he reviewed (ER, ¶9(b)) and says at ¶20 that he *"ha[s] reviewed the Disclosure Letter (Exhibit C-2) and note[s] the disclosures relevant to the Affected Vessels."* He therefore had the document that contradicted his instruction, read it, and repeated the instruction anyway.

**The accumulated disclaimers.** The expert declines to verify the timeline (¶27), the financial statements (¶¶32(d), 34), the repair estimates (¶34), liability and legal causation (¶35), and the contractual limitations (¶36); and he assumes that *"[t]he factual background and timeline of events as described by counsel are accurate"* (¶32(e)). The result is that **no expert evidence connects any head of loss to any breach.** Causation is asserted in the Statement of Claim and expressly disclaimed in the report.

**A dating anomaly.** The report is dated **1 October 2024** and states on its cover *"Prepared in advance of Statement of Claim filed October 7, 2024"* — an assertion the report could not make on the date it bears. Combined with the exhibit-numbering collapse (Issue 2), this warrants a request for the report's version history under PO No. 1, ¶35, which places the burden of establishing authenticity on the producing party.

**Strength: Strong.**

## IX. PART F — CONDITIONS PRECEDENT, LIMITATION AND NOTICE

### Issue 42 — Only the warranties actually specified in the 15 February 2024 notice survive

**Standard.** Clause 8.4.1: no liability for a Warranty Claim *"unless written notice of such claim is given to the Seller in accordance with Clause 8.2 on or before"* the Warranty Claim Deadline, which Clause 8.4.2 fixes as **30 May 2024**. Clause 8.2.1 requires the notice to set out *"in reasonable detail"*: (a) the nature of the claim; **(b) the specific Warranty or Warranties alleged to have been breached**; (c) the factual basis; and (d) a good faith estimate of Losses.

**The shortfall.** SoC ¶¶21 and 173 describe the 15 February 2024 notice only as specifying *"the warranty breaches alleged under Schedule 4 of the SPA"* and *"the warranty breaches alleged"* — without saying which paragraphs of Schedule 4 were identified. The Statement of Claim, filed **7 October 2024**, more than four months after the Deadline, advances claims under Schedule 4 ¶12 and ¶15 and, by implication of its factual allegations, engages ¶¶12.2, 12.3 and 12.4.

**Consequence.** **Any Warranty not specifically identified in the notice is now irrevocably time-barred**, and the bar cannot be cured. Clause 8.2.2's saving for defective notice applies only to failures to give notice *"in accordance with this Clause 8.2"* generally, subject to prejudice; it does not extend the Clause 8.4.1 deadline itself, which is expressed as a condition of liability. This is a high-value, potentially claim-narrowing point that turns entirely on the text of **Exhibit C-4**, which we have not seen.

**Strength: Strong**, pending production of C-4. **Priority production request.**

### Issue 43 — The notice may not have been served at the contractual address

**Standard.** Clause 11.4.1 requires notices to be given *"to the party due to receive such notice... at the address set out below"*. The address specified for Bellwood is **400 Lexington Avenue, Suite 3100, New York, NY 10170**, marked *"Attention: James Okoro, General Counsel."*

**The shortfall.** SoC ¶173 states only that the notice *"was addressed to Bellwood at its **registered address** and was delivered by international courier with confirmed receipt."* That is not the contractual formulation. The Statement of Claim gives Bellwood's address throughout as **415 Lexington Avenue** (SoC, cover page and ¶¶2, 14), as does PO No. 1 (¶10 and the case caption) — while the SPA (Clause 1, definition of "Seller", and Clause 11.4.1) and the Disclosure Letter (addressee block) both give **400 Lexington Avenue**. If the Clause 8.4.1 notice went to 415 Lexington Avenue rather than the contractual notice address, and without the required attention line to Mr. Okoro, the condition precedent to liability in Clause 8.4.1 has not been satisfied and the **entire First Cause of Action falls**.

**Consequence.** This is the single highest-leverage unresolved question on the file. Establish immediately from Bellwood's own records how, where and to whom the 15 February 2024 letter was delivered, and request the courier waybill and proof of delivery in production.

**Strength: Potentially decisive**, entirely dependent on production. **Priority production request.**

### Issue 44 — Delay in notification, and demonstrable prejudice

**Standard.** Clause 8.2.1 requires notice *"as soon as reasonably practicable"*. Clause 8.2.2 provides that failure does not defeat the claim *"except to the extent that the Seller demonstrates that it has been actually and materially prejudiced by such failure"*, with the burden on the Seller.

**The record.** Discovery of the *Sovereign* cracks: **March 2023** (SoC, ¶57). *Pioneer* special survey failure: **June 2023** (SoC, ¶66). *Endurance* engineering report: **28 August 2023** (SoC, ¶76). First letter to Bellwood: **12 October 2023** (ER, ¶26(f)) — seven months after the first discovery, and characterised by the Claimant itself as merely *"preliminary in nature"* (SoC, ¶175). Formal notice: **15 February 2024** — eleven months after the first discovery.

**The prejudice is real and provable.** By the time Bellwood was formally notified, on the Claimant's own pleading, the *Sovereign* had been *"taken out of service and drydocked for emergency repairs"* (SoC, ¶63) and the *Pioneer* had been *"subjected to emergency repair works at the Rotterdam yard"* (SoC, ¶72). Bellwood was thereby deprived of the opportunity to (i) inspect the vessels in their as-found condition, (ii) instruct its own surveyors before the evidence was cut out and replaced, (iii) participate in scoping and pricing the repairs and challenge betterment, (iv) require pursuit of hull and machinery insurance and shipyard warranty claims under Clause 8.5.2(c) while cover was live under the policy year ending 31 March 2023, and (v) exercise its Clause 9.2 right to participate in dealings with the classification societies. Evidence going to the age and cause of the cracking and the corrosion has been physically destroyed by the repairs.

**Strength: Moderate to strong.** Requires evidence of prejudice from Bellwood's own witnesses and a marine engineering expert.

### Issue 45 — The quantum has grown by USD 12.6 million since notification, unexplained

**The shortfall.** The 15 February 2024 notice gave a preliminary damages figure of *"approximately USD 75 million"* (SoC, ¶¶21, 173). The claim is USD 87,600,000 — an increase of **USD 12,600,000**, or 16.8%, with no explanation offered. Clause 8.2.1(d) requires *"the Buyer's good faith estimate of the amount of Losses claimed"* or an indication of the basis of calculation. Given that Head 2 alone contains a USD 6,062,500 arithmetic error (Issue 25), the good faith and reliability of both figures are in issue.

**Strength: Moderate.**

### Issue 46 — Third Party Claims were not notified under Clause 9

**The shortfall.** Clause 9.1 defines a Third Party Claim to include any claim, action or demand by *"any governmental or regulatory authority, **classification society**, insurer, **charterer**, or other counterparty"* that may give rise to a Clause 8 claim, and requires notice within **twenty Business Days**, prohibits admissions or settlements without Bellwood's prior written consent, and requires the Buyer to take such action as Bellwood reasonably requests. On the Claimant's own pleading the following occurred without any notification appearing anywhere in the record: Bureau Veritas imposing **conditions of class** on the *Pioneer* in June 2023 (SoC, ¶66); the classification society requiring immediate remediation of the *Sovereign*'s hull (SoC, ¶58); and the North Sea charterer **terminating a charter** (SoC, ¶156(a)).

**Strength: Moderate**, pending production; contributes to the Clause 8.5 mitigation case.

**Tort defences**

## XI. PART H — COUNTERCLAIM

### The Second Deferred Payment — USD 8,500,000

48. Clause 3.1(b)(ii) required Horizon to pay the Second Deferred Payment of **USD 8,500,000 on or before 14 October 2024**. Clause 3.2 provides that *"[t]he Purchase Price shall be paid in full and shall not be subject to any set-off, deduction, counterclaim, or withholding whatsoever, save as may be expressly provided in this Agreement or as may be required by applicable law."*

49. The record establishes that Horizon has withheld it. SoC ¶34, filed one week before the due date, states that *"Horizon reserves all rights in respect of that payment in light of the claims advanced herein."* The Expert Report goes further and treats the payment as already withheld: *"the second deferred payment of USD 8,500,000, due on October 14, 2024, is the subject of a counterclaim or set-off assertion by Horizon in this arbitration"* (ER, ¶18). That is doubly revealing: Horizon is the Claimant and cannot bring a counterclaim, and **no set-off, counterclaim, deduction or declaration in respect of the Second Deferred Payment appears anywhere in the Statement of Claim's Prayer for Relief**. The expert has been briefed on a position his instructing party has not pleaded.

50. **Recommendation.** Counterclaim in the Statement of Defence (PO No. 1, ¶22 requires any counterclaim to be included there) for:

- USD 8,500,000, being the Second Deferred Payment;
- contractual or statutory interest from 14 October 2024;
- a declaration that Clause 3.2 precludes any set-off, deduction, counterclaim or withholding against the Purchase Price; and
- costs.

51. The counterclaim is liquidated, is supported by an unqualified payment obligation, is protected by an express anti-set-off clause, and is admitted in substance by the Claimant's own expert. It converts Bellwood from a pure defendant into a claimant on a clean debt, changes the settlement dynamic, and gives the Tribunal a straightforward award to make in Bellwood's favour even if some part of the warranty claim succeeds within the cap.

## XIII. PART J — MATTERS CONSIDERED AND NOT PURSUED

58. **Arbitrator challenge.** The record discloses no ground. PO No. 1, ¶8 records that the Presiding Arbitrator was appointed by the ICC Court under Art. 13(3) after the parties failed to agree, and that both co-arbitrators were confirmed under Art. 13(2); ¶9 records that each has filed a statement of impartiality and independence and that no challenge has been received. On the objective, informed-observer standard, none of the recognised factors — overlapping appointments, non-disclosure, financial interest, relationship with a party or counsel, previously expressed views — is engaged on anything in this file. The Claimant's misstatement at SoC ¶26 about the Tribunal's constitution is a pleading error, not a ground of challenge. Any challenge would in any event now be out of time under ICC Rules Art. 14(2) as to anything known at constitution, and would risk serious damage to Bellwood's standing before this Tribunal. **Not recommended.**

59. **Jurisdictional objection.** Clause 14.2.1 is a broad clause covering *"[a]ny dispute arising out of or in connection with this Agreement, including any question regarding the existence, validity, interpretation, performance, breach, or termination"*, and Clause 14.5 makes English law the law of the arbitration agreement. The deceit claim is a *"non-contractual... claim... arising out of or in connection with"* the SPA within Clause 14.1 and falls within the submission. **No viable objection.**

60. **"Horizon should have found it in the VDR."** Legally wrong as a reliance answer: an opportunity to discover the truth is no defence to misrepresentation. The volume-of-documents theme belongs only to the contractual Fairly Disclosed analysis (where it is the Claimant's argument, and a bad one — Issue 12) and to defence D2. **Do not plead it as an answer to reliance.**

61. **Enforcement resistance.** Premature and, on the present record, unnecessary to consider. The only forward-looking point worth preserving is Clause 14.2.5's exclusion of punitive, exemplary and multiple damages, which caps the Tribunal's mandate and would found a challenge at the seat under the Arbitration Act 1996 if exceeded.

### Housekeeping risks in our own documents

62. Three matters in Bellwood's own papers should be anticipated rather than discovered at the hearing:

- **DL §2.1** states the VDR was made available *"from June 15, 2022, through to and including the Closing Date"*, while **SPA Clause 1** defines the Disclosure Bundle by reference to a VDR available *"during the period from June 1, 2022 to October 14, 2022"*, and **DL §4.5** fixes a VDR Cut-Off Time of 11:59 p.m. CET on 29 November 2022. The Claimant may argue the Disclosure Bundle is confined to documents in the VDR as at 14 October 2022. This does not affect the *Pioneer* report (18 March 2022) or the *Endurance* deferral approval (29 July 2022), both of which pre-date every candidate cut-off, but the point should be addressed in the pleading rather than left open.
- **DL §4.4** describes Clause 8.5 as the *"overall cap on liability"* when Clause 8.5 is the mitigation provision and Clause 8.3.1 is the Aggregate Cap. A drafting slip in our own document; correct it in the pleading and do not let it be presented as evidence of confusion about the limitation regime.
- The **415 vs. 400 Lexington Avenue** divergence (Issue 43) cuts both ways: it is a strong point on the validity of the Clause 8.4 notice, but it also means Bellwood's own current correspondence address differs from its contractual notice address. Establish the position from Bellwood's records before pleading it.
