## I. QUESTION PRESENTED AND EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

**Question.** Whether the Arbitrator Disclosure Statement submitted by Hon. Margaret R. Fairhaven (Ret.) on June 17, 2024 satisfies AAA Commercial Arbitration Rules R-16 and R-17, and whether the omissions and inaccuracies identified on comparison with the matter file give rise to justifiable doubt as to her impartiality or independence sufficient to support a challenge under Rule R-18.

**Short answer.** No, and yes. The disclosure is materially incomplete in nine respects and contains five affirmative statements that are inaccurate or materially misleading as written. Assessed cumulatively — which is how the governing test requires them to be assessed — the omissions establish justifiable doubt. The two most heavily weighted considerations under the applicable standard, non-disclosure and financial interest, are both present and both are engaged at or near the top of their range: the arbitrator omitted eight of the fifteen prior or concurrent engagements involving counsel for the parties while certifying that her list was complete, and she disclaimed "direct equity positions" in circumstances where the live question is an *indirect* interest she has admitted she never checked for.

**The single most damaging fact.** The disclosure identifies the arbitrator's shared advisory-board service with Ellen Wu, a senior partner at Respondent's counsel, but omits that Garrett T. Whitmore — Respondent's founder, Managing Partner, and an anticipated adverse witness — sits on the **Board of Trustees** of the same small nonprofit, the body that appoints the advisory board on which the arbitrator serves. An arbitrator who searched the Institute's roster closely enough to find opposing counsel's partner, and disclosed her, but did not report the named party's principal, cannot easily characterise the omission as an oversight.

**Bottom line for our posture.** Four of the five undisclosed connections run in our client's favour. That inverts the usual calculus. The operative risk to Whitmore is not that this arbitrator will be unfair to us; it is that (i) Cascade discovers what we have discovered and challenges her before the AAA, or, far worse, (ii) Cascade sits on the information, loses, and moves to vacate a favourable award for evident partiality under 9 U.S.C. § 10(a)(2), by which time we will have spent roughly $170,000 and eighteen months to obtain an award that will not survive. We therefore recommend **not** filing a challenge, but instead demanding supplemental disclosure through the AAA under Rule R-17(b)–(c) before the June 24 window closes. See Part VIII.

## III. THE GOVERNING DISCLOSURE STANDARD

### A. The contractual standard: AAA Rules R-16 and R-17

The arbitration is administered under the AAA Commercial Arbitration Rules by mandate of Section 12.3 of the Cascade Operating Agreement (Demand § IX). The seat is Minneapolis, Minnesota; the governing substantive law is Delaware law; the Federal Arbitration Act, 9 U.S.C. §§ 1–16, governs enforceability and procedure (*id.*). The Rules are therefore contractually binding on the arbitrator and supply the operative standard.

**Rule R-16** requires disclosure of "any circumstance likely to give rise to justifiable doubt as to the arbitrator's impartiality or independence, including any bias or any financial or personal interest in the result of the arbitration, or any past or present relationship with the parties, their representatives, or their counsel." Two features of R-16 bear emphasis because they defeat the narrow readings the arbitrator's disclosure appears to have adopted:

1. The obligation "shall be construed **broadly** to further the goal of ensuring that arbitrators are and remain impartial and independent."
2. The standard "is intended to encompass **all** circumstances that a **reasonable person** would consider relevant in evaluating an arbitrator's ability to render a fair and unbiased determination."

The test is thus objective and framed from the perspective of the informed outside observer, not the arbitrator's own subjective confidence in her own fairness. The disclosure repeatedly substitutes the latter for the former ("I do not believe that my advisory board service creates any conflict of interest"; "I do not believe that Lakeview's receipt of referrals ... creates a conflict of interest"; "I do not believe that my subject matter experience creates any predisposition"). That is the wrong question, asked by the wrong person.

**Rule R-17(a)** enumerates four non-exhaustive categories, each of which is engaged here:

- **R-17(a)(i)** — "[a]ny known **direct or indirect** financial or personal interest in the outcome," including any interest "in any party, **or in any entity that may be affected by the result** of the proceeding." The rule expressly reaches indirect and beneficial interests.
- **R-17(a)(ii)** — existing or past "financial, business, professional, family, or social relationships with any party ..., its counsel, representatives, **or witnesses**" that are likely to affect impartiality "**or that might reasonably create an appearance** of partiality," expressly including relationships arising from "professional association, joint ventures, board memberships, **academic collaboration**, or regular social contact."
- **R-17(a)(iii)** — "[a]ny prior or pending cases in which the arbitrator has been or is involved ... with any party ..., **its counsel**, or representatives," extending to "**all** matters, whether administered by the AAA or by any other arbitral institution."
- **R-17(a)(iv)** — catch-all for "[a]ny other circumstances that might cause a reasonable party to question the arbitrator's ability to render a fair and impartial determination," to "be interpreted broadly," and expressly including "the arbitrator's prior publications, **public statements, or expressed views on legal or factual issues relevant to the dispute**."

**Rule R-17(b)** makes disclosure a continuing obligation and provides that "[t]he arbitrator shall **not await inquiry** by a party or by the AAA before making any required supplemental disclosure." **Rule R-17(d)** provides that failure to make "complete and timely disclosures" may result in removal under R-18 and "may constitute grounds for vacatur of any resulting award under applicable law, including ... Section 10 of the Federal Arbitration Act."

**Rule R-18** supplies the challenge mechanism, the seven-day clock, and the AAA's decisional criteria — including, notably, that in deciding a challenge the AAA "shall consider ... **the disclosures required but not made**" (R-18(d)). Non-disclosure is thus an express decisional input, not merely a route to the underlying facts.

### B. The statutory backstop: FAA § 10(a)(2)

Section 10(a)(2) permits vacatur "where there was evident partiality or corruption in the arbitrators." The AAA Rules excerpt itself directs parties to this provision twice (R-17(d); R-18(e); Editor's Note). Rule R-18(e) confirms that the AAA's administrative decision on a challenge does not affect a party's right to seek vacatur, and R-18(f) preserves evident-partiality arguments in a confirmation or vacatur proceeding where the grounds "were not previously known and subject to waiver."

*Commonwealth Coatings Corp. v. Continental Casualty Co.*, 393 U.S. 145 (1968), remains the foundational authority for vacatur premised on an arbitrator's failure to disclose a substantial relationship. Circuit formulations of "evident partiality" in non-disclosure cases vary, and the Eighth Circuit standard applicable to a Minneapolis-seated award should be confirmed before any submission relies on it. That verification is flagged in Part IX.

### C. Structure of the test: a balancing standard, applied cumulatively

The doctrinal framework applicable to arbitrator challenges classifies the "justifiable doubts" enquiry as a **balancing test**: a set of weighted factors, no one of which is dispositive, resolved by asking whether an objective, informed observer would conclude that there is a real possibility of doubt as to the arbitrator's impartiality. This is the same enquiry the AAA Rules describe in R-16 ("all circumstances that a reasonable person would consider relevant"), and it converges with the "justifiable doubts" formulation of UNCITRAL Model Law art. 12 from which the AAA's own wording derives. The leading modern treatment of overlapping appointments and the disclosure duty is *Halliburton Co. v. Chubb Bermuda Insurance Ltd.* [2020] UKSC 48.

Three consequences follow, and they shape the whole of Part IV:

1. **No single omission needs to be independently disqualifying.** The correct question is not whether the Halcyon omission, standing alone, would unseat the arbitrator. It is whether the aggregate of the identified circumstances crosses the objective-observer threshold. A defence that picks off each item individually — which is exactly what we should expect from the arbitrator's response — misapplies the standard.
2. **The factors are weighted, and two carry the greatest weight: non-disclosure itself and financial interest.** Overlapping appointments and relationships with a party or counsel carry substantial but lesser weight; previously expressed views carry moderate weight. Non-disclosure is not merely the mechanism by which the other factors came to light; under *Halliburton* and under R-18(d) it is an independently weighted input into the objective assessment.
3. **Custom of the field is a mitigating factor, and only a light one.** The predictable rebuttal — that repeat appointments, referral relationships between ADR centres and law firms, and ADR-organisation board service are ordinary features of a concentrated regional arbitration market — is a real point, and we should assume the arbitrator will make it. But it is weighted lightly, it cuts only in her favour, and it has no purchase on the items that fall outside any market custom: an undisclosed relationship between the arbitrator's spouse and the opposing side's key expert witness, an undisclosed indirect economic interest in a fund managed by a party, and an undisclosed shared organisational role with a party's principal. Market custom in *Halliburton* went to whether a circumstance needed disclosure at all; it does not rehabilitate a certification of completeness that was untrue.

A caution on the authorities in the preceding paragraph: Model Law art. 12, *Halliburton*, and the Hong Kong Arbitration Ordinance (Cap 609) are **not controlling** in a Minneapolis-seated, FAA-governed AAA arbitration. They are used here for the *structure* of the test and for the treatment of overlapping appointments, which is directly transferable because AAA R-16/R-17 adopt the same "justifiable doubt" formulation. The operative authority remains the AAA Rules as contractually incorporated, with FAA § 10(a)(2) as the statutory backstop.

Under *Halliburton*, non-disclosure of overlapping appointments is itself a matter the fair-minded observer takes into account. The affirmative completeness certification converts what might have been an incomplete disclosure into an inaccurate one.

#### Issue 2 — The economic dimension of the B&T relationship is entirely omitted

**What the disclosure says.** Section IX discloses her rate ($850/hour), her time estimate (400 hours), the resulting fee estimate ($340,000), and the 50/50 allocation ($170,000 per side). Nothing else about her economics is disclosed.

**What the record shows.** Over the past 36 months (Appointment Data, *Fee Summary*):

- B&T appeared in **9 of her last 20 completed matters (45%)**, generating **$1,020,000**, or **48.0%** of her total arbitration income of $2,125,000.
- **$652,000 of that $1,020,000 — 63.9% of her B&T-derived income — comes from the six B&T matters she did not disclose.** The disclosure reveals the smaller share of the relationship.
- FM, by contrast, appeared in 5 matters generating $410,000 (19.3%).
- Of her **$835,000 pending pipeline**, **$560,000 (67.1%)** sits in the three matters where B&T appears (Appointment Data, *Pending Matters*, Summary row).
- **This matter alone, at $340,000, is her single largest engagement in the dataset — 3.2× her average matter of $106,250, and roughly 16% of her entire 36-month arbitration income.**

**Rules engaged.** R-17(a)(i) (indirect financial interest in an entity that may be affected by the result); R-17(a)(ii) (business and professional relationships with counsel); and in any event the R-17(a)(iv) catch-all, "interpreted broadly."

**Assessment.** Supports a challenge. Note carefully what this point *is* and is not. It is **not** an outcome-bias argument. B&T's clients prevailed in 7 of 9 matters (77.8%) against FM's 1 of 4 adjudicated (25.0%), but the sample is small, the matters are unrelated, and — importantly for our credibility — the *disclosed* subset does not flatter B&T: B&T's clients won all three disclosed completed matters, so the omissions did not conceal an unfavourable outcome pattern. Arguing bias from win rates would be overreach and would invite an easy rebuttal. What the omissions concealed is **volume and economic concentration**: an arbitrator for whom one law firm supplies roughly half of past income and two-thirds of the forward book, who is presently deciding the largest matter of her post-judicial career, in which that firm appears. That is the disclosable fact, and it was not disclosed in any form.

**Anticipated rebuttal.** Repeat appointments are endemic to a concentrated regional ADR market. That is the market-custom factor, it is genuine, and it is weighted lightly. It also does not answer the point: custom might excuse *having* the relationship; it does not excuse omitting the eight matters that constitute it.

#### Issue 3 — Undisclosed indirect financial interest in Whitmore Fund II LP, and a materially misleading denial

**What the disclosure says.** Section IV: "I hold **no direct equity positions** in any party to this arbitration. I have not purchased, sold, or otherwise transacted in the stock, membership interests, partnership interests, or other equity securities of Cascade ..., Whitmore ..., or any entity that I understand to be a direct subsidiary or affiliate of either party." It then concedes: "I note that **I have not undertaken a position-by-position review of each fund or sub-investment held within my managed accounts**," and disclaims awareness of any material indirect interest.

**What the record shows** (Aldridge Memo §§ III.4, 4.3; SEC filings; Northbridge Select Alternatives Fund public filing dated **March 2024**):

1. The arbitrator's portfolio is managed on a discretionary basis by **Northbridge Wealth Advisors** (per the Disclosure itself, § IV).
2. Northbridge Wealth Advisors also manages the **Northbridge Select Alternatives Fund**, a fund-of-funds product.
3. Per its most recent public filing, March 2024, that fund holds a **2.3% limited partner interest in Whitmore Fund II LP**.
4. **Whitmore Fund II LP** owns **Volaris Manufacturing Group LLC** — the direct beneficiary of the disputed ThermalCore licence (Aldridge Memo §§ 1.1, 1.3).
5. Claimant seeks **rescission** of that licence and **disgorgement of all Volaris profits** attributable to it, in addition to $46.9 million in damages (Demand, Prayer for Relief ¶¶ 1–3). An award granting any of that relief would directly impair the value of Fund II's portfolio and, derivatively, of any LP interest in Fund II.

**Why the denial is defective.** "No **direct** equity positions" is a negative pregnant. It is literally accurate even if the arbitrator holds a substantial indirect interest, because an LP interest held through a fund-of-funds two entities removed is not a "direct equity position" in a party. But R-17(a)(i) requires disclosure of "[a]ny known **direct or indirect** financial or personal interest in the outcome," expressly including an interest "in any entity that may be affected by the result of the proceeding." The rule reaches exactly the structure the disclosure's chosen wording excludes. Where a rule names both direct and indirect interests and the disclosure denies only the direct, the informed observer reads the omission as deliberate — the more so where the same section concedes she never looked.

**Honest limitation.** The record does **not** establish that the arbitrator's portfolio actually holds an allocation to the Northbridge Select Alternatives Fund. The Aldridge Memo says so expressly (§ III.4: "publicly available information does not confirm whether the arbitrator's personal portfolio includes an allocation"). We should not assert the interest as established fact; doing so would be both wrong and strategically costly if the AAA can disprove it in a sentence. **The point to press is not "she holds it" but "she does not know, she said she does not know, and she certified diligent inquiry anyway."** The uncertainty is entirely of her own making and entirely within her power to resolve — a single enquiry to her portfolio manager settles it. Under R-17(b) she was obliged to make that enquiry without awaiting a request.

**Assessment.** Supports a challenge, and engages the second heavily weighted factor. If the allocation is confirmed, this becomes the dominant issue and, in our judgment, independently sufficient. If it is disconfirmed, the value of the point shifts to Issue 9 (defective certification) — which is itself substantial.

#### Issue 4 — Garrett T. Whitmore sits on the Board of Trustees of the same nonprofit whose advisory board the arbitrator serves on

**What the disclosure says.** Section VI discloses her advisory board service at the **Minnesota ADR Ethics Institute** since 2021, and volunteers that **Ellen Wu**, Senior Partner and head of B&T's national arbitration practice, serves on the same advisory board. It describes the board as twelve members, meeting twice yearly, unpaid, with a function "limited to providing non-binding guidance." It concludes: "I am not aware of any other organizational affiliation that would give rise to justifiable doubt as to my impartiality."

**What the record shows.** Per the Institute's **IRS Form 990 filings for fiscal year 2023**, **Garrett T. Whitmore** — Founder and Managing Partner of Respondent Whitmore Capital Partners LLC, and an anticipated adverse witness at the hearing (Demand § VIII.A.2) — is a member of the Institute's **Board of Trustees** (Aldridge Memo §§ 1.1, III.3, 4.2). Further:

- The Board of Trustees is the Institute's **governing body**, responsible for strategic direction, budgetary oversight, and **appointments to leadership positions, including appointments to the advisory board** on which the arbitrator sits (Aldridge Memo §§ 1.1, III.3).
- The Institute is a **small nonprofit** with limited staff; per its publicly available bylaws and posted minutes, both bodies attend annual meetings and hold **periodic joint sessions** on programming and organisational strategy (Aldridge Memo §§ III.3, 4.2).
- The Disclosure does not mention Whitmore's role, and does not address whether **any party or party principal** holds any position at the Institute.

**Assessment.** This is the omission least susceptible to an innocent explanation, and it is why we regard the disclosure's overall good faith as genuinely in question rather than merely imperfect. The arbitrator scrutinised the Institute's roster carefully enough to identify a senior partner at one firm and disclose her by name. The named Respondent's founder sits on the *governing* board of the same twelve-to-thirty-person organisation, in a body that appoints hers. The asymmetry between what was found and what was not is the point: it is difficult to construct a search that surfaces Ellen Wu and misses Garrett Whitmore.

R-17(a)(ii) expressly names "board memberships." Section VI's closing sentence — "I am not aware of any other organizational affiliation that would give rise to justifiable doubt" — is at best an assertion of subjective non-awareness on a matter documented in a public Form 990 concerning a party she was independently obliged to research.

**Anticipated rebuttal.** The two bodies are formally distinct; her role is unpaid, non-binding, and meets twice a year. Granted. But the objective-observer test does not ask whether the affiliation is *substantial*; it asks whether a reasonable person would consider it **relevant** in evaluating impartiality (R-16). An arbitrator and a party's principal in shared voluntary service at a small ethics organisation, where his body appoints hers, plainly clears that bar. And in any event, the failure to *disclose* is separately weighted from the affiliation itself.

#### Issue 5 — The arbitrator's spouse co-authored a peer-reviewed paper with Claimant's key expert witness, and the disclosure affirmatively denies any such relationship

**What the disclosure says.** Section V identifies her husband, **Dr. Alan Fairhaven**, retired professor of mechanical engineering at the University of Minnesota, and states: "He is not currently engaged in any active research collaborations with industrial partners, and **to the best of my knowledge, he has no financial or professional relationship with Cascade Thermal Systems Inc., Whitmore Capital Partners LLC, or any of their officers, directors, or employees**." She adds that she is "not aware of any personal or family relationship with any party, counsel, or individual identified in the arbitration demand that would give rise to justifiable doubt."

**What the record shows** (Aldridge Memo § III.2; academic publication databases):

> **"Optimization of Phase-Change Heat Exchangers in High-Load Industrial Applications,"** *Journal of Advanced Thermal Engineering*, Vol. 42, Issue 3 (**September 2022**). Authors: **Alan R. Fairhaven, Ph.D.; Rita S. Peshkov, Ph.D.**

**Dr. Rita Peshkov** is Vice President of Research & Development at Cascade Thermal Systems Inc., has held that role since 2017, is the named inventor or co-inventor on the majority of Cascade's fourteen patents, oversaw development of the ThermalCore technology, and is designated in the Demand as Claimant's **key expert witness** (Demand ¶¶ 52–53, § VIII.B.2, Ex. H).

**Why the statement is inaccurate, not merely incomplete.** Dr. Peshkov is an officer and employee of Cascade. A jointly authored peer-reviewed paper is a professional relationship with an employee of Cascade. The sentence quoted above is therefore inaccurate as written, subject only to the "to the best of my knowledge" qualifier — and that qualifier is very hard to sustain on this record:

1. The Demand, which the arbitrator states she reviewed (Disclosure § I: "I have reviewed the identities of the parties, their counsel, and **the individuals identified in the arbitration demand**"), names Dr. Peshkov as key expert witness and states that she "has published extensively in the field of advanced thermal engineering, including in peer-reviewed journals such as **the Journal of Advanced Thermal Engineering**" (Demand ¶ 52) — the very journal in which her husband published with her.
2. Her husband is a mechanical engineering professor in the same specialised subfield, in the same metropolitan area.
3. Her Section V statement is *specific*: it addresses "active research collaborations with industrial partners." She was thinking about precisely this category and answered it in the negative.

**Subject-matter proximity.** The paper concerns optimisation of **phase-change heat exchangers in high-load industrial applications**. ThermalCore is described in the Demand as a "next-generation **phase-change heat-exchange** system with applications in heavy industrial manufacturing environments" (Aldridge Memo § 1.2). This is not adjacent subject matter; it is the same subject matter. Dr. Peshkov is expected to testify on "the technical specifications, functionality, and uniqueness of the ThermalCore technology" and on "the technical basis for the valuation methodology" (Demand § VIII.B.2) — testimony the arbitrator will have to weigh, having lived in the same household as one of that witness's co-authors.

**Rule engaged.** R-17(a)(ii) expressly reaches "family ... relationships with any party ..., its counsel, representatives, **or witnesses**" and expressly names "**academic collaboration**" as a covered category. It is difficult to draft a rule that more precisely captures these facts.

**Assessment.** Supports a challenge, and note that **this is the one undisclosed connection that runs against our client** — the relationship links the arbitrator's household to *Cascade's* expert. That makes it strategically the safest item for us to raise and the one least vulnerable to the charge that we are conflict-shopping. It also means Cascade cannot use this issue against us without confronting that its own key expert sits at the centre of it.

**Honest limitation.** The record establishes co-authorship and permits the inference of "a sustained professional collaboration" (Aldridge Memo § III.2). It does **not** establish current contact, a personal friendship, or any communication about this dispute. We should describe the paper and let the objective-observer test do the work rather than characterising the relationship beyond the evidence.

#### Issue 6 — Undisclosed CLE co-panel with Respondent's lead counsel on the exact subject matter of this arbitration

**What the disclosure says.** Nothing. Section III addresses only arbitrations. Section V states that she has "not socialized with, and do[es] not maintain a personal friendship with, any counsel of record." Section VIII addresses her subject-matter experience at length and asserts no predisposition.

**What the record shows** (Aldridge Memo §§ III.6, 4.1; Lakeview and Minnesota State Bar Association CLE archives):

- On **October 14, 2023**, the **Lakeview Dispute Resolution Center** — the ADR centre with which the arbitrator is herself affiliated as a neutral — hosted a CLE panel titled **"Arbitrating Complex IP Licensing Disputes"** at 750 Nicollet Mall, Suite 3200, Minneapolis.
- The panellists were **Hon. Margaret R. Fairhaven (Ret.)**; **Dominic Hargrave**, Lead Partner at B&T and lead counsel for Respondent in this arbitration; Professor Naomi J. Strickland (University of Minnesota Law School); and Marcus DelVecchio (Henning & Burwell P.A.).
- The panel addressed **valuation methodologies, arbitrator selection in technology-intensive cases, and the management of expert testimony** — that is, the three procedural and substantive axes of this case.
- The event was five months before the Demand was filed (March 15, 2024) and eight months before appointment was confirmed (June 10, 2024).

**Two distinct disclosable circumstances.**

*(a) Relationship with lead counsel.* Co-panelling involves shared preparation, coordination of materials, joint appearance, and informal professional interaction. R-17(a)(ii) covers "professional ... relationships" with counsel and "professional association." Section V's denial of *socialising* and *personal friendship* is narrower than the rule and does not answer it. The venue compounds the point: the event was hosted by her own affiliated ADR centre, so this is not a chance encounter at a bar association event.

*(b) Previously expressed views.* R-17(a)(iv) expressly reaches "the arbitrator's prior publications, **public statements, or expressed views on legal or factual issues relevant to the dispute**." A panel on valuation methodology in IP licensing arbitration is squarely within that language. Section VIII of the Disclosure is the section where this belonged: the arbitrator devotes an entire section to her familiarity with "reasonable royalty calculations, licensing economics," and expert-testimony management, and omits the one public engagement at which she spoke on those subjects alongside Respondent's lead counsel.

**Assessment.** Supports a challenge on limb (a) at substantial weight and on limb (b) at moderate weight. Limb (b) should be pressed carefully: the record establishes that she spoke on these topics but **contains no transcript or summary of what she actually said**. We cannot and should not assert that she expressed a view favourable to either side. The disclosable circumstance is the engagement and its subject matter, which R-17(a)(iv) captures regardless of content.

#### Issue 7 — The Lakeview affiliation is disclosed but materially incomplete

**What the disclosure says.** Sections II and VII: she is a panel neutral at Lakeview; Harold Lund is Managing Director; Lakeview has approximately thirty neutrals; it "receives case referrals from various law firms ... **including from both Barrow & Thistlewood LLP and Fielding Marsh & Collier LLP**"; this arbitration was not referred through Lakeview and Lakeview has no administrative role in it.

**What is omitted** (Aldridge Memo § 4.1):

- The **structure** of the affiliation — employment, independent contractor, or **equity-holder / member-owner**. The public record does not disclose it; only the arbitrator can.
- The **financial terms** — whether Lakeview takes a percentage of her fees, receives separate administrative fees on referred matters, or otherwise depends on maintaining referral relationships with firms that retain its neutrals.
- The **volume and materiality** of B&T's and FM's referrals to Lakeview.
- That **Lakeview hosted the October 14, 2023 CLE** at which she and Mr. Hargrave co-panelled (Issue 6).

**Assessment.** Contributory only, on the present record. Volunteering the existence of referral relationships is a point in the arbitrator's favour and we should acknowledge it. But she disclosed the fact while withholding every variable that would let a party evaluate it, and she then resolved the question herself ("I do not believe that Lakeview's receipt of referrals from counsel for either party creates a conflict of interest") — which, under the objective standard in R-16, is not hers to resolve. If the affiliation turns out to be an equity or revenue-share arrangement, this migrates from Issue 7 to Issue 3 and becomes a financial-interest question of heavy weight. **This is the single most valuable target for a supplemental-disclosure request**, because the answer is unobtainable from public sources and could be materially adverse.

#### Issue 8 — Ellen Wu's role in arbitrator selection for this matter is omitted

**What the disclosure says.** Section VI names Ms. Wu, describes her as Senior Partner and head of B&T's arbitration practice, notes their shared advisory board service since 2021, and states that their interactions have been "limited to attendance at advisory board meetings and related correspondence concerning Institute programming."

**What the record shows.** Ms. Wu "supervises the firm's arbitration practice and **is involved in arbitrator selection strategy for matters handled by the practice group, including this arbitration**" (Aldridge Memo § 2.1).

**Assessment.** Contributory only, and it requires care from us: this fact is sourced to our own client's internal knowledge of its counsel's staffing, and the arbitrator could not reasonably be expected to know it. It is not a disclosure failure by her. Its significance is different and, for us, uncomfortable: **the B&T partner who shares a board with the arbitrator is the partner who participated in selecting her**, and Respondent ranked her **first** while Claimant ranked her third (Aldridge Memo § III.1). If Cascade develops this, the narrative writes itself. We flag it here because it materially raises the vacatur risk discussed in Part VII, not because it strengthens a challenge we would bring.

#### Issue 9 — The certification is internally inconsistent, and the disclosure misstates the challenge deadline

**(a) "Diligent inquiry."** Section X certifies: "**based on a diligent inquiry**, the foregoing represents all circumstances known to me that might give rise to justifiable doubt." That certification cannot be reconciled with two admissions elsewhere in the same document:

- Section IV: "**I have not undertaken a position-by-position review** of each fund or sub-investment held within my managed accounts."
- Section III: "To the best of my **recollection** ..." — where AAA records of her own docket exist and would have produced the complete list in minutes.

A disclosure that certifies diligent inquiry while conceding that no inquiry was made into the arbitrator's own financial holdings, and that the appointment list rests on memory rather than records, is defective on its face. Rule R-17(b) is explicit that the arbitrator "shall **not await inquiry** by a party or by the AAA before making any required supplemental disclosure."

**(b) The R-18 clock.** Section X states that "the parties have until June 24, 2024 — seven (7) calendar days from the date of this disclosure — to raise any objection," and that absent objection "I will proceed with my duties as Sole Arbitrator."

That misstates Rule R-18(a), which sets the seven-day period running from the **later** of (i) communication of the appointment, or (ii) the date "the circumstances giving rise to the challenge **became known or should reasonably have become known** to the challenging party." It also ignores Rule R-18(f), which preserves challenges "based on grounds that were not and could not reasonably have been known at the time of the deadline," and preserves evident-partiality arguments at the confirmation or vacatur stage.

The practical consequence is significant and favourable to whichever party is disadvantaged: **grounds arising from facts the arbitrator did not disclose cannot have "become known" from a disclosure that omitted them.** The arbitrator is also not the officer who administers R-18; the AAA is. We do not press this as a stand-alone ground of partiality — it reads more like drafting overreach than bias — but it is an inaccuracy the client asked us to identify, it forecloses any waiver argument premised on a hard June 24 cutoff, and it fits a pattern in which every ambiguity in this document resolves in the direction of less disclosure and less scrutiny.

**Assessment.** Substantial weight, principally as an aggravator. It converts Issues 1–3 from "she did not disclose X" into "she certified diligence she did not exercise, and the specific inquiries she omitted are the ones that would have surfaced X."

## V. CUMULATIVE ASSESSMENT UNDER THE BALANCING STANDARD

The governing test is a weighted balance, resolved by asking whether an objective, informed observer would consider that there is a real possibility of doubt as to impartiality. No single factor is dispositive; the factors below are weighted, and the correct output is a cumulative judgment. The tiering is used here as an ordering and emphasis device, not as an arithmetic formula — nothing in the AAA Rules assigns numerical weights.

| Factor | Tier | Present? | Record basis | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Failure to disclose** | **Heavy** | **Strongly — near the top of range** | 8 of 15 engagements omitted against an express completeness certification (Issue 1); Whitmore trusteeship omitted while Wu disclosed (Issue 4); spouse/witness co-authorship denied (Issue 5); CLE omitted (Issue 6); "diligent inquiry" certified without inquiry (Issue 9) | Against arbitrator |
| **Financial interest** | **Heavy** | **Contingently — unresolved by her own choice** | Northbridge → Select Alternatives Fund → 2.3% LP in Whitmore Fund II → Volaris (Issue 3); admitted non-review of holdings; undisclosed Lakeview financial terms (Issue 7) | Against arbitrator if confirmed; the *non-inquiry* counts against her regardless |
| **Overlapping appointments** | Substantial | **Strongly** | 11 prior/concurrent B&T engagements, 3 concurrent; 48.0% of 36-month income; 67.1% of pending pipeline; largest matter of her career (Issues 1–2) | Against arbitrator |
| **Relationship with party or counsel** | Substantial | **Strongly, and across all three vectors** | Party principal (Whitmore, Issue 4); opposing witness via spouse (Peshkov, Issue 5); lead counsel (Hargrave, Issue 6) | Against arbitrator |
| **Previously expressed views** | Moderate | **Partially** | Oct. 14, 2023 CLE on IP licensing valuation and expert management; content unknown (Issue 6(b)) | Mildly against arbitrator |
| **Custom of the field** | **Light** (mitigating) | Yes | Repeat appointments, ADR-centre referral relationships, and ADR board service are ordinary in a concentrated regional market | **For arbitrator** |

**Conclusion.** Both heavily weighted factors are engaged; both substantially weighted factors are engaged, the relationship factor across all three of its vectors; the moderate factor is partially engaged; and the only mitigating factor is the lightest available and does not reach the items that matter most. On any reasonable application of a cumulative balancing standard, **the threshold of justifiable doubt is met.**

Three points sharpen the conclusion:

1. **The defence will be atomistic, and that is the wrong method.** Each item has an individually plausible answer: repeat appointments are normal; the boards are formally distinct; the paper is her husband's, not hers; the CLE was a public professional event; the fund interest is unconfirmed. Under a conjunctive or gateway test those answers might matter individually. Under a balancing test they do not, because the question is the aggregate impression on an informed observer, and the aggregate here is an arbitrator connected to the Respondent's side through a party principal, a lead counsel, a law firm supplying half her income, and possibly a fund holding — none of which she disclosed — while separately connected to the Claimant's key expert through her spouse, which she also did not disclose and effectively denied.

2. **The pattern of what was disclosed is more damaging than any single omission.** She disclosed Ellen Wu but not Garrett Whitmore. She disclosed Orion but not Halcyon. She disclosed three B&T matters but not six. She disclosed the *existence* of Lakeview referrals from both firms but no term by which they could be evaluated. She disclosed her rate and this matter's fee but not that one firm supplies 48% of her income. Every one of these pairs shows the less significant fact disclosed and the more significant fact withheld. That pattern is itself evidence bearing on the objective assessment, and it is what makes the "inadvertence" defence hard to sustain across nine items.

3. ***Halliburton* cuts both ways and we should say so.** It supports the proposition that non-disclosure of overlapping appointments is itself weighed by the fair-minded observer. But on its facts the UKSC declined to remove the arbitrator, emphasising the custom of the relevant market. The distinctions here are real and should be stated plainly rather than elided: this case involves not two overlapping references but eleven engagements with one firm; not a common party but a common counsel with quantified economic concentration; and — decisively — undisclosed relationships with a **party's principal**, a **key witness**, and a possible **financial interest**, none of which any market custom addresses.

## VI. TIMING AND WAIVER

**The rule.** Rule R-18(a): a challenge must be made by written notice to the AAA and the other party, "stating with specificity the grounds," within seven calendar days after the **later** of (i) communication of the appointment, or (ii) the date the circumstances "became known or should reasonably have become known" to the challenging party.

**Application to the two categories of grounds.**

| Category | Trigger | Deadline |
|---|---|---|
| Grounds apparent on the face of the Disclosure — Lakeview referrals from both firms (Issue 7, in part); Wu advisory-board overlap (Issue 4, in part); admitted non-review of holdings and defective certification (Issues 3, 9) | Receipt of the Disclosure, **June 17, 2024** | **June 24, 2024** |
| Grounds first ascertainable from public records compiled **June 19, 2024** — omitted engagements including Halcyon; economic concentration; Whitmore trusteeship; Peshkov co-authorship; Hargrave CLE (Issues 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6) | **June 19, 2024** | **June 26, 2024** |

**Waiver exposure.** Rule R-18(f) deems a party to have waived grounds "then known" if it does not challenge timely, but expressly preserves grounds "that were not and could not reasonably have been known," and further preserves evident-partiality arguments in a confirmation, modification, or vacatur proceeding subject to the same qualification.

**Practical direction.** Act by **June 24, 2024** for everything. The June 26 date should be treated as a fallback, not a plan: the second category rests on the proposition that facts in public Form 990s, SEC filings, CLE archives, and AAA award databases "could not reasonably have been known" earlier — a proposition that weakens with every day we hold information we already have. A party that possesses the Aldridge Memo as of June 19 and files on June 26 invites the argument that it should reasonably have known sooner. **Filing within the June 24 window moots the entire question.**

The arbitrator's own statement that objections are due June 24 does not bind the AAA and does not shorten R-18(a) as to undisclosed grounds (Issue 9(b)). But it is a date the AAA is likely to treat as salient, which is a further reason to meet it.

## VII. RISKS SPECIFIC TO OUR POSTURE

This analysis would end differently if we acted for Cascade. We do not, and the asymmetry must drive the recommendation.

**The connections run our way.** Of the five undisclosed connections, four favour Respondent: the arbitrator's possible indirect interest in Whitmore Fund II; her shared Institute service with Garrett Whitmore; her CLE co-panel with Mr. Hargrave; and her economic reliance on B&T. Only one — the spouse's co-authorship with Dr. Peshkov — favours Cascade. Add that Respondent ranked her **first** and Cascade ranked her **third** (Aldridge Memo § III.1), and that the B&T partner who shares her advisory board participated in arbitrator selection strategy for this matter (Issue 8), and the optics from Cascade's side are poor in a way that no explanation from us improves.

**Three risks follow, in ascending order of severity.**

1. **Cascade challenges before the AAA.** Recoverable. Under Rule R-19 a replacement is appointed and the replacement decides in her discretion what, if anything, must be repeated. Cost and delay, but no lasting damage.

2. **We stay silent and are later held to have waived.** If we press on knowing what we know, we forfeit our own ability to raise these grounds later (R-18(f)), and we place the firm in the position of having possessed a detailed relationship map since June 19 and said nothing. That is uncomfortable in itself and worse if it emerges in a vacatur proceeding.

3. **We win, and Cascade moves to vacate under FAA § 10(a)(2).** This is the controlling risk. Rule R-17(d) states expressly that failure to make complete and timely disclosures "may constitute grounds for vacatur of any resulting award under applicable law, including ... Section 10 of the Federal Arbitration Act," and Rule R-18(e) confirms that the AAA's administrative disposition of any challenge does not affect that right. The exposure is concrete: **$46.9 million in compensatory damages, plus rescission of the March 1, 2023 licence and disgorgement of all Volaris profits** (Demand, Prayer ¶¶ 1–3). A defence award on those claims is exactly the award Cascade would attack, and it would attack it with facts that were available on June 19, 2024, that we knew, and that the arbitrator did not disclose. Fifteen hearing days are scheduled for January 13 – February 7, 2025, at an estimated $170,000 to our client in arbitrator fees alone before counsel time. An award vacated for evident partiality would cost all of that and return the parties to the start.

**The uncomfortable corollary.** The best protection for a favourable award is a full and unambiguous record of disclosure made *before* the hearing — even though every fact that record would surface is a fact that helps Cascade argue this arbitrator was too close to us. Curing the defect is worth more than the tactical advantage of an undisclosed tilt, because an undisclosed tilt is not an advantage; it is a latent defect in the award.

## VIII. RECOMMENDATION

**1. Do not file a Rule R-18 challenge. File a written request for supplemental disclosure under Rule R-17(b)–(c), addressed to the AAA with a copy to Fielding Marsh & Collier, on or before June 24, 2024.**

This is the move that captures the benefit of a challenge without its cost. It stops the waiver clock by putting the grounds on the record with specificity; it obtains the two facts we cannot get from public sources (the fund allocation and the Lakeview terms); it demonstrates that Respondent raised the issues rather than concealing them, which is the single most useful fact we could have in a later vacatur fight; and it preserves the arbitrator, who has not in fact done anything unfair to either side. Draft it as a neutral request for completeness, not as an accusation. Reserve all rights under R-18(a) and R-18(f) expressly.

**2. Request supplemental disclosure on each of the following, itemised:**

| # | Request |
|---|---|
| (a) | A **complete** list of all matters, AAA-administered or otherwise, in which B&T or FM has appeared before her in any capacity, 2019 to date, with case number, role, dates, and status — specifically including *Crestfield*, *Ashford*, *Westbrook*, *Trident*, *Redstone*, *Thornwall*, *Warwick*, and the pending ***Halcyon Biotech v. Stonewall Partners*** |
| (b) | All **currently pending** matters in which either firm appears, and her estimated remaining fees in each |
| (c) | Aggregate fees received from matters in which each firm appeared, over 36 months, in absolute terms and as a share of total arbitration income |
| (d) | Whether her Northbridge-managed portfolio holds, directly or indirectly, any interest in the **Northbridge Select Alternatives Fund** or any vehicle with exposure to **Whitmore Fund I, II, or III LP**, or to **Volaris Manufacturing Group LLC** — with a direct enquiry to Northbridge Wealth Advisors rather than reliance on recollection |
| (e) | The **structure and financial terms** of her Lakeview affiliation: employment, contractor, or equity/member-owner; any fee share or administrative charge; and the volume of B&T and FM referrals to Lakeview |
| (f) | Any knowledge of **Garrett T. Whitmore's** service on the Minnesota ADR Ethics Institute Board of Trustees, and any interaction with him at Institute meetings or joint sessions since 2021 |
| (g) | Any professional or personal relationship between **Dr. Alan Fairhaven** and **Dr. Rita Peshkov**, including the September 2022 co-authored paper, and any contact between them since |
| (h) | All CLE, conference, or panel engagements since 2021 at which counsel of record appeared, or at which she addressed IP licensing valuation or arbitrator selection — including the **October 14, 2023** Lakeview panel — together with any materials, slides, or published remarks |

**3. Hold the challenge in reserve, and pre-commit to the trigger.** If supplemental disclosure confirms (i) a portfolio allocation reaching Whitmore Fund II, or (ii) an equity or revenue-share interest in Lakeview coupled with material B&T referral volume, the financial-interest factor moves to the top of its band and a challenge becomes the correct course notwithstanding that the interest tilts our way — because such an interest is very likely to sustain a § 10(a)(2) vacatur later, and an award we cannot keep is worth nothing. Decide that in advance, in writing, so it is not relitigated under time pressure.

**4. Consider a joint approach to Fielding Marsh.** A jointly signed request for supplemental disclosure, or an agreed waiver following full disclosure, is the cleanest available outcome: it produces a complete record and a documented waiver by both sides, which together substantially foreclose a later evident-partiality attack. Weigh against this that it alerts Cascade to material it has not yet found. On balance we favour it, because Cascade's counsel can reach every one of these facts from public sources — Form 990s, EDGAR, CLE archives, and AAA award databases — and is likely to do so before a $46.9 million hearing.

**5. Preserve the record.** Diary the June 24 and June 26 dates. Preserve the public-source materials underlying the Aldridge Memo in a form that can be produced without the memorandum itself. Log the date on which each fact became known to the firm; if waiver is ever litigated, that log is the evidence.

**6. Escalate.** The client should be advised before June 24. The decision whether to surface material that is, on balance, favourable to it is the client's to make, and it should be made on a full explanation of the vacatur asymmetry in Part VII.

That question is unresolved on this record and could add a further ground.

**Data-integrity discrepancies in the source materials.** Three, none of which alters the conclusions, all of which should be checked before any figure is put in a filing:

1. **Undisclosed-matter count.** The *Case List* Summary row states "Undisclosed matters involving B&T or FM: 5." Its own Notes column flags **six** rows as "Not disclosed by arbitrator," and a seventh — *Thornwall Systems v. Kessler Automation* (01-23-0002643, FM as respondent counsel) — is absent from the Disclosure though unflagged. **The row-level data is the reliable layer; the correct figure is seven undisclosed completed matters, plus Halcyon Biotech pending, for eight in total.** Similarly, the Summary row's "Disclosed matters: 7 of 20" appears to include Orion, which sits on the *Pending Matters* sheet; only six of the twenty completed matters were disclosed.

2. **FM fee figures.** The Aldridge Memo (§ III.5) reports FM involvement in "four (4)" completed matters generating "$280,000 (13.2%)." The *Fee Summary* sheet reports **five** matters generating **$410,000 (19.3%)**. The memo's count appears to track adjudicated matters (excluding the 2021 settlement) while its fee figure excludes *Westbrook* ($130,000) yet includes that settlement ($55,000) — an internally inconsistent basis. **Use the workbook figures.** The discrepancy understates FM and therefore overstates the B&T-to-FM asymmetry in the memo's favour; correcting it slightly narrows, but does not close, the gap.

3. **Total-income denominator.** The *Fee Summary* uses **$2,125,000** as total 36-month arbitration income, but the *Case List* line items sum to **$1,974,000** — an unexplained $151,000. Percentages in this memorandum use the workbook's $2,125,000 denominator, which is the **conservative** choice: on the line-item denominator, B&T's share rises from **48.0% to 51.7%**. The discrepancy does not weaken the concentration point and, if anything, understates it.

**Evidentiary limitations.**

- The **Northbridge allocation is not established.** The record shows only that the same adviser manages both her portfolio and a fund holding 2.3% of Whitmore Fund II. Do not assert the holding as fact (Issue 3).
- The **content of the October 14, 2023 CLE remarks is unknown.** No transcript or materials are in the file. Do not assert that she expressed any view on any issue (Issue 6(b)).
- The **current state of the Fairhaven–Peshkov relationship is unknown.** The record establishes co-authorship of a September 2022 paper and supports an inference of sustained collaboration during its preparation; it does not establish present contact (Issue 5).
- **Fee figures throughout are estimates** derived from public sources and extrapolation, as the Aldridge Memo states (§ III.5). Actual fees may differ.
- **Ellen Wu's role in arbitrator selection** (Issue 8) is sourced to internal knowledge and is not a fact the arbitrator could have been expected to disclose.
