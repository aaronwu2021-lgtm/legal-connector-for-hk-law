## 1. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

### 1.1 Bottom line

The Final Award is, on the whole, a robust and enforceable instrument. Pacífico's procedural and jurisdictional objections are weak, largely waived, and in several instances contradicted by Pacífico's own pleadings and by the concurrence of its own party-appointed arbitrator. The Award is final and binding, no set-aside application was ever filed at the seat, and the window to file one has closed.

**There is, however, one serious defect, and it is the defect Dra. Olvera identified.** The Award's dispositif orders payment of **USD $14,700,000**. The Tribunal's own reasoning — and the Tribunal's own summary table and its own express declaration at paragraph 86 — support a total of **USD $9,530,861**. The difference is **USD $5,169,139**, and there is nothing anywhere in the Award that explains it. This is not a matter of interpretation. I have re-performed every step of the Tribunal's damages calculation and it reconciles exactly to $9,530,861. The $14,700,000 figure appears in the Award precisely once, in operative paragraph 6, and is supported by nothing.

That single defect is the whole of Greenleaf's enforcement risk. Every other ground Pacífico can raise is weak. This one is capable, if mishandled, of defeating enforcement in its entirety.

### 1.2 Principal recommendation

**Greenleaf should apply to the Juzgado de Distrito in Guadalajara for recognition and enforcement of USD $9,530,861 — the figure the Tribunal itself declared at paragraph 86 of the Award — plus post-award interest at 5% per annum simple from June 28, 2023, expressly reserving all rights as to the balance.**

Greenleaf should **not** seek enforcement of the $14,700,000 dispositif figure. The reasons are set out in full at §15. In short: the unreasoned $5.17 million is the only argument Pacífico has that a Guadalajara judge could plausibly accept; pressing it converts Pacífico's weakest overall position into its strongest single point; and it creates a real risk that the court refuses the *entire* award rather than severing it — particularly given the local bench's demonstrated willingness (two or three cases over 2013–2023, on Dra. Olvera's account) to refuse enforcement on *orden público* grounds. Voluntarily claiming the reasoned figure removes the hook altogether and reframes Greenleaf, before a court that has not yet formed a view, as the party seeking only what the Tribunal actually reasoned.

## 3. THE GOVERNING LEGAL FRAMEWORK

### 3.1 The Convention applies without threshold obstacle

Dra. Olvera's summary of the treaty position is correct and I confirm it. Mexico acceded to the Convention on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards (New York, 1958) on **April 14, 1971**, without the reciprocity reservation and without the commercial reservation. Canada acceded on **May 12, 1986**. One nuance: Canada's accession was accompanied by a declaration applying the Convention only to differences arising out of legal relationships considered commercial under Canadian law (except as regards Quebec). That declaration governs enforcement *in Canada* and is irrelevant to enforcement in Mexico; in any event this dispute — an exclusive distribution agreement between two trading companies — is commercial on any view.

The Award was made in Calgary, Alberta, Canada. From the perspective of the Mexican enforcement court it is therefore a **foreign award**, and Mexico's non-reservation position means the Convention applies irrespective of the seat's treaty status. There is no threshold admissibility issue.

### 3.2 The Mexican implementing provisions

The Convention is implemented in Mexican law through Book Five, Title Four of the **Código de Comercio**. For the enforcement application it will be useful to keep the provisions distinct, because Dra. Olvera's letter refers to Articles 1457 and 1462 together:

- **Article 1461** — the documentary prerequisites for a party relying on an award (the Article IV requirements: the duly authenticated original award or a certified copy, the original arbitration agreement or a certified copy, and a certified translation where required).
- **Article 1462** — the **operative provision for the present purpose**: the grounds on which recognition and enforcement of an award may be refused. Its structure mirrors Article V of the Convention: Article 1462(I) corresponds to Article V(1) (grounds that must be pleaded and proved by the party resisting), and Article 1462(II) corresponds to Article V(2) (non-arbitrability and public policy, which the court may raise of its own motion).
- **Article 1463** — the procedural route (the *incidente* by which recognition and enforcement is sought).
- **Article 1457** — the grounds for **annulment** (*nulidad*) of an award. This article is the Mexican analogue of Model Law Article 34 and applies to awards whose seat is in Mexico. It is *not* the provision under which Pacífico will resist enforcement of a Calgary award, and the distinction is worth preserving in the petition: Pacífico has no annulment remedy in Mexico in respect of a foreign award, only a resistance remedy under Article 1462.

### 3.3 The five structural principles that govern every ground below

Each of the following is settled, and each should be stated expressly and early in the enforcement petition, because together they do most of the work of defeating Pacífico's opposition.

**(a) The grounds are exhaustive.** Article V sets out a closed list. A court asked to enforce a Convention award has no residual discretion to refuse on grounds outside it. Pacífico cannot invoke a general appeal to fairness, commercial hardship, or the size of the award.

**(b) The burden lies wholly on Pacífico.** Article V(1) opens: "Recognition and enforcement of the award may be refused, at the request of the party against whom it is invoked, **only if that party furnishes to the competent authority** ... **proof** that ...". Greenleaf's affirmative burden is discharged by satisfying Article IV. Everything thereafter is Pacífico's to plead and prove. Only the Article V(2) grounds may be raised by the court of its own motion.

**(c) The grounds are construed narrowly, in favour of enforcement.** The Convention's object and purpose is the effective circulation of awards, and the near-universal practice of Contracting States is to read Article V restrictively. Dra. Olvera confirms this is the prevailing interpretive trend in the Mexican federal courts.

**(d) There is no review of the merits.** The enforcement court does not sit in appeal. It does not reconsider the Tribunal's findings of fact, its construction of the contract, its choice of damages methodology, or its assessment of the expert evidence. *No hay revisión del fondo.* This principle disposes, at a stroke, of the great majority of what Pacífico will want to argue: the force majeure ruling, the COFEPRIS ruling, the 35% gross margin, the Years 4 and 5 projection, the 15% mitigation discount, and the reasonableness of the legal fees are all merits determinations placed beyond review. **The single qualification** — and it is the one that matters here — is that an award whose operative order contradicts its own reasons is not challenged on the merits at all; the objection is that the award is internally incoherent, which is a formal, not a substantive, defect.

**(e) Refusal is discretionary, not mandatory.** Article V provides that enforcement "**may** be refused." Even where a ground is technically made out, the court retains a discretion to enforce — routinely exercised where the defect is trivial, cured, waived, or caused no prejudice. This is the principal answer to the 28-day notice point at §9.3.

### 3.4 The law of the seat, and PAC Rule 24.1

Two further instruments matter throughout.

The *lex arbitri* is the **International Commercial Arbitration Act, R.S.A. 2000, c. I-5 (Alberta)**, which gives force of law in Alberta to the UNCITRAL Model Law on International Commercial Arbitration. This is confirmed at Award ¶29 and ¶51 and at Procedural Order No. 1 ¶1. Model Law Article 16 governs the treatment of jurisdictional pleas; Article 34 governs annulment and its time limit.

The **PAC Rules (2018 Edition)** are incorporated into the parties' agreement by Section 14.2 of the Distribution Agreement. This has an important consequence for Article V(1)(d): where that provision asks whether the arbitral procedure accorded with "the agreement of the parties," the agreement here *includes the PAC Rules*. A breach of the Rules is therefore capable of engaging V(1)(d) — which is why the Rule 22.4 notice point at §9.3 has more substance than any of Pacífico's other procedural complaints. But the Rules cut both ways, and one provision in particular does an enormous amount of work for Greenleaf:

> **Rule 24.1 — Waiver.** "A party that knows or ought to know that any provision of these Rules, any direction of the Tribunal, or any requirement of the arbitration agreement has not been complied with, and yet proceeds with the arbitration without promptly stating its objection, shall be deemed to have waived its right to object to such non-compliance."

Rule 24.1 is a contractual waiver provision to which Pacífico agreed. Rule 24.2 preserves only (i) matters not waivable under the law of the seat or other mandatory law, and (ii) jurisdictional objections under Rule 18. **Every procedural complaint Pacífico now advances, other than jurisdiction, falls squarely within Rule 24.1** — and Pacífico raised none of them at the time.

## 4. THE CENTRAL FINDING: THE DISPOSITIF DISCREPANCY

Dra. Olvera asked me to verify the arithmetic of the damages section and to confirm whether the $14.7 million figure is supported. I have done so, step by step. **It is not.**

### 4.1 The Tribunal's calculation, reconstructed

Every figure below is taken from the Award itself. Each line reconciles exactly.

| Step | Award ¶ | Computation | Amount (USD) |
|---|---|---|---|

| Year 4 lost profits | 72 | $7,500,000 × 0.35 | 2,625,000 |

| **Net lost profits after mitigation** | **78** | | **7,218,750** |

The Tribunal then states, in terms, at **Award ¶86**:

> "The Tribunal therefore determines that the total amount due from Respondent to Claimant is **USD $9,530,861** (Nine Million Five Hundred Thirty Thousand Eight Hundred Sixty-One United States Dollars)."

The figure is written out in both numerals and words. The summary table at Section IX.F of the Award reproduces the same components and the same total.

**Four paragraphs later, the dispositif at Award ¶6 of Section XI orders:**

> "Respondent shall pay to Claimant the total sum of **USD $14,700,000** (Fourteen Million Seven Hundred Thousand United States Dollars)."

### 4.2 The magnitude and character of the discrepancy

**USD $14,700,000 − USD $9,530,861 = USD $5,169,139.**

The excess is **54.2% on top of** the reasoned total. It is not a rounding difference, an interest recalculation, or an uplift the Tribunal explained and I have overlooked. I have read the Award in full. The figure $14,700,000 appears exactly once — in operative paragraph 6 — and again in operative paragraph 7 by cross-reference ("the amount set forth in paragraph 6 above"). It appears nowhere in Section IX (Damages), nowhere in Section X (Post-Award Interest), and in no table.

Two observations about its likely origin, which are relevant to how the point is argued:

**(a) It is consistent with a transcription error.** $14,700,000 is exactly **ten times** $1,470,000 — the Year 3 lost-profits figure at Award ¶72. A decimal-place or line-transposition error in assembling the dispositif is the most economical explanation. This matters because it supports the characterisation of the defect as a **clerical slip in the operative part**, rather than a deliberate but unexplained award of additional damages. That characterisation is significantly more favourable to Greenleaf: a clerical slip in one line is severable; an unexplained substantive award may be said to infect the damages determination as a whole.

**(b) It is *below* the total relief claimed.** This is an important and non-obvious point that materially weakens Pacífico's *ultra petita* argument. Greenleaf's Statement of Claim of May 30, 2022 sought USD $12,600,000 in damages (per the bundle chronology, comprising lost profits, goodwill impairment and costs of cover), together with pre-award interest, arbitration costs, legal fees and expert fees (Award ¶9, ¶34). Adding the interest and costs the Tribunal in fact awarded, the global prayer was approximately **USD $14,912,111**. The dispositif figure of $14,700,000 therefore falls *within* the amount claimed. Pacífico consequently cannot say the Tribunal awarded something nobody asked for. **The defect is not excess over the claim; it is absence of reasons.** That distinction reorders the analysis: it moves the point away from Article V(1)(c), where it is comparatively weak, and towards Article V(1)(d) and Article V(2)(b), where it is strongest. §8.2 and §12.3 develop this.

### 4.3 Two subsidiary arithmetic errors

While verifying the calculation I found two further errors in the same section. Neither is material in amount, but both should be known before Pacífico finds them:

**(i) The pre-award interest figure is overstated by about $200.** The Award states (¶80) that interest is "$7,218,750 × 5% × 1.633 = USD $589,610.63." The correct product of those three numbers is **$589,410.94**. The Award therefore overstates the arithmetic result of its own stated formula by **$199.69**.

**(ii) The day count is wrong by one day.** The Award states that November 10, 2021 to June 28, 2023 is "596 days, or approximately 1.633 years." It is **595 days**, which on a 365-day basis is 1.6301 years, yielding interest of $588,377.57.

These are trivial sums and no court would refuse enforcement over $200. Their significance is entirely rhetorical: they establish that the damages section of this Award contains **three** distinct arithmetic defects, which lends credibility to Pacífico's narrative that the quantum determination was carelessly assembled. Greenleaf's answer is the correct one and should be given without embarrassment — the errors are *de minimis*, they are self-evidently clerical, and clerical error in an award is not an Article V ground. But Greenleaf should not be surprised by them in the courtroom.

### 4.4 The remedy that was available, and was lost

**PAC Rule 29** provided the exact remedy for this defect:

> **29.1** "Within thirty (30) days of receipt of the award, any party may ... request the Tribunal to: (a) correct any computational, clerical, or typographical error in the award ..."
>
> **29.2** "The Tribunal may, on its own initiative, correct any computational, clerical, or typographical error in the award within thirty (30) days of the date of the award."

The Award is dated June 28, 2023. The Tribunal's own-initiative window therefore closed on or about **July 28, 2023**, and the parties' window closed thirty days after receipt. **Neither party sought a correction.** The Tribunal is now *functus officio*, and Rule 29.3 does not reopen the period.

This is a significant and unwelcome fact, and it should be recorded plainly. The single cleanest solution to Greenleaf's only real enforcement problem — a two-line corrective addendum from the Tribunal reconciling ¶86 with the dispositif — was available for thirty days and was not taken. It is now unavailable. Any suggestion that Greenleaf should now write to the Centre or the Tribunal seeking a correction should be resisted: the request would be time-barred and futile, and it would create a contemporaneous written record, in Greenleaf's own hand, characterising the Award as defective. That document would be in Velázquez Ibarra's hands within the week.

The lapse does, however, cut in Greenleaf's favour in one respect. **Pacífico also did not seek correction.** Pacífico's counsel plainly knew of the discrepancy — its letter of September 20, 2023 identifies the $5,169,139 figure precisely. A party that identifies a computational error, declines to invoke the contractual mechanism for its correction, allows the annulment period to expire, and then raises the same error for the first time in a foreign enforcement court is exposed to a powerful waiver argument under Rule 24.1 and to the general principle that a party may not sleep on a procedural remedy and then rely on the defect it declined to cure.

## 5. THRESHOLD REQUIREMENTS: ARTICLE IV AND ARTICLE 1461

Before any Article V ground arises, Greenleaf must satisfy the formal prerequisites. These are not "defences" in the Article V sense, but they are a real route by which Velázquez Ibarra can defeat or — more likely — delay the application, and they are the only part of the case that is entirely within Greenleaf's own control. They should be treated as a checklist, not an argument.

**Article IV(1) requires the applicant to supply, at the time of the application:**

- **(a)** the duly authenticated original award or a duly certified copy thereof; and
- **(b)** the original arbitration agreement, or a duly certified copy thereof.

**Article IV(2)** requires a translation into the official language of the enforcing country, certified by an official or sworn translator or by a diplomatic or consular agent, where the award or agreement is not in that language. Article 1461 of the Código de Comercio implements these requirements.

**Practical points for the Guadalajara filing:**

1. **The award to be produced is the whole instrument, including Annex A.** PAC Rule 26.3 provides that "[a] dissenting opinion shall form part of the award document." Greenleaf must therefore produce the Dissenting Opinion of Lic. Beltrán Cisneros as part of the certified award. Producing the majority award alone would give Pacífico a clean objection that the instrument tendered is not the award. This is unwelcome — the dissent is unhelpful on quantum — but it is unavoidable, and it is better to tender it voluntarily and frame it (per §14) than to have Pacífico tender it and characterise the omission as concealment.

2. **Certified Spanish translation of the entire Award and of Section 14 of the Agreement.** The Award runs to 87 paragraphs plus the dispositif and a 12-page dissent; translation should be commissioned immediately, as it is likely to be the long pole in the filing timetable. Given the centrality of Award ¶86, the translation of that paragraph and of the Section IX.F summary table should be reviewed personally by Bufete Castañeda & Olvera before certification.

3. **Legalisation of the Canadian originals.** As at the date of this memorandum, Canada is **not yet** a party to the Hague Apostille Convention. Canada deposited its instrument of accession in 2023, but the Convention does not enter into force for Canada until **January 11, 2024**. Documents executed in Canada and tendered in Mexico before that date therefore require **consular legalisation** through the Mexican consulate with jurisdiction over Alberta or British Columbia, not an apostille. Given the intended filing in the first week of November 2023, this must be started now. If the filing were to slip past mid-January 2024, an apostille would suffice and the process would be materially faster — a factor worth weighing if the timetable comes under pressure for other reasons.

4. **The arbitration agreement.** Section 14.2 of the Distribution Agreement, produced with the whole Agreement (both parties signed on March 15, 2019; Margaret Tsai for Greenleaf, Rodrigo Espinoza Villanueva for Pacífico). Note Section 15.9: the Agreement was negotiated, drafted and executed in English and the English version prevails over any translation — a useful provision to have before the court when Pacífico complains about language.

5. **Corporate identity must be stated consistently.** The record is inconsistent on Greenleaf's incorporation: the Award (¶1) describes Greenleaf as incorporated under the **Canada Business Corporations Act, R.S.C. 1985, c. C-44**, while Procedural Order No. 1 describes it as "a corporation incorporated under the laws of the **Province of British Columbia**." The petition must state one, correctly, and must match the corporate documents tendered. See §16.

**Assessment: Article IV — a delay risk, not a defeat risk.** Fully curable and entirely within Greenleaf's control, but capable of costing weeks if the legalisation chain is started late.

## 6. ARTICLE V(1)(a) — INCAPACITY AND INVALIDITY OF THE ARBITRATION AGREEMENT

### 6.1 The standard

Article V(1)(a) permits refusal where the resisting party proves that:

> "The parties to the agreement referred to in article II were, under the law applicable to them, under some incapacity, or the said agreement is not valid under the law to which the parties have subjected it or, failing any indication thereon, under the law of the country where the award was made."

Two limbs. The first concerns capacity of the parties. The second concerns validity of the arbitration agreement, and it contains an express conflicts rule with a **two-stage cascade**: (i) the law chosen by the parties *to govern the arbitration agreement*; and, failing any such indication, (ii) **the law of the seat**.

### 6.2 First limb — incapacity: no arguable case

Both parties are corporations of substance. Greenleaf is a Canadian corporation with annual revenue of approximately CAD $95 million (Award ¶1). Pacífico is a *sociedad anónima de capital variable* with estimated annual revenue of approximately MXN $1.2 billion, three distribution warehouses and a refrigerated fleet (Award ¶2). The Agreement was signed by Greenleaf's Chief Executive Officer and by Pacífico's Director General, Sr. Rodrigo Espinoza Villanueva, who has held that office since Pacífico's incorporation in 2014 (Statement of Defence ¶1). No capacity or authority objection was raised at any point in the arbitration.

**Strength: negligible.** Address it in one sentence in the petition for completeness and move on.

### 6.3 Second limb — the applicable law question

This is the point Dra. Olvera correctly identifies as capable of exploitation. Section 14.1 of the Agreement designates British Columbia law as the governing law of the **Agreement**. Section 14.2, the arbitration clause, contains **no separate designation** of the law governing the arbitration agreement itself. The Tribunal expressly noted this gap (Award ¶29, ¶46).

Pacífico's argument, pleaded at Statement of Defence ¶25(d), is that in the absence of a designation the validity of the arbitration clause should be assessed under **Mexican law** as "the law with the closest and most real connection to the arbitration agreement."

**That argument is wrong on the face of the Convention, and Greenleaf should say so bluntly.** Article V(1)(a) does not apply a closest-connection test. It applies a two-stage cascade, and the fallback at stage two is not "the most closely connected law" but **"the law of the country where the award was made."** The award was made in Calgary, Alberta (Award ¶¶29, 51; Rule 27.3; Procedural Order No. 1 ¶1). The applicable law is therefore **Alberta law**. This is what the Tribunal held at Award ¶46, and it held it correctly, applying the Convention's own rule.

There is an additional layer of protection. Even if a Mexican court were persuaded to apply a closest-connection analysis, the connecting factors do not favour Mexico: the arbitration agreement is contained in an English-language contract governed by British Columbia law, providing for arbitration seated in Alberta under the rules of an Alberta institution, in English. The only Mexican connection is Pacífico's domicile — a factor that, if it sufficed, would allow every respondent to have the validity of every arbitration clause tested by its own home law, which is precisely the outcome Article V(1)(a)'s cascade is designed to prevent.

### 6.4 The unconscionability argument

Pacífico's substantive case (Statement of Defence ¶25; Jurisdictional Objection of September 2, 2022; Award ¶¶12, 42) is that Section 14.2 is unconscionable because it obliges a Mexican company to arbitrate in English in Canada. Four elements are pleaded: structural imbalance and take-it-or-leave-it drafting; language barrier; forum disadvantage (Calgary has no connection to the dispute); and the applicable-law point addressed above.

**Greenleaf's answers, in order of force:**

**(a) The Tribunal rejected the objection *unanimously* — including Pacífico's own appointee.** Award ¶45: "the Tribunal **unanimously** rejects Respondent's jurisdictional objection." Lic. Beltrán Cisneros confirms this in his Dissenting Opinion at ¶1: "I concur with the majority's decision on jurisdiction — specifically, that the Tribunal has jurisdiction over this dispute and that Respondent's jurisdictional objection is properly dismissed." **The Mexican arbitrator nominated by Pacífico, sitting in Mexico City, expressly concurred that the arbitration clause is valid and that the Tribunal had jurisdiction.** This is the single most powerful fact available to Greenleaf on this ground and should be quoted verbatim, in Spanish translation, in the petition. It will be very difficult for Velázquez Ibarra to persuade a Guadalajara judge that a clause is unconscionable under Mexican principles when the Mexican arbitrator his own client selected — and who dissented vigorously on everything else — found it valid.

**(b) Validity is governed by Alberta law, under which the clause is plainly valid.** Alberta's International Commercial Arbitration Act incorporates the UNCITRAL Model Law, under which an arbitration agreement between commercial parties is presumptively valid (Award ¶47).

**(c) Both parties are sophisticated and were legally represented.** Award ¶47. Pacífico's assertion that the clause was "drafted unilaterally by Greenleaf and presented ... on a take-it-or-leave-it basis" (Statement of Defence ¶25(a)) is a bare assertion; no evidence of it is recorded anywhere in the Award, and the Tribunal found the Agreement to have been freely negotiated at arm's length.

**(d) Pacífico's own evidence destroyed its own pleading on language.** Statement of Defence ¶25(b) pleads that Sr. Espinoza Villanueva "is a native Spanish speaker with **limited professional proficiency in English**." At the evidentiary hearing, **Sr. Espinoza Villanueva testified in English without an interpreter** (Award ¶21), and the Tribunal relied on his "proficiency in English" in rejecting the unconscionability argument (Award ¶47). Pacífico's principal witness disproved Pacífico's principal factual premise. Greenleaf should put this contradiction squarely before the Mexican court.

**(e) Two express contractual answers to the "forum disadvantage" point.** First, Section 15.9 records that the Agreement "has been negotiated, drafted, and executed in the English language" and that the English version prevails — Pacífico contracted in English with its eyes open. Second, Section 14.3 records that each party "irrevocably waives any and all right to trial by jury" in "any proceeding for the recognition or enforcement of an arbitral award rendered pursuant to Section 14.2" — Pacífico expressly contemplated and contracted around foreign enforcement proceedings. Neither clause is dispositive, but together they defeat the picture of an unsophisticated party ambushed by a foreign forum.

**(f) The Tribunal correctly characterised the mandatory-law limb as a merits point, not a jurisdictional one.** Award ¶48. Whether Mexican mandatory rules displace the parties' choice of British Columbia law is a question about the *substantive law applicable to the dispute*, not about whether the parties agreed to arbitrate. That reasoning is orthodox and should be defended.

### 6.5 The timeliness point — available, but do not overstate it

PAC Rule 18.2 provides: "A plea that the Tribunal does not have jurisdiction shall be raised **not later than in the Statement of Defence**." Procedural Order No. 1 ¶6 repeats the requirement. The Statement of Defence was filed **July 15, 2022**; the full Jurisdictional Objection was filed **September 2, 2022** — seven weeks later.

Greenleaf can and should note the delay. But it should not be pressed hard, because **the Statement of Defence did raise the plea in preliminary form**. Paragraph 5 expressly reserves "all of its rights to challenge the jurisdiction and competence of this Tribunal, including the validity and enforceability of the arbitration clause contained in Section 14.2," and Section III of the Defence is captioned "JURISDICTIONAL OBJECTION (PRELIMINARY STATEMENT, WITH FULL OBJECTION RESERVED)," setting out the unconscionability grounds at ¶¶23–26. Rule 18.3 in any event permits the Tribunal to admit a late plea, and the Tribunal implicitly did so by entertaining and deciding the objection. **Rule 24.2 also expressly carves jurisdictional objections out of the Rule 24.1 waiver.** An argument that the objection was waived would therefore be met with the Rules' own text and should be advanced, if at all, as a makeweight only.

### 6.6 Assessment — Article V(1)(a)

**Strength as Pacífico would present it: WEAK.**

The applicable-law argument fails on the plain words of Article V(1)(a). The unconscionability argument fails on Alberta law, on the parties' sophistication, on Pacífico's own witness's conduct, and — decisively — on the unanimous concurrence of Pacífico's own party-appointed Mexican arbitrator. The only residual risk is that a Mexican judge, invited to treat unconscionability as a matter of Mexican *orden público*, conflates this ground with Article V(2)(b). That risk is addressed at §12.

## 7. ARTICLE V(1)(b) — LACK OF PROPER NOTICE; INABILITY TO PRESENT ONE'S CASE

### 7.1 The standard

Article V(1)(b) permits refusal where:

> "The party against whom the award is invoked was not given proper notice of the appointment of the arbitrator or of the arbitration proceedings or was otherwise unable to present his case."

This is the Convention's due-process guarantee. Three features of it govern the analysis:

- **It is a fundamental-fairness standard, not a technical-compliance standard.** The question is not whether every procedural rule was observed — that is Article V(1)(d) — but whether the party was deprived of the opportunity to be heard. A defect that did not impair the ability to present the case does not engage V(1)(b).
- **Materiality and prejudice are required.** A party that received notice, attended, was represented, examined witnesses and made submissions has, by definition, presented its case.
- **Objections must be raised contemporaneously.** A party that perceives a due-process defect and proceeds without complaint cannot rely on it later. Here, that general principle is reinforced by the parties' own contractual waiver in PAC Rule 24.1.

### 7.2 Notice of the supplemental hearing of March 3, 2023

This is Pacífico's best-documented procedural complaint, and it is pleaded at paragraph (d) of the September 20, 2023 letter to the Court of King's Bench. It has two components.

**Component 1 — the notice period was two days short.** The Centre's case manager sent notice by email on **February 3, 2023** for a hearing on **March 3, 2023**. That is **28 days**. PAC Rule 22.4 provides:

> "Not less than **30 days'** advance written notice of any hearing shall be given to the parties by the Centre or the Tribunal. The parties may agree to a shorter notice period in writing, or the Tribunal may shorten the notice period in exceptional circumstances **with the consent of all parties**."

Rule 22.6 confirms that supplemental hearings "shall be subject to the notice requirements of Rule 22.4 unless the parties agree otherwise." Compliant notice would have had to issue on or before **February 1, 2023**. There is no record of any written agreement to shorten, or of any consent. **This is a genuine, documented breach of a mandatory rule**, and Greenleaf should not attempt to argue otherwise; the arithmetic is unanswerable and denying it would cost credibility on the points that matter.

The correct framing is that the breach is real but immaterial, and that it belongs under Article V(1)(d), not V(1)(b). It is addressed on its merits at **§9.3**.

**Component 2 — the party-copy went to a decommissioned address.** On **August 12, 2022**, Velázquez Ibarra Abogados wrote to the Centre's case manager stating that Pacífico's designated corporate email address should be updated from `admin@pacifico-dist.com.mx` to `legal@pacifico-dist.com.mx`, that the former "is being decommissioned," and requesting confirmation that the Centre's records had been updated. The February 3, 2023 notice was nonetheless copied to **`admin@pacifico-dist.com.mx`** — the decommissioned address (Bundle Document 4).

Greenleaf must concede that PAC Rule 4.2 was not observed: the Centre was obliged, on receipt of the August 12 letter, to update its records, and it did not. Note also that **Rule 4.4's safe harbour does not assist**: it protects communications sent to the last address on file where "[a] party's failure to update its contact information" is the cause. Here Pacífico *did* update. That door is closed and should not be pushed.

**But the point is nonetheless unanswerable on the facts, for four reasons:**

**(a) Proper notice was given to counsel of record, at the correct address.** The February 3, 2023 email was addressed **To:** `hvelazquez@velazquezibarra.com.mx` — Lic. Héctor Velázquez Ramos, lead counsel for Pacífico, at the precise address recorded in Procedural Order No. 1 ¶5. Rule 4.2 requires communications to be sent to the addresses provided by the parties "**or their authorized representatives**." Notice to counsel of record *is* proper notice under Article V(1)(b). The `admin@` copy was a redundant belt-and-braces courtesy copy to the client; its misdirection deprived Pacífico of nothing.

**(b) Pacífico's own August 12, 2022 letter confirms the point.** That letter states: "**All other contact information for Respondent and for this firm remains unchanged. Communications to Respondent's counsel should continue to be directed to hvelazquez@velazquezibarra.com.mx.**" Pacífico itself designated its counsel's address as the continuing channel. The notice went there.

**(c) Pacífico received the notice and acted on it.** Pacífico's counsel attended the March 3, 2023 hearing; Pacífico's damages expert, Econ. Ricardo Fuentes Medina, attended and gave further testimony responding to the Tribunal's questions on his projection methodology; and Pacífico cross-examined Dr. Thornberry (Award ¶26). A party that attends a hearing with counsel and its expert has, definitionally, received notice of it.

**(d) No objection was raised — then, or for over seven months afterwards.** Award ¶27: "**No procedural objections were raised by either party at the commencement of, or during, the supplemental hearing. Both parties participated fully in the proceedings.**" The bundle chronology for March 3, 2023 records the same. Pacífico then filed post-hearing briefs and reply briefs without complaint, and first raised the notice point on **September 20, 2023** — after the Award had gone against it. PAC Rule 24.1 deems that a waiver.

### 7.3 Language and interpretation

Dra. Olvera asks whether the use of interpreters can support a V(1)(b) argument. It cannot, and the record on this point is unusually favourable.

**The facts.** English was designated as the language of the arbitration by **Section 14.2 of the Agreement itself**. At the case management conference of April 18, 2022, Pacífico requested that Spanish be added as a second language. The Tribunal declined, "noting that the Agreement expressly designates English as the language of dispute resolution," but directed that "Spanish-English interpretation services will be arranged **at the expense of the Centre** for any witness who requires them ... so as to ensure that no party or witness is disadvantaged by the language requirement" (Procedural Order No. 1 ¶2). At the hearing, two of Pacífico's fact witnesses testified in Spanish through **Ms. Elena Rojas, a certified court interpreter registered with the Alberta Association of Court Interpreters** (Award ¶20). Counsel noted for the record that the witnesses were more comfortable in Spanish and that interpretation "might not capture all nuances," and the Tribunal acknowledged that observation on the record. **"No formal objection to the interpretation or to the use of English as the language of the proceedings was lodged by Respondent at any time during the hearing"** (Award ¶20). The February 3, 2023 notice again offered interpretation, inviting the parties to request it by February 17, 2023.

**Greenleaf's answers:**

1. **English was the parties' bargain.** A party cannot invoke the Convention's due-process guarantee to escape the language it contractually agreed to. Section 15.9 reinforces this: the Agreement was negotiated, drafted and executed in English.
2. **Pacífico got *more* than it asked for on cost.** Statement of Defence ¶30 requested that interpretation be provided "at the **Claimant's** cost." The Tribunal ordered it at the **Centre's** expense — better for Pacífico than the relief it sought. It is difficult to construct a denial of due process out of a ruling more generous than the request.
3. **The interpreter was independently certified**, and the Tribunal expressly confirmed her qualifications on the record (Award ¶20).
4. **A generalised concern that "nuance may be lost" is not an inability to present one's case.** No specific mistranslation is identified anywhere in the record; no correction was sought; no re-examination was requested; no objection was lodged.
5. **PAC Rule 16.3 was complied with in terms.** It requires the Tribunal to "make appropriate arrangements for interpretation at hearings where a witness ... does not speak the language of the arbitration." It did.
6. **Rule 24.1 waiver.** No contemporaneous objection at the hearing.

One caveat for candour: Pacífico *did* object to the language regime early, at the April 18, 2022 case management conference, and had that objection recorded and ruled upon. Greenleaf should therefore avoid the overstatement that Pacífico "never raised language" — the accurate and still-decisive statement is that Pacífico's request was heard, ruled upon in a reasoned procedural order, partially accommodated on terms better than Pacífico sought, and never renewed at or after the hearings.

### 7.4 The joinder of jurisdiction to the merits, framed as a V(1)(b) point

Pacífico's letter of September 20, 2023 complains that joinder "compelled Respondent to participate in a full merits proceeding ... before the threshold question of jurisdiction had been resolved." As a V(1)(b) argument this fails immediately: Pacífico was not prevented from presenting anything. Procedural Order No. 3 ¶1.3 **directed** Pacífico to include its jurisdictional arguments in its written submissions and gave it "the opportunity to present evidence and argument in support of its Jurisdictional Objection at the evidentiary hearing and in its post-hearing brief." Procedural Order No. 3 ¶2 records the Tribunal's express finding: "Respondent will have a full and fair opportunity to present its jurisdictional arguments ... **Joinder does not prejudice Respondent's rights.**" The complaint is about **cost**, not about opportunity — Pacífico's own recorded submission was that joinder "compel[led] Respondent to incur the full costs of merits proceedings." Incurring cost is not being unable to present one's case. The joinder point properly belongs under Article V(1)(d) and is addressed at §9.4.

### 7.5 The unreasoned $5,169,139 as a due-process argument

Pacífico may argue that it could not "present its case" against a quantum determination for which no reasoning exists — one cannot address reasoning one has never seen.

The argument has a certain rhetorical appeal but is misconceived as a V(1)(b) point. V(1)(b) is concerned with the opportunity to be heard **during the proceedings**, and Pacífico had a full opportunity on quantum: it pleaded a detailed quantum defence (Statement of Defence ¶¶37–40), retained and served a damages expert, cross-examined Dr. Thornberry at a three-day hearing, attended a **supplemental hearing convened specifically to address damages quantification** at which its expert testified further, and filed post-hearing and reply briefs. On any view Pacífico was heard on damages. A defect appearing for the first time in the award itself is a defect in the **award**, not in the **process**, and belongs under Article V(1)(d) (§9.5) and Article V(2)(b) (§12.3).

### 7.6 Assessment — Article V(1)(b)

**Strength as Pacífico would present it: WEAK.**

The notice defects are real but caused no prejudice and were waived. The language complaint is defeated by the parties' own contract, by the Tribunal's more-than-adequate accommodation, and by Pacífico's own witness testifying in English. The joinder complaint concerns expense, not opportunity. Greenleaf should be alert, however, to the risk that a Mexican court treats the *cumulative* effect of the notice shortfall, the misdirected email and the interpreter issue as a composite due-process grievance. The answer is the same in each case and should be presented as a single, unified point: **Pacífico appeared, was represented, examined and cross-examined witnesses, filed briefs, and objected to none of it at the time.**

## 8. ARTICLE V(1)(c) — MATTERS BEYOND THE SCOPE OF THE SUBMISSION

### 8.1 The standard, and the severability proviso

Article V(1)(c) permits refusal where:

> "The award deals with a difference not contemplated by or not falling within the terms of the submission to arbitration, or it contains decisions on matters beyond the scope of the submission to arbitration, **provided that, if the decisions on matters submitted to arbitration can be separated from those not so submitted, that part of the award which contains decisions on matters submitted to arbitration may be recognized and enforced**."

Three points of principle:

**(a) The ground is about *scope*, not *correctness*.** It asks whether the tribunal decided something it was not asked to decide. It does not ask whether the tribunal decided it well. A tribunal that misconstrues the contract, misapplies the governing law, or errs in its arithmetic has made an error *within* its mandate, not an excess of it. This distinction disposes of most of what Pacífico will want to say.

**(b) The proviso is Greenleaf's friend.** The second half of the article is an express severability mechanism, and it is exactly the tool the Guadalajara court would use if it accepted any part of Pacífico's complaint about the dispositif. This is the doctrinal foundation of the recommendation at §15.

**(c) *Infra petita* is not a V(1)(c) ground.** Article V(1)(c) addresses awards that go *beyond* the submission. An award that fails to decide something submitted is not within its terms. The remedy for omission was a supplementary award under PAC Rule 29.1(c), and that window has closed.

### 8.2 The $5,169,139 excess

**The argument Pacífico will make:** the Tribunal awarded $5,169,139 that its own reasoning does not support, and in doing so decided a matter beyond the scope of what was submitted to it.

**Why it is weaker under V(1)(c) than it first appears** — this is the analytical point Greenleaf should lead with:

1. **The dispositif figure is *within* the relief claimed.** As established at §4.2(b), Greenleaf's global prayer was approximately $14,912,111 ($12.6 million in damages plus interest, arbitration costs, legal fees and expert fees). The dispositif awards $14,700,000. The Tribunal therefore awarded **less than was asked of it**. It did not award a head of relief nobody claimed, or damages of a type outside the submission, or relief against a non-party. Every component of the claim — breach of the Distribution Agreement, lost profits, interest, costs, fees — falls squarely within Section 14.2's submission of "[a]ny dispute, controversy, or claim arising out of or in connection with this Agreement."

2. **A quantification error is an error *within* jurisdiction.** The Tribunal was unquestionably empowered to determine the quantum of Greenleaf's damages. Having been empowered to fix a number, it fixed one. That the number is unsupported by its reasoning is a defect of reasoning, not of mandate.

3. **The characterisation as a clerical slip cuts against V(1)(c).** If, as the arithmetic suggests (§4.2(a)), the figure results from a transcription error, then it is precisely the kind of defect PAC Rule 29.1(a) treats as "computational, clerical, or typographical" — categories that presuppose the tribunal acted within its powers.

**Why it nonetheless remains the live risk.** None of the above tells the court what to *do* with an operative order that contradicts its own reasons. A Guadalajara judge unpersuaded by the technical answer may reach for Article V(1)(c) as the most convenient vehicle to refuse the excess, using the severability proviso to enforce $9,530,861 and refuse the balance. **That outcome is, in substance, the outcome Greenleaf should be seeking anyway** — which is the core of the recommendation at §15. The danger is not severance; the danger is a court that declines to sever and refuses the whole.

**Strength: MODERATE**, and materially higher as against the $5,169,139 delta specifically than as against the reasoned $9,530,861.

### 8.3 Costs, legal fees and expert witness fees

Dra. Olvera asks whether the fee awards can be challenged as exceeding the submission. They cannot, but there is a citation problem to be aware of.

**The awards:** arbitration costs USD $485,000 (Award ¶82); legal fees USD $1,150,000 (Award ¶83); expert witness fees USD $87,500 (Award ¶84). Total USD $1,722,500.

**The authority relied upon by the Tribunal is miscited.** Award ¶83 states that the Tribunal "relies on **Rule 38.2** of the Prairie Arbitration Centre Commercial Arbitration Rules (2018 Edition), which provides that '[t]he Tribunal may allocate costs of the arbitration, including reasonable legal fees, among the parties, taking into account the circumstances of the case, including the outcome and the parties' conduct during the proceedings.'" **In the 2018 Rules, Rule 38 is "Exclusion of Liability" and has nothing to do with costs.** The quoted text is also not a verbatim reproduction of any provision.

**But the substantive authority plainly exists, in two places:**

> **Rule 34.1** — "The costs of the arbitration shall include: ... (d) such other costs as the Tribunal determines to have been reasonably incurred by the parties in connection with the arbitration, **including the reasonable fees and expenses of counsel, witnesses, and experts retained by the parties**."
>
> **Rule 35.2** — "**The Tribunal may allocate costs of the arbitration, including reasonable legal fees, among the parties.** In making such allocation, the Tribunal shall take into account the outcome of the case, the conduct of the parties during the proceedings, and any other circumstances it considers relevant."

Rule 34.1(d) captures counsel's fees *and* party-appointed experts' fees — covering both the $1,150,000 and the $87,500. Rule 35.2 authorises their allocation on precisely the criteria the Tribunal applied (outcome and conduct). The Rules are incorporated by Section 14.2 of the Agreement, so the parties conferred this authority by contract.

Note also that **Pacífico itself sought exactly the same relief**. Statement of Defence, Relief Requested ¶45(5) asks the Tribunal to "[o]rder Greenleaf to bear all costs of the arbitration, **including Pacífico's reasonable legal fees, expert costs, translation and interpretation expenses, and travel costs**." A party that asks a tribunal to award it legal and expert fees cannot afterwards say the tribunal lacked power to award them. This is a complete estoppel and should be pleaded as such.

Award ¶82 similarly cites "**PAC Rule 37.1**" for the equal advance on costs; the correct provision is **Rule 33.1** (Rule 37 is Confidentiality). Same character of error, same answer.

**Greenleaf's position:** concede the miscitation cheerfully and immediately — it is a typographical error in a rule number, not an absence of authority — and point to Rules 34.1(d) and 35.2 and to Pacífico's own prayer for relief. An enforcement court does not refuse to enforce an award because the tribunal wrote "38.2" where it meant "35.2."

**Strength: WEAK.**

### 8.4 The Section 10.1 exclusion of lost profits — a latent ground not yet raised

This requires careful handling. It is the one argument available to Pacífico that has not yet been made by anyone, is not addressed anywhere in the Award, and would be the most damaging if a court entertained it. Dra. Olvera asked for every conceivable ground, "however creative or attenuated," and this is the one that is neither.

**Section 10.1 of the Distribution Agreement provides:**

> "EXCEPT FOR BREACHES OF CONFIDENTIALITY OBLIGATIONS UNDER SECTION 13 AND INDEMNIFICATION OBLIGATIONS UNDER SECTION 10.2, NEITHER PARTY SHALL BE LIABLE TO THE OTHER PARTY FOR ANY INDIRECT, INCIDENTAL, SPECIAL, CONSEQUENTIAL, OR PUNITIVE DAMAGES, **INCLUDING WITHOUT LIMITATION LOSS OF PROFITS**, LOSS OF REVENUE, LOSS OF BUSINESS OPPORTUNITY, LOSS OF GOODWILL, OR COST OF PROCUREMENT OF SUBSTITUTE GOODS OR SERVICES, ARISING OUT OF OR IN CONNECTION WITH THIS AGREEMENT, REGARDLESS OF THE THEORY OF LIABILITY ..."

Section 10 survives termination by operation of Section 12.4(e). **The entirety of the Award's damages component is lost profits** — $8,085,000 gross, $7,218,750 net (Award ¶¶70–78). Neither of the two carve-outs applies: this is not a confidentiality breach and not a third-party indemnity claim. **The Award does not mention Section 10.1 anywhere.**

**Why this is nonetheless a weak ground, and why Greenleaf's position is strong:**

**(a) Pacífico never pleaded it.** I have reviewed the Statement of Defence excerpts closely. Pacífico's damages defence (¶¶37–40) attacks quantum on three bases — speculativeness of Years 4 and 5, failure to mitigate, and the 35% margin. **It never invokes Section 10.1.** Nor does the prayer for relief. A tribunal cannot be said to have exceeded its authority by declining to apply a contractual defence that the party entitled to it never raised.

**(b) Rule 24.1 waiver applies with full force.** This is a Rule 24.1 case *par excellence*: Pacífico knew or ought to have known of a clause in its own contract, proceeded through a 17-month arbitration, two hearings and two rounds of post-hearing briefs without stating the objection, and has not raised it even now — its September 20, 2023 set-aside letter does not mention it.

**(c) On its merits, the clause probably does not bar this claim.** The exclusion is of "INDIRECT, INCIDENTAL, SPECIAL, CONSEQUENTIAL, OR PUNITIVE DAMAGES, INCLUDING WITHOUT LIMITATION LOSS OF PROFITS." The better construction is that "loss of profits" is enumerated as a *species of the excluded categories* — that is, **indirect** lost profits are excluded, not the direct expectation loss on the very transactions the contract obliged Pacífico to make. Greenleaf's claim is for the margin it would have earned on purchases Pacífico was contractually bound to place under Section 4.2 and Schedule C. That is the paradigm of *direct* expectation damage. A construction that excluded it would render the Minimum Purchase Commitments — described in Section 4.2 as "firm obligations ... not subject to adjustment, reduction, or deferral" — legally unenforceable in damages, which cannot have been intended.

**(d) It is a merits question, and merits questions are not reviewable.** Even if the construction were wrong, the enforcement court is not permitted to reconsider it. *No hay revisión del fondo.*

**Recommended handling:** **do not raise this issue in the enforcement petition.** Do not pre-empt it, do not "address it head-on," do not mention Section 10.1 at all. Pacífico has not thought of it; there is no benefit in supplying it. Prepare a reply brief that can be deployed if Velázquez Ibarra's Mexico City co-counsel finds it on a fresh reading of the contract — the four answers above, in that order — but keep it in reserve. Greenleaf's counsel team should also be conscious of it when framing the damages narrative: the petition should describe the award as compensating Greenleaf for **the profit on purchases Pacífico was contractually obliged to make**, which is both accurate and pre-frames the damages as direct rather than consequential.

**Strength as Pacífico would present it: WEAK** — but this is the ground with the widest gap between its doctrinal strength (low) and its rhetorical force before a judge inclined to look for a reason (meaningful). It is the only unraised ground I would rate as worth active contingency planning.

### 8.5 *Infra petita*: the counterclaim heads not separately addressed

For completeness. Pacífico's counterclaim was pleaded as three distinct heads (Statement of Defence ¶41): loss of exclusivity rights (USD $1,500,000); **wasted investment (USD $1,200,000**, itemised as warehouse modifications $420,000, four refrigerated vehicles $310,000, Spanish-language marketing $275,000, and COFEPRIS fees $195,000); and reputational harm (USD $500,000).

The Award dismissed the counterclaim as one for "wrongful termination and loss of exclusivity rights" and reasoned that "Respondent adduced no evidence to support a separate, independent basis for its Counterclaim apart from its allegation of wrongful termination" (Award ¶67). That characterisation is imprecise as to the **wasted investment** head, which was pleaded as an itemised reliance claim.

Pacífico could argue the Tribunal failed to decide a submitted claim. The answer is short and complete: (i) *infra petita* is not an Article V ground — V(1)(c) addresses excess, not omission; (ii) the contractual remedy was a supplementary award under Rule 29.1(c), which Pacífico did not seek within thirty days and which is now time-barred; (iii) the Tribunal in any event disposed of the whole counterclaim in the dispositif at operative ¶5 and swept up the remainder at operative ¶8 ("All other claims and requests for relief not expressly granted herein are dismissed"); and (iv) the reliance claim was in substance dependent on wrongful termination, which the Tribunal rejected on the merits. Above all, a Mexican enforcement court has no power to revive a dismissed counterclaim; the only forum that could have was the Court of King's Bench of Alberta, and that door is now closed (§10).

**Strength: NEGLIGIBLE.**

### 8.6 Assessment — Article V(1)(c)

**Strength as Pacífico would present it: WEAK overall, save as to the $5,169,139 delta, where it is MODERATE.**

The costs and fees challenge fails on Rules 34.1(d) and 35.2 and on Pacífico's own prayer. The Section 10.1 point is unpleaded, waived and probably wrong. The *infra petita* point is not a Convention ground. The dispositif excess is the only element with real traction, and even there the strongest characterisation is not "excess of scope" but "absence of reasons," which lands under V(1)(d) and V(2)(b).

## 9. ARTICLE V(1)(d) — COMPOSITION OF THE TRIBUNAL; ARBITRAL PROCEDURE

### 9.1 The standard

Article V(1)(d) permits refusal where:

> "The composition of the arbitral authority or the arbitral procedure was not in accordance with the agreement of the parties, or, failing such agreement, was not in accordance with the law of the country where the arbitration took place."

Because Section 14.2 incorporates the PAC Rules, "the agreement of the parties" here **includes the Rules**. This is the ground on which Pacífico's procedural complaints properly belong, and it is where its only genuine, documented rule breach lives.

Two limiting principles are decisive:

- **Materiality.** The overwhelming weight of authority requires that the deviation be *material* — that it could have affected the outcome. Trivial or technical departures do not justify refusing enforcement of an award, and the discretionary "may" in Article V reinforces this.
- **Waiver.** A party that does not object contemporaneously cannot rely on the deviation later. Here that principle is not merely a general doctrine but an express contractual term to which Pacífico agreed: **PAC Rule 24.1**.

### 9.2 Composition of the Tribunal

No arguable case. Section 14.2 required three arbitrators; three were appointed. The mechanism followed Rules 6.2, 7.1 and 7.2: Greenleaf nominated Dr. Nadia Ferrante (February 15, 2022; accepted February 18); Pacífico nominated **Lic. Arturo Beltrán Cisneros** (March 1, 2022; accepted March 4); and the two co-arbitrators jointly selected Prof. Alistair Drummond as President (March 25, 2022; accepted March 28). The Tribunal was constituted on April 5, 2022 (Award ¶¶5–6; Rule 9.1). All three signed declarations of independence and impartiality. **"No challenges to any arbitrator were filed by either party at any point during these proceedings"** (Award ¶6). Procedural Order No. 1 records that at the case management conference of April 18, 2022 "neither party objects to the constitution of the Tribunal." The Award was signed by all three arbitrators (Rule 27.1 requires only a majority).

Pacífico nominated the arbitrator who dissented in its favour. It cannot complain about the composition it chose.

**Strength: NEGLIGIBLE.**

### 9.3 Rule 22.4 — the 28-day notice of the supplemental hearing

**This is the strongest procedural defence Pacífico has, and the only clean, documented breach of the agreed rules in the record.** It must be taken seriously and answered properly.

**The breach.** Notice issued February 3, 2023 for a March 3, 2023 hearing: **28 days** against Rule 22.4's mandatory minimum of 30. No written agreement to shorten and no consent of all parties, as Rule 22.4 would have required. Rule 22.6 expressly applies Rule 22.4 to supplemental hearings. Compliant notice would have had to issue by February 1, 2023.

**Greenleaf's answers, in ascending order of force:**

**(a) The deviation is *de minimis*.** Two days out of thirty — a shortfall of 6.7%. Article V(1)(d) is not a mechanism for enforcing timetables to the day.

**(b) There was no prejudice, and none has ever been articulated.** Pacífico's counsel attended. Pacífico's damages expert attended and testified. Pacífico cross-examined Greenleaf's expert. The Award records that "[b]oth parties were afforded a full and fair opportunity to examine and cross-examine the expert witnesses" (¶26). Pacífico has never identified a single thing it would have done differently with two more days. Its September 20, 2023 letter asserts inadequate notice but pleads no consequence.

**(c) Pacífico had far more than 28 days' effective notice of the subject matter.** The February 3 notice identified the three topics precisely — expert testimony on quantification, methodology for Years 4 and 5 lost profits, and mitigation — all of which had been fully litigated at the January 9–11 hearing and in the parties' briefs. Pacífico's expert was being recalled to elaborate on a report he had served on December 9, 2022. This was not a new case; it was a continuation.

**(d) Pacífico was given a distinct procedural opportunity and used it.** The notice required each party to identify witnesses by February 17, 2023 and to request interpretation by the same date. Pacífico engaged with that process and produced its expert.

**(e) Rule 24.1 waiver — decisive.** Pacífico received the notice on February 3 and could compute 28 days as easily as I can. It said nothing. It attended on March 3 and, per Award ¶27 and the bundle chronology, raised **no objection at the commencement of or during the hearing**. It filed post-hearing briefs and reply briefs. It first raised the point on **September 20, 2023** — seven and a half months later, and nearly three months after losing. Rule 24.1 deems that a waiver in terms, and Rule 24.2's carve-outs do not assist: this is neither a jurisdictional objection under Rule 18 nor a matter incapable of waiver under Alberta law.

**(f) The discretionary "may."** Even on a finding of breach, the court retains discretion to enforce. A two-day shortfall, waived at the time and raised only after an adverse result, is the archetype of a defect a court declines to act on.

**Strength as Pacífico would present it: WEAK, verging on MODERATE.** It is weak on the law and on prejudice, but it has the rhetorical advantage of being **objectively true and arithmetically simple** — a judge can verify it in ten seconds. Expect Velázquez Ibarra to lead with it precisely because it is the one point on which Greenleaf must concede the underlying fact. Greenleaf should therefore concede it immediately, in its own opening papers if tactically appropriate, and frame it as the best Pacífico has: a two-day timing slip on a hearing Pacífico attended, participated in fully, and never complained about until it lost.

### 9.4 The joinder of the jurisdictional objection to the merits

**Pacífico's complaint** (September 20, 2023 letter, ground (b); Procedural Order No. 3 ¶3): the Tribunal failed to rule on jurisdiction as a preliminary matter, contrary to the parties' agreement.

**The complete answer is PAC Rule 18.4, which authorises precisely what the Tribunal did:**

> "**The Tribunal may rule on a jurisdictional objection as a preliminary question or may join the objection to the merits of the dispute.** The Tribunal shall exercise its discretion in this regard having due regard to the nature of the objection, the efficiency of the proceedings, and the interests of the parties. The Tribunal's decision to join a jurisdictional objection to the merits shall not be construed as a waiver of the objection or an indication of the Tribunal's views on the merits of the objection. Where the Tribunal elects to join a jurisdictional objection to the merits, **it shall address jurisdiction as a threshold matter in the final award before proceeding to address the merits**."

Every element is satisfied:

1. **The discretion existed.** Rule 18.4 confers it in express terms. Rule 14.2(b) reinforces it, empowering the Tribunal to order "the bifurcation or consolidation of issues, including the separation of jurisdictional, liability, and quantum phases." Model Law Article 16(3), which applies as the *lex arbitri*, is to the same effect.
2. **The discretion was exercised on the stated criteria, with reasons.** Procedural Order No. 3 ¶2 addresses the nature of the objection, the overlap with the merits, the risk of delay, and procedural economy — the exact factors Rule 18.4 prescribes.
3. **Both parties were heard first.** Pacífico's submission of September 2, Greenleaf's response of September 12, and a dedicated case management conference by videoconference on September 16, 2022 at which both counsel were heard on bifurcation. Procedural Order No. 1 ¶6 had promised exactly this consultation, and it was delivered.
4. **The Rule 18.4 sequencing requirement was honoured.** The Award decides jurisdiction at **Section VI**, before turning to applicable law at Section VII and the merits at Section VIII. Operative paragraphs 1 and 2 of the dispositif deal with jurisdiction first. This is not a technicality — it is the specific safeguard Rule 18.4 imposes on joinder, and the Tribunal complied with it exactly.
5. **The objection was preserved, not lost.** Rule 18.4 provides that joinder "shall not be construed as a waiver of the objection," and Procedural Order No. 3 ¶3 expressly records Pacífico's objection and reservation of rights.
6. **Pacífico's own appointee concurred in the jurisdictional outcome.** Dissenting Opinion ¶1.

**One matter to be prepared for.** Both Procedural Order No. 1 ¶6 and Procedural Order No. 3 ¶2 cite the source of this discretion as "**Rule 15.3**," and Procedural Order No. 3 purports to **quote** it: "[t]he Tribunal may rule on jurisdictional objections as a preliminary question or may defer its ruling to the Final Award." **There is no Rule 15.3 in the 2018 PAC Rules.** Rule 15 is "Seat of Arbitration" and contains only 15.1 and 15.2. The correct provision is Rule 18.4, whose wording differs from the quotation.

Velázquez Ibarra will find this. Greenleaf's answer must be prepared and must be candid: the Tribunal misnumbered the rule, but the power it exercised exists, is conferred by Rule 18.4 in materially identical terms, and was exercised in accordance with Rule 18.4's substantive requirements — including its sequencing safeguard. A misnumbered cross-reference in a procedural order is not a departure from the agreed procedure. **Do not attempt to defend "Rule 15.3" as though it existed**; concede the slip and pivot immediately to Rule 18.4.

**Strength: WEAK.**

### 9.5 Rule 27.2(e) — failure to state reasons for the operative amount

**This is where the dispositif discrepancy has its greatest doctrinal force as an Article V(1)(d) argument**, and Greenleaf should expect Velázquez Ibarra's Mexico City co-counsel to reframe it this way rather than leaving it under V(1)(c).

**PAC Rule 27.2 provides that the award "shall state":**

> "(e) **the reasons upon which the award is based**, unless the parties have agreed in writing that no reasons are to be given;
> (f) the operative part (dispositif) of the award, **including the precise amounts awarded (if any)**, any interest awarded, any orders made, and any determination regarding the allocation of costs."

The parties did not agree to dispense with reasons. Rule 27.2(e) therefore applied, and the requirement to give reasons is part of the agreed procedure. **As to $5,169,139 of the operative amount, no reasons exist.** The Award states a reasoned total of $9,530,861 at ¶86 and then orders a different, larger figure at operative ¶6.

The argument is therefore not that the Tribunal reasoned badly — which would be unreviewable — but that as to a discrete and precisely quantifiable portion of the award **it did not reason at all**, in breach of a procedural requirement the parties contractually adopted. That is a genuine Article V(1)(d) argument and it is materially stronger than anything else Pacífico has.

**Greenleaf's answers:**

1. **The Award abundantly satisfies Rule 27.2(e) taken as a whole.** It contains 87 paragraphs of detailed reasoning across jurisdiction, applicable law, liability, force majeure, regulatory obligations, termination, the counterclaim, damages methodology, mitigation, interest and costs, together with a 12-page dissent. This is not an unreasoned award. The defect is confined to a single line of the operative part.
2. **The defect is severable and precisely quantified — by the Tribunal itself.** Rule 27.2(f) required "the precise amounts awarded," and the Tribunal supplied them: at ¶86, in numerals and words, and in the Section IX.F summary table. The reasoned award is not a matter of the court's own reconstruction; it is the Tribunal's own stated determination. **A court that enforces $9,530,861 is enforcing the Tribunal's reasons exactly, not substituting its own.** This is the pivotal point for the severance argument at §15.
3. **Materiality is confined to the delta.** Whatever the position as to $5,169,139, the reasoning supporting the $9,530,861 is complete, transparent and arithmetically verifiable at every step.
4. **Rule 29 was the contractual remedy and Pacífico did not use it.** Pacífico identified the error, quantified it to the dollar in its September 20, 2023 letter, and never requested a correction within the thirty days Rule 29.1 allowed.

**Strength as Pacífico would present it: MODERATE**, as against the $5,169,139. **Weak** as against the reasoned $9,530,861.

### 9.7 Rule 25.3 and the terms of the contract

## 10. ARTICLE V(1)(e) — AWARD NOT BINDING, SET ASIDE OR SUSPENDED; AND ARTICLE VI

### 10.1 The standard

Article V(1)(e) permits refusal where:

> "The award has not yet become binding on the parties, or has been set aside or suspended by a competent authority of the country in which, or under the law of which, that award was made."

Three distinct limbs: not yet binding; set aside; suspended. **None is available to Pacífico**, and this ground — which Pacífico plainly intended to be a centrepiece — has expired.

### 10.2 The award is binding

**PAC Rule 30.1:** "The award shall be **final and binding on the parties from the date it is rendered**. The parties agree that the award shall constitute a binding resolution of the dispute and **may be enforced in any court of competent jurisdiction**."

**PAC Rule 30.2:** the parties "undertake to carry out any award without delay and are deemed to have waived their right to any form of appeal or recourse to a court of law, to the extent that such waiver may validly be made under applicable law."

Section 14.2 of the Agreement itself provides that disputes "shall be **finally settled** by arbitration." The Award is dated June 28, 2023 and was rendered on that date. Under the parties' own agreement it became binding immediately. No appeal on the merits lies against it. This limb is closed by contract.

### 10.3 The award has not been set aside — and can no longer be

**The critical factual finding Dra. Olvera asked me to confirm: Pacífico never filed a set-aside application.**

What exists is a **letter**, dated **September 20, 2023**, from Velázquez Ibarra Abogados to the Registrar of the Court of King's Bench of Alberta. Its own language is dispositive of its character. It writes "to advise the Honourable Court of our client's **intent to file** an application to set aside"; it states that Pacífico "**intends to seek**" the setting aside on four grounds; it says "[w]e are **in the process of preparing** the formal application materials, including supporting affidavit evidence and a memorandum of argument, and **expect to file** same"; and it requests only "that this letter be placed on the Court's file **as notice of the forthcoming application**."

**A letter of intent addressed to a registrar is not an originating application.** It commences no proceeding, it is not served as an originating process, it is unsupported by affidavit, and it seeks no relief. The bundle chronology confirms: "**No formal application is filed with the Court on this date.**"

**The time limit has now expired.** The Alberta International Commercial Arbitration Act gives force of law to the UNCITRAL Model Law, Article 34(3) of which provides that an application for setting aside "may not be made after **three months** have elapsed from the date on which the party making that application had received the award." Three months from June 28, 2023 is **September 28, 2023**. Pacífico's letter of September 20 fell inside the window — with eight days to spare — and Pacífico did not use them.

**One correction to the record, which matters.** The bundle chronology states that the deadline was "**90 days**" and expired on **September 26, 2023**, "calculated as June 28, 2023 + 90 days." That is wrong twice over: Model Law Article 34(3) prescribes **three months**, not ninety days, and it runs from the date the party **received** the award, not from the date the award was made. The correct outside date is **September 28, 2023 at the earliest**, and later if Pacífico received the Award after June 28. Greenleaf must not file the bundle's calculation in Guadalajara: if Velázquez Ibarra demonstrates that Greenleaf's own chronology misstated the limitation period, it damages the credibility of the whole bundle at exactly the moment Greenleaf needs the court to accept its factual account. See §16.

**Action item.** Obtain a **certificate or search result from the Court of King's Bench of Alberta confirming that no application to set aside the Award in PAC-2022-0041 has been filed**, together with a short affidavit of Alberta counsel deposing to the three-month limit under the ICAA and Model Law Article 34(3) and to its expiry. This converts Greenleaf's best point on this ground from submission into evidence, and it should be tendered with the petition rather than held back.

### 10.4 The award has not been suspended

"Suspended" in Article V(1)(e) means suspended by order of a competent authority at the seat, not merely challenged. No Alberta court has made any order concerning this Award. No application is pending capable of producing one.

### 10.5 Article VI — the stay application Pacífico will nonetheless attempt

Article VI provides:

> "If an application for the setting aside or suspension of the award has been made to a competent authority referred to in article V(1)(e), the authority before which the award is sought to be relied upon may, if it considers it proper, adjourn the decision on the enforcement of the award and may also, on the application of the party claiming enforcement, order the other party to give suitable security."

Expect Velázquez Ibarra to seek an adjournment of the Guadalajara proceedings pending a set-aside application in Calgary. **The answer is that Article VI's condition precedent is unsatisfied: no application "has been made."** A letter announcing an intention to make one is not the making of one, and the period in which one could have been made has expired. Article VI is simply unavailable.

If, contrary to expectation, Pacífico files a late application in Alberta and seeks to reopen the limitation period, Greenleaf should: (i) resist an adjournment on the footing that the application is prima facie time-barred and therefore not a bona fide basis for delay; (ii) in the alternative, invoke Article VI's second limb and **apply for security in the full amount** as the price of any adjournment; and (iii) instruct Alberta counsel to oppose any extension application. Model Law Article 34(3) is a hard limit; it is not a limitation period subject to the general discretion to extend.

### 10.6 Assessment — Article V(1)(e)

**Strength as Pacífico would present it: NEGLIGIBLE.**

The Award is binding by contract (Rule 30.1) and from the date rendered. It has not been set aside, has not been suspended, and can no longer be either. This is one of the strongest features of Greenleaf's position and should be pleaded affirmatively and early — supported, ideally, by the Alberta court search — rather than left as a response to a defence.

## 11. ARTICLE V(2)(a) — NON-ARBITRABILITY

### 11.1 The standard, and why this ground must be addressed

Article V(2) provides:

> "Recognition and enforcement of an arbitral award may also be refused if the competent authority in the country where recognition and enforcement is sought finds that: **(a) The subject matter of the difference is not capable of settlement by arbitration under the law of that country** ..."

**Dra. Olvera's list of grounds omitted Article V(2)(a).** It should not be omitted from the analysis, for two reasons. First, unlike the Article V(1) grounds, Article V(2)(a) may be raised by the court **of its own motion** — Pacífico need not plead it for the judge to consider it. Second, Pacífico has already argued the substance of it: its case that "the dispute is subject to the **exclusive jurisdiction of the courts of Mexico** by operation of Mexican mandatory commercial law" (Procedural Order No. 3, recitals; Award ¶43; Statement of Defence ¶¶18–22) is, translated into Convention language, precisely a non-arbitrability argument. Expect Pacífico's new Mexico City counsel to relabel it as such, because it is materially better framed under V(2)(a) — where the court applies **Mexican** law and may act *sua sponte* — than under V(1)(a), where Alberta law governs.

### 11.2 Analysis

The argument nonetheless fails.

1. **Commercial distribution disputes are arbitrable in Mexico.** The Código de Comercio's arbitration provisions (Book Five, Title Four) apply generally to commercial matters, and Mexico's non-reservation accession to the Convention reflects a broad conception of arbitrability. The categories of non-arbitrable subject matter in Mexican law are narrow and do not extend to disputes over an exclusive distribution contract between two trading companies. *Confirmation of the precise scope of non-arbitrable matters under Mexican law, with authority, is required from Bufete Castañeda & Olvera.*

2. **The presence of mandatory regulatory rules does not render a dispute non-arbitrable.** This is the central conceptual error in Pacífico's position. It is now the settled international position — and, I understand, the Mexican position — that the applicability of mandatory rules to a relationship does not remove disputes about that relationship from arbitration; it merely means the tribunal must consider whether to apply them. The existence of COFEPRIS regulation and NOM standards governing the *sale of nutritional products in Mexico* says nothing about whether a *contractual dispute about purchase commitments* between the manufacturer and its distributor is arbitrable.

3. **The dispute the Tribunal actually decided was purely contractual.** The Tribunal decided whether Pacífico met the Minimum Purchase Commitments in Schedule C, whether Section 11.1's force majeure notice condition was satisfied, whether Section 5.3 allocated regulatory responsibility to Pacífico, whether the Section 12.2 termination was valid, and what damages followed. No consumer's rights were adjudicated; no NOM standard was applied or disapplied; no COFEPRIS decision was reviewed. **The Ley Federal de Protección al Consumidor protects consumers**, and Pacífico is not a consumer — it is a distributor with approximately MXN $1.2 billion in annual revenue.

4. **Pacífico submitted to the arbitration.** It nominated an arbitrator, filed a Statement of Defence, **advanced a counterclaim for USD $3,200,000**, participated in document production, called witnesses at two hearings, filed post-hearing and reply briefs, and asked the Tribunal to award it costs. A party that asks a tribunal for USD $3.2 million cannot coherently maintain that the subject matter was incapable of arbitral settlement.

5. **Pacífico's own Mexican arbitrator disagreed.** Lic. Beltrán Cisneros concurred that the Tribunal had jurisdiction (Dissenting Opinion ¶1).

### 11.3 Assessment — Article V(2)(a)

**Strength as Pacífico would present it: WEAK.**

But because the court may raise it *sua sponte*, and because it shares a factual substratum with the public policy ground, Greenleaf should **address it affirmatively in the petition rather than waiting for it to be pleaded**. A short, confident passage establishing that commercial distribution disputes are arbitrable in Mexico, and that the presence of regulatory rules does not alter that, will inoculate the court against the framing Pacífico will attempt under Article V(2)(b).

## 12. ARTICLE V(2)(b) — PUBLIC POLICY OF MEXICO

### 12.1 The standard

Article V(2)(b) permits refusal where:

> "The recognition or enforcement of the award would be contrary to the public policy of that country."

Implemented in Mexico by Article 1462(II) of the Código de Comercio. Four principles frame the analysis:

**(a) It is the *international* public policy of the enforcing state that governs, not its domestic public policy.** Dra. Olvera states this correctly. The distinction between *orden público interno* and *orden público internacional* is fundamental: were the domestic standard applied, every mandatory rule of the forum would become a ground for refusal and the Convention would fail of its purpose. What is protected is the forum's most basic notions of morality and justice.

**(b) The threshold is exceptionally high.** The internationally accepted formulation — associated with *Parsons & Whittemore Overseas Co. v. Société Générale de l'Industrie du Papier (RAKTA)* (2d Cir. 1974) and adopted in substance across Convention jurisdictions — is that enforcement may be refused only where it "would violate the forum state's most basic notions of morality and justice."

**(c) The objection is to enforcing *the award*, not to *the tribunal's reasoning*.** The question is whether giving effect to the award's operative outcome would offend Mexican international public policy, not whether the Tribunal's route to that outcome was one a Mexican court would have taken.

**(d) The ground is not a gateway to merits review.** This must be stated repeatedly and forcefully in the petition, because every merits argument Pacífico has will be re-dressed as a public policy point.

### 12.2 The application of British Columbia law rather than Mexican mandatory law

**Pacífico's argument** (Statement of Defence ¶¶18–22; Award ¶43; September 20, 2023 letter, ground (a)): the Tribunal applied British Columbia law and declined to apply Mexican *leyes de orden público* — the Ley Federal de Protección al Consumidor, NOM standards, and mandatory distributor protections. Enforcing an award that ignored Mexican mandatory law offends Mexican public policy.

Dra. Olvera rightly identifies this as the argument with the most traction before a Guadalajara bench. It is nonetheless answerable, and comprehensively.

**Greenleaf's answers:**

**(a) Party autonomy in the choice of governing law is itself a principle of Mexican and international public policy.** Section 14.1 designates British Columbia law. The Tribunal gave effect to that choice (Award ¶¶50, 52), as PAC Rule 25.1 required: "The Tribunal shall decide the substance of the dispute **in accordance with the rules of law chosen by the parties**." Refusing enforcement because a tribunal honoured the parties' own choice of law would invert the Convention. Mexico's own conflicts framework recognises party autonomy in international commercial contracts.

**(b) The conflation of domestic and international public policy must be confronted directly.** The rules Pacífico invokes are domestic Mexican regulatory rules. That a Mexican court applying Mexican law would have decided differently is not the test. **The test is whether enforcing an award requiring one commercial party to pay another for breach of freely negotiated minimum purchase commitments offends Mexico's most fundamental notions of justice.** Framed that way — and it must be framed that way, expressly, in the petition — the answer is plainly no. Greenleaf should ask the court to state the correct standard before applying it, and should supply the Mexican authority for the *interno/internacional* distinction. **Bufete Castañeda & Olvera should provide the strongest available Mexican appellate authority on this distinction; it is the single most valuable Mexican-law input to the petition.**

**(c) The mandatory rules invoked have nothing to do with this dispute's outcome.** The Ley Federal de Protección al Consumidor protects consumers; Pacífico is not one. NOM standards and COFEPRIS registration govern product labelling and market authorisation; no product was sold in Mexico in breach of them by reason of this Award, and enforcing a money judgment against Pacífico will not cause any unregistered product to enter the Mexican market. The Award compels one thing only: **payment of money by one company to another.** There is no Mexican public policy against that.

**(d) The "mandatory distributor protections" are asserted, not established.** Statement of Defence ¶20 asserts rights to manufacturer support for regulatory compliance, to reasonable adjustment of performance obligations, and against bad-faith termination, characterising them as mandatory. No Mexican statutory provision creating such rights is identified. Mexican law, as I understand it, does not have a distributor-protection statute analogous to those found in some Latin American, Middle Eastern and European jurisdictions. **This should be confirmed by Bufete Castañeda & Olvera and, if confirmed, stated bluntly in the petition** — the absence of any such statute would be a complete answer.

**(e) The Tribunal did consider the argument and gave reasons.** Award ¶52: the Tribunal "has carefully considered Respondent's arguments regarding the applicability of Mexican mandatory distribution law" and declined to apply them because "party autonomy in the selection of governing law is a fundamental principle" and no extraordinary circumstances were established. The argument was heard and reasoned, not ignored.

**(f) Pacífico invoked British Columbia law itself when it suited.** Statement of Defence ¶43 pleads the legal basis for the **counterclaim** as "British Columbia law — **the governing law of the Agreement**." Pacífico cannot claim BC law for its own claim and denounce it as contrary to Mexican public policy for Greenleaf's.

**Strength: WEAK on doctrine; MODERATE in practice** before a bench with the track record Dra. Olvera describes. Doctrinally this argument should fail. The risk is entirely one of judicial reception, and it is best managed by putting the correct legal standard in front of the judge early, with Mexican authority, before Pacífico can frame the question as "should Mexican law have applied in Mexico?"

### 12.3 The dispositif discrepancy as a failure of *motivación* — **THE PRINCIPAL RISK**

This is the ground on which Greenleaf could actually lose, and it should be treated as such.

**The argument Pacífico will make.** Articles 14 and 16 of the Mexican Constitution require that acts affecting a person's rights be founded and reasoned — *fundamentación y motivación*. A judicial or arbitral determination whose operative order contradicts the reasons given to support it is the paradigm of an unmotivated decision. Enforcing an order to pay USD $14,700,000 when the instrument's own reasoning states that the sum due is USD $9,530,861 — and identifies no basis whatever for the additional USD $5,169,139 — would require a Mexican court to lend the coercive power of the Mexican state to an obligation for which no reason exists. That, Pacífico will say, offends *orden público* at the most basic level: not because the Tribunal decided wrongly, but because as to $5.17 million it did not decide anything at all.

**Why this argument is dangerous in a way none of the others is:**

1. **It requires no merits review.** Greenleaf's most powerful and most repeatable answer — *no hay revisión del fondo* — does not reach it. The court need not evaluate the gross margin, the Years 4 and 5 projection, or the force majeure ruling. It need only read ¶86 and then read operative ¶6. **The defect is visible on the face of the instrument, in two numbers, and requires no expertise to grasp.**
2. **It is arithmetically incontestable.** Greenleaf cannot deny it. I have verified it; the Tribunal's own summary table confirms it.
3. **It engages a constitutional principle that Mexican judges apply daily.** *Fundamentación y motivación* is not an exotic doctrine imported for the occasion; it is the everyday grammar of Mexican public law.
4. **It sits precisely in the fault line Dra. Olvera identified.** A bench that has refused enforcement on *orden público* grounds two or three times in a decade, and that has on at least one occasion blurred the *interno/internacional* line, is a bench that could be receptive.
5. **Nothing can cure it.** The Rule 29 window has closed and the Tribunal is *functus officio* (§4.4). Greenleaf cannot go back and fix this.

**Greenleaf's answers, if it insists on the full $14,700,000:**

- The Award as a whole is extensively reasoned; the defect is confined to one line of the operative part.
- The figure is *within* the relief claimed, so nothing was awarded that was not sought (§4.2(b)).
- Article V(1)(c)'s severability proviso permits enforcement of the reasoned portion.
- Pacífico identified the error, declined the Rule 29 remedy, and allowed the annulment period to lapse.
- The public policy ground protects against outcomes that offend fundamental justice, not against arithmetic slips.

**These answers are respectable. They are not, in my assessment, reliable enough to stake the whole enforcement on.** Each of them explains why the court should not refuse enforcement *entirely* — but none explains why the court should enforce the *unreasoned $5,169,139*. Greenleaf has, in truth, no good answer to the question "on what reasoning is my client obliged to pay this $5.17 million?" — because the Tribunal supplied none.

**Strength as Pacífico would present it: MODERATE to STRONG as against the $5,169,139 delta; WEAK as against the reasoned $9,530,861.**

**This asymmetry is the entire basis of the recommendation at §15.** If Greenleaf claims $9,530,861, this ground evaporates — there is nothing unreasoned left to attack, and the sum claimed is the sum the Tribunal itself declared due at ¶86. If Greenleaf claims $14,700,000, this ground becomes the centre of the case, and it is the one case Greenleaf can lose.

### 12.4 Excessive or penal damages

Pacífico may argue that damages of $14.7 million on a $30 million five-year contract, where actual purchases in the final measured year were $1.8 million, are so disproportionate as to be penal, and that Mexican public policy resists punitive awards.

**Answers:** (i) The Award is expressly compensatory. Award ¶69 states the British Columbia expectation measure — placing the non-breaching party in the position it would have occupied had the contract been performed — and nothing punitive appears anywhere. (ii) The Tribunal in fact reduced the award, applying a 15% mitigation discount (¶78) and, in doing so, criticising Greenleaf's own failure to appoint a replacement distributor. A tribunal that discounts a claimant's recovery for its own failure to mitigate is not acting punitively. (iii) The damages are grounded in the Minimum Purchase Commitments in Schedule C, which Section 4.2 describes as "**firm obligations** of Pacífico ... not subject to adjustment, reduction, or deferral." (iv) The reasoned figure of $9,530,861 is well within the total claimed and includes $1,722,500 of costs and fees. (v) **Note, however, that this argument gains real force if applied to the unreasoned $14.7 million**, precisely because no compensatory rationale for the excess exists. It is another reason to enforce the reasoned figure.

**Strength: WEAK against $9,530,861; MODERATE against $14,700,000.**

### 12.5 The fee award as a public policy matter

Pacífico may argue that an award of USD $1,150,000 in legal fees offends Mexican public policy, Mexican civil procedure being generally less generous in fee-shifting than common-law practice. This fails: (i) fee-shifting was authorised by the PAC Rules the parties adopted (Rules 34.1(d), 35.2) and is standard in international commercial arbitration; (ii) the Tribunal gave five reasons for finding the amount reasonable (Award ¶83); (iii) **Pacífico sought the identical relief for itself** (Statement of Defence ¶45(5)); and (iv) a difference between Mexican domestic costs practice and international arbitral practice is a difference of domestic policy, not a violation of international public policy.

**Strength: WEAK.**

### 12.6 The composite "cumulative unfairness" argument

Expect Velázquez Ibarra to gather every point — the 28-day notice, the misdirected email, the interpreters, the joinder, the unreasoned $5.17 million, the miscited rules, the dissent — and present them as a cumulative picture of an unfair proceeding that Mexican public policy should not endorse.

**Greenleaf's answer must be structural, not merely itemised.** Take each component and show that it was either (i) expressly agreed by Pacífico (English language, Calgary seat, PAC Rules, three arbitrators), (ii) expressly authorised by the Rules Pacífico adopted (joinder under Rule 18.4; fee-shifting under Rules 34.1(d) and 35.2), (iii) waived by Pacífico's own conduct under Rule 24.1 (the notice shortfall, the interpretation), or (iv) a clerical slip with no effect on any party's rights (the rule miscitations, the $200 interest error). Then state the single fact that answers all of it together: **Pacífico was represented by experienced counsel of its own choosing throughout a 17-month arbitration, nominated one of the three arbitrators, produced 890 documents, called three fact witnesses and an expert, participated in a three-day hearing and a supplemental hearing, filed post-hearing and reply briefs, and objected to none of what it now complains of until after it lost.**

### 12.7 Assessment — Article V(2)(b)

**Strength as Pacífico would present it: overall MODERATE — and it is the only ground capable of defeating enforcement.**

Broken down:
- Mexican mandatory law / choice of law: **weak doctrinally, moderate in practice.**
- **The dispositif discrepancy as a failure of *motivación*: moderate to strong as against the $5,169,139 delta.** This is the risk.
- Excessive damages: weak, unless attached to the unreasoned figure.
- Fee award: weak.

## 14. THE DISSENTING OPINION AS A LITIGATION WEAPON

Dra. Olvera asks whether Pacífico's counsel might weaponise the dissent, and whether it provides a roadmap for the opposition. The short answer: **yes on quantum, but it is a net asset for Greenleaf on jurisdiction, and it is largely defused by the recommendation at §15.**

### 14.1 The legal answer is complete

**PAC Rule 26.3:**

> "Any arbitrator may attach a separate or dissenting opinion to the award. A dissenting opinion shall form part of the award document but **shall not affect the validity or enforceability of the majority decision**. The existence of a dissenting opinion shall be noted in the award."

This is not merely a background principle of arbitration law; it is a **term of the parties' own agreement**, incorporated by Section 14.2. Pacífico contracted for a rule providing that a dissent does not affect enforceability, and cannot now argue the contrary. Rule 26.1 provides that decisions are made by majority. This should be quoted in the petition, in Spanish translation, at the first mention of the dissent.

### 14.2 What the dissent gives Pacífico

Three things, all on quantum:

1. **An arbitrator-authored finding that the Years 4 and 5 damages are speculative.** Dissent ¶13: "these damages are speculative and fail to meet the standard of reasonable certainty required under British Columbia contract law." Dissent ¶15: "There is no reasonable evidentiary basis to assume that Respondent would have met 100% of the substantially higher Year 4 ... and Year 5 ... commitments when it was struggling to reach even 30% to 47% of its Years 2 and 3 commitments."
2. **A specific alternative figure.** Dissent ¶¶19, 21: "I would have awarded damages in the range of approximately **USD $4,500,000 to USD $5,000,000**, before interest and costs."
3. **A direct attack on the mitigation discount.** Dissent ¶18: "The majority's 15% mitigation discount ... is wholly inadequate ... A 15% discount on speculative full-commitment damages does not transform those damages from speculative to certain."

Expect Velázquez Ibarra to place these passages before the Guadalajara judge with the framing: *even the Tribunal's own Mexican member — a distinguished arbitrator practising in Mexico City — concluded that this award is approximately three times too large.*

### 14.3 What the dissent gives Greenleaf — which is more

This is the part that should be exploited rather than merely defended.

1. **The dissent concurs on jurisdiction.** Dissent ¶1: "I concur with the majority's decision on jurisdiction — specifically, that the Tribunal has jurisdiction over this dispute and that **Respondent's jurisdictional objection is properly dismissed**." Pacífico's own nominee, a Mexican lawyer applying his own professional judgment, rejected Pacífico's Article V(1)(a) case, its unconscionability case, and its Mexican-mandatory-jurisdiction case. **This single sentence does more damage to items 3, 4 and 22 of the table at §13 than any argument Greenleaf can construct.**
2. **The dissent concurs on the counterclaim.** Dissent ¶1: "I further concur with the majority's dismissal of Respondent's Counterclaim." Item 12 disposed of.
3. **The dissent confirms the force majeure notice failure as a fact.** Dissent ¶¶3, 5 accept that no notice was given and argue only that the requirement should have been applied "more flexibly." That concedes the factual premise of the majority's holding. It is also a weaker position than the dissent acknowledges: Section 11.1 of the Agreement provides in terms that "**[f]ailure to provide such notice within the required fifteen (15) Business Day period shall constitute a waiver of the right to claim relief under this Section**" — the contract itself prescribes the consequence, leaving no room for the flexible construction the dissent favours. The dissent does not address that sentence.
4. **The dissent would have limited force majeure relief to Year 2 only** (Dissent ¶¶6–7), worth $840,000 — a fraction of what Pacífico argued for.
5. **The dissent nowhere mentions the dispositif discrepancy.** Lic. Beltrán Cisneros, who scrutinised the damages analysis line by line and disagreed with it in detail, did not remark on the $5,169,139 gap. The most natural inference is that the discrepancy was introduced at the stage of assembling the operative part, after the reasoning was settled — which supports the clerical-slip characterisation at §4.2(a). Greenleaf may deploy this inference, carefully.

### 14.4 The tactical answer, and how §15 defuses the dissent

Beyond Rule 26.3, Greenleaf has three responses:

**(a) The dissent is an invitation to merits review, and the court must decline it.** Everything Pacífico will quote concerns the sufficiency of the evidence for the Years 4 and 5 projection and the adequacy of the mitigation discount. Those are quintessentially merits questions. The correct submission is that the dissent's existence proves only that the Tribunal deliberated and that a minority disagreed — which is what Rules 26.1 and 26.3 contemplate. **To act on the dissent, the court would have to do the one thing Article V forbids.**

**(b) Under Rules 26.1 and 26.3 the majority decides.** A dissent is a record of disagreement, not a partial invalidity.

**(c) The recommendation at §15 substantially defuses the point.** If Greenleaf seeks $14,700,000, the gap between the claim and the dissent's figure is roughly threefold, and the dissent looks like vindication. If Greenleaf seeks **$9,530,861**, the comparison changes materially: the claim is the Tribunal's own reasoned determination, it includes $1,722,500 of costs and fees that the dissent's "$4.5–5.0 million before interest and costs" figure expressly excludes, and the residual gap is a disagreement between two arbitrators and one about the evidentiary basis for a damages projection — which is transparently a merits dispute the court may not enter. **The dissent is far more dangerous deployed against $14.7 million than against $9,530,861.**

### 14.5 Practical handling

Tender the dissent voluntarily as part of the certified award (Rule 26.3 requires it to be treated as part of the award document — §5). Address it proactively in the petition rather than waiting for Pacífico to introduce it: quote Rule 26.3 first, then quote the dissent's concurrence on jurisdiction and on the counterclaim, and only then note that the dissent's disagreement is confined to quantum and is not reviewable. **Handled in that order, the dissent is introduced to the court as a document that supports Greenleaf on jurisdiction before Pacífico can introduce it as a document that undermines Greenleaf on quantum.**

## 15. STRATEGIC RECOMMENDATION ON THE ENFORCEMENT AMOUNT

### 15.1 The recommendation

**Greenleaf should seek recognition and enforcement of USD $9,530,861, plus post-award interest at 5% per annum simple from June 28, 2023 to the date of payment, expressly reserving all rights in respect of the balance of the dispositif.**

The claimed figure should be presented not as a concession or a compromise, but as **the amount the Tribunal itself determined to be due**, quoting Award ¶86 in full and reproducing the Section IX.F summary table in the petition. The framing matters: Greenleaf is not discounting its award; it is asking the court to enforce the Tribunal's own reasoned determination, in the Tribunal's own words and figures.

### 15.2 The reasoning

**(a) It eliminates the only ground capable of defeating enforcement.** §12.3 identifies the failure-of-*motivación* argument as the single ground with real prospects. That argument exists only because the operative amount exceeds the reasoned amount. Claim the reasoned amount and the argument has no object: there is nothing unreasoned left to attack. Every remaining ground is weak, waived, or contradicted by Pacífico's own record.

**(b) It converts Pacífico's best point into Greenleaf's.** If Greenleaf claims $9,530,861 and Velázquez Ibarra nonetheless argues that the Award is internally inconsistent, Greenleaf's answer is devastating: *my client seeks precisely what the Tribunal reasoned, in the Tribunal's own figures; the respondent's complaint concerns a sum my client is not asking this court to enforce.* The argument collapses.

**(c) It avoids the risk of total refusal.** This is the decisive consideration. Article V(1)(c)'s severability proviso permits a court to sever, but **it does not compel it to.** A judge presented with a claim for $14,700,000 that the instrument's own reasoning contradicts may take one of three courses: enforce in full (unlikely); sever and enforce $9,530,861 (the best realistic outcome of that course); or conclude that the Award's damages determination is so internally incoherent as to be unenforceable, and refuse altogether. **The third course is a genuine possibility** before a bench that has refused enforcement on *orden público* grounds two or three times in a decade and has, on Dra. Olvera's account, blurred the *interno/internacional* line. Claiming $9,530,861 makes that course unavailable.

**(d) The risk/reward is plainly adverse.** Pressing for the extra $5,169,139 offers a marginal upside that is, in my assessment, very unlikely to be realised — the sum has no reasoning behind it and no court will supply reasoning the Tribunal omitted — against a downside of jeopardising the $9,530,861 that is solidly supported. Greenleaf would be wagering a strong claim to secure a weak one.

**(e) It has a real effect on judicial disposition.** Recognition proceedings are conducted before a judge who has formed no prior view. A claimant who voluntarily limits its claim to the reasoned figure, explains why, and puts the discrepancy before the court candidly, presents as reasonable and reliable. A claimant who presses for a figure its own award's reasoning contradicts — and is then shown to have done so — presents as opportunistic, at precisely the moment when Pacífico is asking the court to find that something about this award offends fundamental fairness.

**(f) Speed and collectability.** Pacífico has substantial, identifiable, attachable Mexican assets: three distribution warehouses in Guadalajara, Monterrey and Mexico City, and a fleet of refrigerated trucks, against annual revenue of approximately MXN $1.2 billion (Award ¶2). A clean enforcement of $9,530,861 that survives *amparo* and proceeds to execution is worth materially more to Greenleaf than a contested $14,700,000 that spends two further years in the Mexican courts. Every month of delay compounds Pacífico's opportunity to reorganise its asset position.

### 15.3 Alternatives considered and rejected

**(i) Seek a Rule 29 correction from the Tribunal.** Rejected: time-barred. The parties' window under Rule 29.1 was thirty days from receipt and the Tribunal's own-initiative window under Rule 29.2 closed on or about July 28, 2023. The Tribunal is *functus officio*. Worse, a request would create a contemporaneous document, authored by Greenleaf, characterising the Award as defective — which would be in Velázquez Ibarra's hands promptly and would be tendered in Guadalajara as Greenleaf's own admission. **Do not make this request.**

**(ii) Claim $14,700,000 and fall back to $9,530,861 in reply.** Rejected. It sacrifices the credibility benefit of §15.2(e), obliges the court to perform the severance itself rather than accepting Greenleaf's own framing, and hands Pacífico the opening submission of the case. If a fallback is to be made at all, making it first is worth far more than making it second.

**(iii) Claim $9,530,861 and pursue the balance separately.** Not recommended as an active strategy, though the reservation of rights costs nothing and should be included. Realistically the balance is unrecoverable: the Tribunal is *functus officio*, the reasoning does not exist, and no forum can now supply it. The reservation is there to preserve position, not to found a plan.

**(iv) Enforce the dissent's figure ($4.5–5.0 million).** Rejected without hesitation. There is no basis for it. The dissent is not the award (Rule 26.3), and adopting a minority arbitrator's figure would concede the merits review Greenleaf must resist throughout.

### 15.4 Points of drafting for the petition

1. **Claim USD $9,530,861 as the principal sum**, quoting Award ¶86 in the original and in certified translation, and reproducing the Section IX.F summary table.
2. **Explain the limitation openly and briefly, in a single confident paragraph.** Something to the effect that Greenleaf seeks enforcement of the sum the Tribunal determined to be due in the reasoned portion of its Award, and does not ask the court to enforce the operative figure to the extent it exceeds that determination. Candour here is a strategic asset, not a concession. It also forecloses any suggestion that Greenleaf concealed the discrepancy.
3. **Claim post-award interest at 5% per annum simple from June 28, 2023**, per dispositif ¶7 and Award ¶87 — **not** from the date of termination. The bundle chronology's statement that interest runs "from the date of termination" is wrong (§16) and would, if followed, double-count the pre-award interest already included within the $9,530,861.
4. **Reserve rights** as to the balance of the dispositif, in one sentence, without elaboration.
5. **Plead the Article V(1)(c) severability proviso affirmatively**, as authority for the court to recognise the reasoned portion, so that Greenleaf's own framing supplies the court with its doctrinal route.
6. **Plead the Article V(1)(e) position affirmatively and early**, supported by the Alberta court search (§10.3), rather than waiting for Pacífico to raise it.

## 17. OVERALL RISK ASSESSMENT AND ACTION PLAN

### 17.1 Risk assessment

**If Greenleaf seeks enforcement of USD $9,530,861** (recommended): the prospects are strong. Every Article V ground available to Pacífico is weak, waived, expressly authorised by rules Pacífico adopted, or contradicted by Pacífico's own pleadings and by the concurrence of its own party-appointed arbitrator. The Award is binding by contract, has not been set aside, cannot now be set aside, and no Article VI adjournment is available. The residual risks are (i) judicial receptivity to the Mexican-mandatory-law argument before a bench with a demonstrated *orden público* record, and (ii) delay through *amparo* proceedings. Neither is a likely path to refusal.

**If Greenleaf seeks enforcement of USD $14,700,000**: the risk profile changes materially and for the worse. Refusal of the unreasoned $5,169,139 becomes likely; refusal of the entire award becomes a realistic possibility rather than a remote one; and the litigation is fought on the only terrain where Pacífico has a point that a judge can verify in ten seconds and that Greenleaf cannot answer on its merits.

**The single most consequential decision in this matter is therefore the amount claimed, and it should be settled before anything else is drafted.**

## 18. CONCLUSION

The Final Award in PAC-2022-0041 is an enforceable instrument resting on a sound jurisdictional foundation and a procedurally regular arbitration. Pacífico participated fully for seventeen months, nominated one of the three arbitrators, advanced a counterclaim, and objected contemporaneously to almost nothing it now complains of. Its jurisdictional and unconscionability arguments were rejected unanimously — including by the Mexican arbitrator it chose. Its procedural complaints reduce to a two-day notice shortfall on a hearing it attended without objection. Its set-aside remedy was announced but never exercised, and has now expired.

There is one exception, and it is material. The Award orders payment of USD $14,700,000 when its own reasoning, its own summary table, and its own express determination at paragraph 86 support USD $9,530,861. The unexplained difference of USD $5,169,139 cannot be reconciled, cannot now be corrected, and is the one thing in this record that a Guadalajara court could properly refuse to enforce.

Greenleaf should not ask it to. **Claim the reasoned figure, claim it openly, and let Pacífico explain why a court should refuse to enforce the sum the Tribunal itself said was due.**
