### I.3 The clause under review

The operative text of MSA § 14.2, reproduced in full, is:

> "Any dispute, controversy, or claim arising out of or relating to this Agreement, or the breach, termination, or invalidity thereof, shall be finally resolved by arbitration administered by the Stonebridge Arbitral Institute in accordance with the SAI Arbitration Rules in effect at the time of the signing of this Agreement. The arbitration shall be conducted by a sole arbitrator mutually agreed upon by the parties within 15 calendar days of the filing of the Request for Arbitration. The seat of arbitration shall be Singapore. The language of the arbitration shall be English and German, with all submissions accepted in either language. The arbitrator shall render a final award within 90 calendar days of the constitution of the tribunal. The arbitrator shall not have the authority to award punitive, exemplary, or consequential damages. All arbitration proceedings, including the existence of the arbitration and any awards, shall remain strictly confidential in perpetuity. Discovery shall be limited to document production only, and each party shall bear its own costs regardless of the outcome. The parties waive any right to appeal the award on any grounds."

[MSA § 14.2.]

The clause must be read together with § 14.1 (New York governing law; CISG excluded; procedural law determined by the seat), § 14.3 (mandatory pre-arbitration escalation), § 14.4 (continued performance), § 12.1–12.2 (liability cap and damages exclusions), and §§ 15.2–15.4 (notice addresses, formalities, and language). Several of the most serious findings in this report arise from the interaction between § 14.2 and those neighbouring provisions rather than from § 14.2 read in isolation.

### I.4 The governing analytical framework

Three provisions of the Rules control how any clause/rule divergence is resolved, and they recur throughout the analysis:

- **Rules art. 1.2** — the arbitration is conducted under the Rules "subject to any modifications that the parties may agree upon, **provided such modifications are not inconsistent with any mandatory provision of these Rules or any mandatory provision of the law of the seat**."
- **Rules art. 1.4** — on conflict, "the agreement of the parties shall prevail, **except where** (a) such agreement would contravene a mandatory provision of these Rules; (b) such agreement would contravene the law of the seat of the arbitration; or (c) the SAI Court determines that giving effect to such agreement would render the arbitration impracticable."
- **Rules art. 33.1** — in matters not expressly provided for, the SAI Court and the Tribunal "shall make every effort to ensure that the award is enforceable at law."

The practical consequence is that most of the clause's departures from the Rules are **permitted** as party modifications. The question in almost every instance below is therefore not "is this allowed?" but rather: *(i)* is the modification caught by an art. 1.4 exception; *(ii)* is it enforceable under the law of the seat (Singapore) or the governing law (New York); and *(iii)* whichever way those two questions resolve, **does the modification help or hurt Greenleaf as claimant?** A provision can be perfectly valid and still be a serious strategic liability. Issues 1, 2, 5, 6, and 10 are of that character, and they are where the real exposure lies.

### I.5 Method

For each issue this report states, in fixed order: the clause wording relied upon; the institutional rule from which it deviates (or the gap it leaves); the nature of the deviation; the legal consequence, including enforceability analysis under both New York and Singapore law where the bifurcation bites; a severity rating; a recommended fix; and the record citations supporting the finding.

## II. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The arbitration clause is enforceable as an agreement to arbitrate. It is not, however, a well-drafted clause, and it was — as the assignment memorandum records — "originally proposed by Kuiper's German counsel at Feldhammer Grau and was accepted by Greenleaf with only minimal revisions" [Memo § 2.2]. The clause's asymmetries run consistently in Kuiper's favour. Fifteen deviations, conflicts, or gaps are identified: **two Critical, four High, seven Moderate, and two Low.**

**The two Critical findings are the ones that should drive strategy.**

**First, the 90-day award deadline (Issue 1) is not merely aggressive — it is arithmetically unachievable and it is a live award-annulment risk.** The clause requires a final award within 90 calendar days of the *constitution* of the tribunal. The Rules' own internal machinery consumes roughly a quarter of that window before the merits phase can begin: art. 9.2 allows the Tribunal 21 calendar days merely to fix a *provisional* timetable. Rules art. 24.1 sets a 180-day target measured from a materially later trigger — the last substantive hearing or last authorised submission — and art. 24.4 states expressly that "the time limit under this Article runs from the last substantive hearing or last authorized submission, **not from the constitution of the Tribunal**." The clause therefore both halves the period and relocates its starting point to the earliest possible moment. Critically, Rules art. 24.3 renders the *institutional* deadline non-invalidating ("Failure to render the award within the time limit shall not render the award invalid"), but that safe harbour by its terms protects only the art. 24.1 target — **it does not protect a deadline the parties themselves imposed by contract.** An award rendered outside a contractually mandated period is exposed to challenge as an award made otherwise than in accordance with the parties' agreed procedure. Because Greenleaf is the anticipated award creditor, this risk is borne almost entirely by our client, and Kuiper has every incentive to stay silent about the overrun and then attack the award. This must be fixed by written agreement with Kuiper before the Request is filed.

**Second, the remedial exclusion (Issue 2) directly attacks 27% of Greenleaf's own claim and, because of the way it is drafted, does so at the level of the tribunal's *authority* rather than as a mere substantive defence.** The clause provides that "[t]he arbitrator **shall not have the authority** to award punitive, exemplary, or consequential damages." Rules art. 34.3 expressly permits parties to limit the tribunal's remedial powers and requires the Tribunal to give such an agreement effect. Under New York law, mutual consequential-damages exclusions between sophisticated commercial parties are routinely enforced. The exclusion therefore stands. Greenleaf characterises $3.2 million of its $11.9 million claim as consequential damages [Memo § 3]. Worse, § 14.2's exclusion contains **no carve-outs at all**, whereas the parallel exclusion in MSA § 12.2 preserves claims arising under Section 13 (Indemnification) and "in cases of willful misconduct or fraud." The two provisions cannot both be given their literal effect: § 12.2 leaves consequential damages recoverable for wilful misconduct while § 14.2 strips the tribunal of power to award them in any circumstance. That internal contradiction is a drafting defect in a clause Kuiper's counsel drafted, and it is the principal doctrinal opening for recovering the $3.2 million. A further exposure the assignment memorandum does not address is that § 12.2's illustrative list of excluded damages expressly includes **"loss of data (other than the Service Provider's obligation to restore data under Section 7.4)"** — which places part of the supposedly "direct" $8.7 million database-corruption claim at risk as well, and makes obtaining the text of § 7.4 an immediate priority.

**Beyond the two Critical items, four High-severity findings require action before filing:** the pre-arbitration escalation condition in § 14.3 had **not** matured as at the date of the assignment memorandum and the record does not establish that the September 12, 2024 notice of breach satisfied the strict service formalities of § 15.3 (Issue 3); the dual English/German language designation imposes a structurally asymmetric cost and evidentiary burden on an English-only U.S. claimant and leaves the language of the award to the tribunal under Rules art. 12.2(c) (Issue 4); the perpetual, carve-out-free confidentiality undertaking omits every one of the five exceptions in Rules art. 20.2 and is being actively weaponised by Kuiper against Greenleaf's regulatory disclosures and its forensic expert (Issue 5); and the phrase "Discovery shall be limited to document production only" is ambiguous enough that Kuiper may argue it forecloses witness statements, expert reports, and cross-examination — in a case whose outcome turns on the Palladian forensic causation evidence (Issue 6).

**Two conclusions cut in Greenleaf's favour and should be defended, not fixed.** The clause's silence on the emergency arbitrator procedure preserves that mechanism in full (Issue 15), confirming the assignment memorandum's provisional view; and the sole-arbitrator designation, though it sits against a CHF ~11.9 million amount in controversy, is the cheaper and faster configuration and is one Greenleaf should resist Kuiper unwinding (Issue 7).

**One correction to the working assumptions in the assignment memorandum is required.** The memorandum contemplates obtaining evidence "potentially, [from] a U.S. federal court under 28 U.S.C. § 1782" [Memo § 5]. That avenue is very likely closed: the Supreme Court held in *ZF Automotive US, Inc. v. Luxshare, Ltd.*, 596 U.S. 619 (2022), that a private commercial arbitral panel is not a "foreign or international tribunal" within the meaning of § 1782. A privately constituted SAI tribunal will not qualify. Greenleaf's realistic preservation routes are the SAI emergency arbitrator under art. 15bis and Appendix II, and the Singapore courts exercising their power to grant interim measures in aid of arbitration.

**On timing, the record discloses a preservation problem more advanced than the assignment memorandum states.** Palladian estimates automated deletion of Kuiper's server logs "within 90 to 120 days from the date of the events," and the earliest relevant events occurred in **June 2024** [Memo § 5]. Ninety to 120 days from June 2024 expired in **September–October 2024** — before the date of the assignment memorandum. Logs for the June events may already be unrecoverable. The August 2024 events place the corresponding window in **late November to late December 2024**. The emergency arbitrator route consumes approximately 17–19 days end to end (Rules art. 15bis.2: appointment within 2 business days; Appendix II, EA-4: schedule within 2 business days of appointment; EA-5: decision within 15 calendar days of appointment). **The emergency application should be prepared and filed immediately and should not wait on resolution of any other issue in this report.** MSA § 14.3(d) expressly permits it notwithstanding the unexpired escalation period.

## V. ISSUE-BY-ISSUE ANALYSIS

---

### ISSUE 1 — The 90-Day Award Deadline, Measured From Constitution of the Tribunal

**Severity: CRITICAL**

#### Clause wording

> "The arbitrator shall render a final award within 90 calendar days of the constitution of the tribunal."

[MSA § 14.2, sentence 5.]

#### Institutional rule from which it deviates

**Rules art. 24.1:** "The Tribunal shall use its best efforts to render the final award within six months (180 calendar days) of the last substantive hearing or the filing of the last authorized written submission, whichever is later."

**Rules art. 24.4:** "For the avoidance of doubt, the time limit under this Article runs from the last substantive hearing or last authorized submission, **not from the constitution of the Tribunal**. The period between the constitution of the Tribunal and the commencement of substantive proceedings is not included in the calculation."

**Rules art. 24.3:** "The time limit under Article 24.1 is a target intended to promote efficiency. Failure to render the award within the time limit shall not render the award invalid or affect the Tribunal's authority to render the award."

**Rules art. 24.2:** "The SAI Court may extend the time limit under Article 24.1 upon a reasoned request by the Tribunal, or upon its own initiative . . . ."

Supporting provisions engaged: art. 9.2 (provisional timetable within 21 calendar days of receipt of the file); art. 13.2 (case management conference no later than the date for establishment of the provisional timetable); art. 14.1 (at least one round of written submissions by each party); art. 25.2 (reasoned award required); art. 28.3 (corrections, interpretations, and additional awards within 60 calendar days).

#### Nature of the deviation

This is the most severe departure in the clause, and it operates on two axes simultaneously.

**Axis one — duration.** The clause halves the institutional period, from 180 calendar days to 90.

**Axis two — trigger point.** This is the more damaging change and it is easily overlooked. The Rules measure their 180 days from the *back end* of the proceeding: the last substantive hearing or the last authorised written submission, whichever is later. Article 24.4 was evidently drafted to foreclose precisely the construction the clause adopts, stating in terms that the period runs "not from the constitution of the Tribunal," and that the interval between constitution and the commencement of substantive proceedings "is not included in the calculation." The clause takes exactly the trigger the Rules expressly disclaim. The result is that the contractual clock covers the *entire* proceeding — pleadings, document production, evidence, hearing, deliberation and drafting — whereas the institutional clock covers only the deliberation-and-drafting phase.

The two provisions are therefore not merely different in length. A tribunal complying with art. 24.1 might permissibly render an award 300 or 400 days after its constitution. The clause allows 90.

**The arithmetic.** Working from the Rules' own mandatory sequence, from Day 0 = constitution (Rules art. 8.1: the date on which the sole arbitrator has been both confirmed or appointed by the SAI Court **and** has filed a signed declaration of independence and impartiality):

| Step | Rule | Days consumed | Cumulative |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transmission of the file to the Tribunal | art. 9.1 | 0–3 | ~3 |
| Provisional timetable and case management conference | arts. 9.2, 13.2 | up to 21 | **~24 of 90** |
| First round of written submissions, both parties | art. 14.1 (minimum one round each) | 30–45 realistic | ~60 |
| Document production (the only disclosure the clause permits) | arts. 14.3, 18.2; MSA § 14.2 | 21–30 realistic | ~85 |
| Witness statements and expert reports | arts. 14.5, 17.4 | overlapping | — |
| Hearing | art. 16.1 (mandatory if any party requests) | 2–5 | — |
| Deliberation and drafting of a **reasoned** award (art. 25.2) | art. 25 | 21–45 realistic | **far beyond 90** |

Roughly a quarter of the contractual window is consumed by a procedural step — fixing a *provisional* timetable — that the Rules allow 21 days for and that produces no substantive progress. A further structural collision: Rules art. 4.4 gives the Respondent 30 calendar days from receipt of the Request to file its Answer. If the sole arbitrator is agreed within the clause's own 15-day window (§ 14.2, sentence 2) and confirmed promptly, **the tribunal may be constituted before Kuiper's Answer is even due** — meaning the 90-day clock could begin running while the Respondent's first pleading is still outstanding.

A final incoherence: Rules art. 28.3 requires the Tribunal to make any correction, interpretation, or additional award "within 60 calendar days of receipt of the request." A correction request made shortly after a timely award would necessarily produce a corrected instrument well outside the 90-day contractual window.

This is a dispute of $13.3 million total value [Memo § 3], turning on contested digital forensic causation across three clinical trial databases and 47 days of service outages [Memo § 2.3], to be conducted in two languages [MSA § 14.2] with a foreign respondent and a third-party forensic expert. As the assignment memorandum correctly observes, the deadline is "extraordinarily aggressive for a dispute of this complexity and dollar amount" [Memo § 4(d)]. That is an understatement: on the Rules' own timetable, it cannot be met.

#### Legal consequence

**(a) The award-annulment risk is real and it falls on Greenleaf.** Rules art. 24.3 provides that failure to meet the time limit "shall not render the award invalid" — but that safe harbour is expressly confined to "the time limit under Article 24.1," i.e. the *institutional* target. It says nothing about, and cannot cure, a breach of a deadline the parties fixed by contract. Likewise, art. 24.2 empowers the SAI Court to extend "the time limit under Article 24.1"; it confers no power to extend a contractual term. On its face, therefore, the Rules leave the 90-day contractual deadline unprotected and unextendable by the institution.

The seat is Singapore [MSA § 14.2], so the supervisory regime is the International Arbitration Act and, through it, the UNCITRAL Model Law. Model Law art. 34(2)(a)(iv) permits an award to be set aside where "the arbitral procedure was not in accordance with the agreement of the parties." An award rendered on day 150 when the parties agreed on 90 is, at minimum, a colourable art. 34(2)(a)(iv) case. The same defect maps onto Article V(1)(d) of the New York Convention at the enforcement stage ("the arbitral procedure was not in accordance with the agreement of the parties"), which matters because Greenleaf will likely need to enforce against a German entity and possibly in a U.S. court under 9 U.S.C. §§ 201–208.

**Greenleaf is the anticipated claimant and therefore the anticipated award creditor.** The asymmetry is severe: Kuiper has no incentive to press the tribunal to meet the deadline, and every incentive to allow it to be missed and then attack the resulting award. Rules art. 30.1 (waiver of the right to object) is only a partial answer — it deems a party to have waived where it "knows of a departure . . . and proceeds with the arbitration without promptly raising an objection," and art. 30.2 fixes the objection deadline at "the next written submission or hearing." A tribunal's failure to render an award by a given date is a departure that crystallises *after* the last submission and hearing, when there may be no procedural occasion left on which Kuiper is required to object. Greenleaf should not rely on art. 30 to cure this.

**(b) The counter-risk: compression at the expense of due process.** If the tribunal instead treats the 90 days as binding and truncates the procedure to meet it, a different annulment ground opens. Rules art. 18.1 requires that "the parties are treated with equality and that each party is given a reasonable opportunity to present its case"; Rules art. 16.1 requires the Tribunal to "hold a hearing if requested by any party." A schedule that denies a reasonable opportunity to present the case engages Model Law art. 18 and, in Singapore, the natural-justice ground for setting aside (see *Soh Beng Tee & Co Pte Ltd v Fairmount Development Pte Ltd* [2007] SGCA 28). Compressing a bilingual, expert-heavy $13.3 million dispute into 90 days from constitution invites exactly that complaint — and Greenleaf, as the party bearing the burden of proving causation through Palladian's forensic evidence [Memo § 2.3], is the party most likely to be squeezed.

**(c) The arbitrator-recruitment problem.** A competent arbitrator asked to accept a mandate that carries a contractual 90-day award obligation in a matter of this size will either decline, or accept subject to a written reservation. This directly compounds Issues 8 and 14: the pool of candidates who are (i) fluent in English and German, (ii) conversant in cloud infrastructure and data management, (iii) conversant in life sciences regulation, (iv) independent and conflict-free, **and** (v) willing to bind themselves to a 90-day award, is very small. The 15-day agreement window (Issue 8) makes finding that person harder still.

**(d) The tribunal's own escape route is uncertain.** A tribunal might reason under Rules art. 33.1 — which directs it to "make every effort to ensure that the award is enforceable at law" — that it should not comply with a deadline whose observance would produce an unenforceable award for want of due process. It might alternatively treat the deadline as directory rather than mandatory, on the analogy of art. 24.3. Neither route is assured, and Greenleaf should not build its strategy on the hope that a tribunal will disapply a term the parties bargained for.

#### Severity rationale

**Critical.** It is the only issue in the clause that can invalidate the award Greenleaf hopes to obtain, and the risk sits asymmetrically on our client. It is also the issue on which Kuiper's incentives are most sharply opposed to Greenleaf's, which means it cannot be left to be sorted out in the proceeding.

#### Recommended fix

1. **Seek a written variation of the 90-day term before the Request is filed.** This is the single highest-value item on the pre-filing checklist. Propose to Kuiper a short bilateral side letter under which the parties agree that (i) the 90-day period in § 14.2 runs from the closure of proceedings under Rules art. 21.1, rather than from constitution; and (ii) the period may be extended by the Tribunal or the SAI Court under Rules art. 24.2. Kuiper has an aligned interest here — it is a respondent asserting a $1.4 million counterclaim [Memo § 2.3; Vogel Email, Reservation of Rights] and will itself be prejudiced by a truncated schedule and by an award vulnerable to annulment. The October 28 email's expressed wish to resolve the matter "in an orderly and efficient manner" [Vogel Email] and its complaint that a 15-day appointment window "cannot be compressed" supply a natural opening.
2. **If Kuiper refuses, secure the point in the Terms of Reference.** Rules art. 13.1 permits the Tribunal to draw up Terms of Reference defining "the procedural rules to be followed." Ask the Tribunal to record, in the Terms of Reference or the first procedural order, the parties' common position on the construction and extendability of the 90-day term. A signed Terms of Reference recording that construction is close to as good as a side letter.
3. **Raise the point on the record at the earliest possible moment and repeat it.** Under Rules art. 4.2(e), include observations on the timetable in the Request itself. Under art. 13.3(a), raise it at the case management conference. Reserve rights in every subsequent submission. The object is twofold: to defeat any later suggestion that Greenleaf waived, and — more importantly — to build a record that Kuiper knew of and acquiesced in the overrun, so that art. 30.1 bites against *Kuiper* if it later attacks the award.
4. **Do not rely on the appeal waiver as protection.** For the reasons in Issue 11, the waiver in § 14.2, sentence 9, will not prevent Kuiper from invoking Model Law art. 34 at the seat, nor Article V of the New York Convention at the place of enforcement.
5. **Instruct candidate arbitrators on the point at the outset.** Any candidate should be told of the 90-day term and of Greenleaf's position on its construction before nomination, so that acceptance is not later revisited.

### ISSUE 2 — Exclusion of Punitive, Exemplary, and Consequential Damages as a Limit on the Arbitrator's *Authority*; Irreconcilable Conflict With MSA § 12.2

**Severity: CRITICAL**

#### Clause wording

> "The arbitrator shall not have the authority to award punitive, exemplary, or consequential damages."

[MSA § 14.2, sentence 6.]

The parallel provision in the body of the MSA reads:

> "EXCEPT FOR A PARTY'S OBLIGATIONS UNDER SECTION 13 (INDEMNIFICATION) AND EXCEPT IN CASES OF WILLFUL MISCONDUCT OR FRAUD, IN NO EVENT SHALL EITHER PARTY BE LIABLE TO THE OTHER PARTY FOR ANY INDIRECT, INCIDENTAL, SPECIAL, OR CONSEQUENTIAL DAMAGES, INCLUDING BUT NOT LIMITED TO LOSS OF REVENUE, LOSS OF PROFITS, LOSS OF BUSINESS, LOSS OF DATA (OTHER THAN THE SERVICE PROVIDER'S OBLIGATION TO RESTORE DATA UNDER SECTION 7.4), OR COST OF PROCUREMENT OF SUBSTITUTE SERVICES . . . ."

[MSA § 12.2.]

#### Institutional rule from which it deviates

**Rules art. 34.1(a):** the Tribunal has power to award "damages, whether compensatory, **consequential**, or otherwise available under the applicable law governing the substance of the dispute."

**Rules art. 34.2:** "The Tribunal shall not award punitive or exemplary damages unless expressly authorized by the applicable law governing the substance of the dispute."

**Rules art. 34.3:** "Nothing in these Rules shall prevent the parties from agreeing to limit the remedies available to the Tribunal. Any such agreement shall be given effect by the Tribunal, subject to the mandatory provisions of the applicable law."

#### Nature of the deviation

Three distinct points must be separated.

**(a) The punitive and exemplary limb is surplusage.** Rules art. 34.2 already bars punitive and exemplary damages absent express authorisation by the substantive law, and the substantive law here is New York law [MSA § 14.1]. New York does not permit punitive damages for ordinary breach of contract; they are available only where the conduct is actionable as an independent tort, is egregious, is directed at the public generally, and is part of a pattern (*Rocanova v. Equitable Life Assurance Society*, 83 N.Y.2d 603 (1994); *New York University v. Continental Insurance Co.*, 87 N.Y.2d 308 (1995)). Nothing in the record suggests that threshold is approached. **This limb costs Greenleaf nothing.**

**(b) The consequential-damages limb is a genuine and material deviation, and it is enforceable.** Rules art. 34.1(a) would otherwise place consequential damages squarely within the tribunal's remedial power. The clause removes them. Rules art. 34.3 expressly authorises that removal and directs the Tribunal to give it effect. There is accordingly **no conflict with the Rules** — the clause is a permitted modification under arts. 1.2 and 34.3. The problem is not validity. The problem is that Greenleaf itself characterises **$3.2 million of its $11.9 million claim — 26.9% — as consequential damages** [Memo § 3].

**(c) The framing as a limit on *authority* makes this materially worse than an ordinary damages waiver.** § 12.2 is drafted as a limitation on *liability* ("in no event shall either party be liable"). § 14.2 is drafted as a limitation on the *arbitrator's power* ("the arbitrator shall not have the authority to award"). That distinction has real consequences. A liability limitation is a substantive defence which, if the tribunal gets it wrong, produces an erroneous award — and an erroneous award is not reviewable. A limitation on the arbitrator's authority is a restriction on the scope of the submission to arbitration. If the tribunal awards consequential damages notwithstanding § 14.2, Kuiper can characterise the award as one dealing with a matter "beyond the scope of the submission to arbitration" — Model Law art. 34(2)(a)(iii) at the Singapore seat, and Article V(1)(c) of the New York Convention at any enforcement forum. **A favourable award on the $3.2 million would therefore be exposed to annulment or refusal of enforcement in a way that an ordinary damages defence would not be.** This substantially raises the cost of simply pleading the $3.2 million and hoping the tribunal is sympathetic.

**(d) § 14.2 and § 12.2 cannot both be given literal effect.** § 12.2 preserves consequential damages in two situations: obligations under Section 13 (Indemnification), and "cases of willful misconduct or fraud." § 14.2 contains **no carve-outs whatsoever**. Read literally, § 14.2 strips the tribunal of authority to award consequential damages even in the very cases § 12.2 says remain actionable — rendering § 12.2's carve-outs a nullity, and doing so in the one forum (arbitration) to which § 14.2 sends every dispute. Under New York's ordinary canons an agreement is construed to give effect to all its provisions and to avoid rendering any provision meaningless. There is therefore a respectable argument that § 14.2's remedial exclusion must be read as **subject to the same carve-outs as § 12.2** — i.e. that it does not bar consequential damages arising from wilful misconduct. That reading is Greenleaf's best doctrinal route to the $3.2 million, and it exists only because Kuiper's own counsel drafted the two provisions inconsistently.

**(e) A further exposure the assignment memorandum does not identify: part of the "direct" $8.7 million may also be caught.** § 12.2's illustrative list of excluded damages expressly includes **"loss of data (other than the Service Provider's obligation to restore data under Section 7.4)."** Greenleaf's $8.7 million "direct" claim is for "corruption of three clinical trial databases, including costs of data reconstruction, delayed trial timelines, and regulatory re-submissions to the U.S. Food and Drug Administration" [Memo § 3]. Kuiper can be expected to argue that (i) the database corruption is "loss of data"; (ii) the parties expressly agreed that the *sole* remedy for loss of data is Kuiper's restoration obligation under § 7.4; and (iii) the "delayed trial timelines" and FDA re-submission components are in substance consequential losses dressed as direct ones. If any part of that argument succeeds, the exposure is not $3.2 million but a much larger share of the claim. **Section 7.4 is not in the record** — the MSA excerpt reproduces only Sections 12, 14, and 15 — and obtaining it is an immediate priority (see Part X).

#### Legal consequence

**Enforceability under New York law: the exclusion will be upheld.** New York enforces mutual limitations of liability and consequential-damages exclusions between sophisticated commercial parties, subject to a public-policy limit for intentional wrongdoing. *Metropolitan Life Insurance Co. v. Noble Lowndes International, Inc.*, 84 N.Y.2d 430 (1994), is directly instructive both on enforceability and on the construction of a "wilful misconduct" carve-out of the kind in § 12.2. MSA § 12.3 reinforces the point by reciting that the limitations "reflect a reasonable allocation of risk . . . and form an essential basis of the bargain." Greenleaf should not expect to defeat the exclusion frontally.

**Enforceability at the seat: no independent Singapore obstacle.** The exclusion is a matter of substantive contract law governed by New York law under § 14.1; Singapore as the seat supplies the procedural law and the supervisory jurisdiction [MSA § 14.1(b)]. There is no mandatory Singapore rule that would displace a New-York-law-governed remedial limitation in a commercial contract between a Delaware corporation and a German GmbH. The seat's relevance here is at the *annulment* stage, per point (c) above, not at the level of validity.

**The route to recovery is recharacterisation, not invalidation.** New York distinguishes *general* (direct) damages — those that flow naturally and directly from the breach and are the "natural and probable consequence" of it — from *special* or *consequential* damages, which arise from the injured party's particular circumstances and are recoverable only if within the contemplation of the parties at contracting (*Kenford Co. v. County of Erie*, 73 N.Y.2d 312 (1989); *American List Corp. v. U.S. News & World Report, Inc.*, 75 N.Y.2d 38 (1990)). Critically, the label is not determinative and the same head of loss can be general or consequential depending on its relationship to the bargain: in *Biotronik A.G. v. Conor Medsystems Ireland, Ltd.*, 22 N.Y.3d 799 (2014), the Court of Appeals held that lost profits were **general** damages where they flowed directly from the contract itself rather than from a collateral business arrangement. That analysis is available here. Greenleaf contracted for a cloud-based LIMS to run clinical research; availability of the platform was the very subject of the bargain, not a collateral use of it. Losses caused by 47 days of outage of the contracted-for service are strongly arguable as general damages flowing directly from the promised performance.

The $3.2 million presently characterised as consequential comprises "lost productivity, third-party vendor penalties, and delayed commercial milestones" [Memo § 3]. These should be disaggregated and re-pleaded:

| Component | Recharacterisation theory | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Lost productivity during 47 days of outage | Direct: loss of the very service contracted for; the value of the bargained-for performance not received | **Strong** |
| Costs incurred to work around the outages | Direct/incidental: mitigation costs, recoverable as a distinct head and not barred by a consequential-damages exclusion | **Strong** |
| Third-party vendor penalties | Consequential in form; arguably general if the vendor arrangements were within the parties' contemplation at contracting | **Moderate — depends on documents not in the record** |
| Delayed commercial milestones | Classic consequential loss; hardest to recharacterise | **Weak** |

Note also that § 12.2 expressly excludes "cost of procurement of substitute services," which will constrain any attempt to plead substitute-provider costs as direct.

**Note on symmetry.** The exclusion is bilateral, and it also constrains Kuiper. But the constraint is worthless to Greenleaf as a matter of exposure: Kuiper's counterclaim is for **$1.4 million in unpaid invoices** [Memo § 2.3; Vogel Email], a liquidated, indisputably direct claim that the exclusion does not touch. **The exclusion therefore operates asymmetrically against Greenleaf in this dispute**, notwithstanding its formally mutual drafting. That asymmetry is consistent with the memorandum's observation that Kuiper's counsel drafted the clause [Memo § 2.2].

**Note on the liability cap.** MSA § 12.1 caps aggregate liability at $15 million per party. Greenleaf's total claim of $11.9 million sits below the cap [Memo § 3], so the cap is not presently engaged — but it becomes relevant if claims are amended upward, and interest and costs should be modelled against it.

#### Severity rationale

**Critical.** It defeats a material portion of the recovery Greenleaf is seeking, it is enforceable, it converts what would otherwise be a merits defence into an annulment ground, and — because of the § 7.4 / "loss of data" point — the true exposure may exceed the $3.2 million the assignment memorandum identifies.

#### Recommended fix

1. **Obtain MSA § 7.4 and Section 13 immediately.** These are the two provisions on which the scope of the exclusion turns and neither is in the record. § 7.4 determines whether data restoration is an exclusive remedy for loss of data; Section 13 determines the width of the indemnification carve-out preserved by § 12.2.
2. **Re-plead the damages case before filing.** Restructure the claim so that the maximum defensible quantum is advanced as general/direct damages under *Biotronik*, with the mitigation-cost and incidental-loss components separately particularised. Do not simply carry forward the memorandum's $8.7m/$3.2m split into the Request; the split as presently framed concedes Kuiper's best point.
3. **Plead the § 12.2 / § 14.2 conflict affirmatively and early.** Advance the construction that § 14.2's remedial exclusion imports § 12.2's carve-outs. Plead wilful misconduct in the alternative if the Palladian findings support it — the assignment memorandum records that Palladian's preliminary findings place the corruption's origin on Kuiper's servers [Memo § 2.3], and any evidence of knowledge of platform defects, suppressed incident reports, or disregarded alerts would support that plea.
4. **Consider seeking an early partial award on construction.** Rules art. 2.8 defines "Award" to include partial and interim awards, and art. 15.3 confirms the Tribunal's discretion as to form. Resolving the § 12.2 / § 14.2 conflict and the direct/consequential characterisation at the outset would let Greenleaf calibrate its claim and settlement posture. The countervailing consideration is the 90-day deadline (Issue 1), which leaves little room for bifurcation — this is another reason to fix Issue 1 first.
5. **Do not seek an award of punitive or exemplary damages.** It is barred by Rules art. 34.2, by New York law, and by the clause; requesting it costs credibility for no possible gain.
6. **Model the downside.** Prepare a recovery model on the assumption that the $3.2 million is wholly excluded and that Kuiper recovers its $1.4 million counterclaim in full — a net position of $7.3 million before costs, against costs that are irrecoverable under the clause (Issue 10). That figure, not $11.9 million, should anchor settlement authority.

Authorities: *Rocanova*, 83 N.Y.2d 603; *N.Y. Univ. v. Continental Ins.*, 87 N.Y.2d 308; *Metropolitan Life v. Noble Lowndes*, 84 N.Y.2d 430; *Kenford v. ### ISSUE 3 — The Pre-Arbitration Escalation Condition Had Not Matured, and Service of the Dispute Notice Is Unproven

**Severity: HIGH**

#### Clause wording

> "(a) Before commencing arbitration under Section 14.2, a Party asserting a claim shall first deliver written notice of the dispute to the other Party in accordance with Section 15.3 (the 'Dispute Notice'). The Dispute Notice shall describe in reasonable detail the nature of the dispute and the relief sought.
> (b) Within thirty (30) calendar days following delivery of the Dispute Notice, senior representatives of each Party (at the level of Vice President or above) shall meet (in person or by videoconference) and attempt in good faith to resolve the dispute through direct negotiation.
> (c) If the dispute is not resolved within sixty (60) calendar days following delivery of the Dispute Notice, either Party may commence arbitration pursuant to Section 14.2."

[MSA § 14.3(a)–(c).] And:

> "All formal notices required under this Agreement, including but not limited to notices of breach, notices of termination, and notices relating to dispute resolution under Section 14, shall be sent to the Parties at their respective addresses set forth in Section 15.2 and to their respective General Counsel (or Head of Legal, as applicable) **by both email and internationally recognized courier service**. A Notice relating to dispute resolution under Section 14 **shall be effective only if delivered in accordance with this Section 15.3**."

[MSA § 15.3 (emphasis added).]

#### Institutional rule engaged (and the gap)

**The SAI Rules contain no multi-tier or pre-condition machinery at all.** Rules art. 4.1 provides simply that "[a] party wishing to have recourse to arbitration under these Rules shall file a Request for Arbitration with the SAI Secretariat. The date on which the Request is received by the SAI Secretariat shall be deemed to be the date of commencement of the arbitration." Article 4.2 lists what the Request must contain; compliance with contractual pre-conditions is not among the requirements, and there is no provision empowering or obliging the Secretariat to screen for it.

**This is a gap, not a conflict** — and it is a gap that cuts against Greenleaf. Because the Secretariat will not police the condition, its non-satisfaction will surface only as an objection by Kuiper, raised before the tribunal after Greenleaf has filed, paid the filing fee, and committed to the proceeding.

#### Nature of the deviation

**(a) On the face of the record, the 60-day period had not expired as at the date of the assignment memorandum.** Greenleaf "issued a formal notice of breach to Kuiper on September 12, 2024" [Memo § 2.3; and see Vogel Email, opening paragraph, acknowledging "receipt of Greenleaf Biotech Solutions, Inc.'s formal notice of breach dated September 12, 2024"]. If that notice constitutes the Dispute Notice, then:

- the § 14.3(b) senior-representative meeting was due by **October 12, 2024**; and
- the earliest date on which arbitration could be commenced under § 14.3(c) was **November 11, 2024**.

The assignment memorandum is dated **November 4, 2024** — seven days before the earliest permissible commencement. Any Request filed on or before November 10, 2024 would be premature on the face of the contract.

**(b) The record does not establish that the September 12 notice satisfies § 15.3.** Three requirements must all be met, and none is documented:

1. *Dual-channel delivery.* § 15.3 requires delivery "by **both** email and internationally recognized courier service," and provides that a Section 14 dispute notice "shall be effective **only if** delivered in accordance with this Section 15.3." The record does not show that the September 12 notice went by courier as well as email. **If it went by email alone, it is contractually ineffective and the 60-day clock has not started at all.**
2. *Correct addressee and address.* § 15.2 designates Dr. Katrin Vogel, Head of Legal, at Leopoldstraße 78, 80802 Munich, and the email address `k.vogel@kuiperdata.de`. See Issue 13 on the address discrepancies in the record.
3. *Content.* § 14.3(a) requires the Dispute Notice to "describe in reasonable detail the nature of the dispute **and the relief sought**." A notice of breach does not necessarily state the relief sought. The record does not contain the September 12 notice itself.

**(c) The record does not establish that the § 14.3(b) meeting occurred.** § 14.3(b) requires senior representatives "at the level of Vice President or above" to meet in person or by videoconference within 30 days. Nothing in the record evidences such a meeting. Kuiper's October 3, 2024 written response [Memo § 2.3] is a pleading of position, not a negotiation meeting. The October 28 email is likewise correspondence, not a meeting, and it in fact invites further discussion ("we would appreciate reasonable advance notice so that the parties may first explore whether a more efficient resolution is possible") [Vogel Email] — language Kuiper could later deploy to argue that Greenleaf refused to engage in the contractually required good-faith negotiation.

#### Legal consequence

**(a) The likely characterisation at the Singapore seat is admissibility, not jurisdiction — which is good news, but incomplete news.** Singapore's appellate courts have drawn a firm distinction between challenges to the tribunal's *jurisdiction* (reviewable by the court, including on a setting-aside application) and objections to the *admissibility* of a claim (a matter for the tribunal, and not a setting-aside ground): see *BBA v BAZ* [2020] SGCA 53 and *BTN v BTP* [2020] SGCA 105. Non-compliance with a pre-arbitration escalation condition is generally treated as going to admissibility. The practical implication is that a premature filing is unlikely to destroy the arbitration or produce an award vulnerable to annulment on that ground.

**(b) But the tribunal can still stay, dismiss, or sanction.** A tribunal that finds the condition unsatisfied may stay the proceedings until the condition is met, or dismiss the claim without prejudice to re-filing. Either outcome would cost Greenleaf months and a second filing fee, and would restart the arbitrator-appointment process. In a matter where evidence is being destroyed by automated retention policies [Memo § 5], delay is the most expensive commodity there is.

**(c) It hands Kuiper a free procedural attack at exactly the wrong moment.** Kuiper's October 28 email is already staking out procedural objections on two fronts [Vogel Email]. A premature or defectively noticed filing gives it a third, and one that would be litigated in the opening weeks of the case, consuming part of the 90-day window (Issue 1) if the tribunal is by then constituted.

**(d) The emergency arbitrator route is unaffected.** MSA § 14.3(d) is express: "Notwithstanding the foregoing, nothing in this Section 14.3 shall prevent a Party from seeking emergency or interim relief pursuant to the arbitration rules referenced in Section 14.2 or from a court of competent jurisdiction where such relief is necessary to prevent irreparable harm." **Greenleaf may therefore apply for emergency arbitrator relief immediately, whether or not the escalation period has run.** This is important given the log-deletion timeline and is the reason the emergency application should be decoupled from the merits filing.

#### Severity rationale

**High.** It is fully curable and it does not threaten the award, which is why it is not Critical. But it is a condition precedent to the entire arbitration, the record does not establish compliance, and getting it wrong costs months in a matter where months are not available.

#### Recommended fix

1. **Verify the September 12, 2024 notice immediately.** Retrieve the notice itself and the transmission records. Confirm: (i) courier dispatch and confirmed receipt in addition to email; (ii) delivery to Dr. Vogel as Head of Legal at the § 15.2 address; and (iii) that the notice describes the nature of the dispute *and the relief sought* in reasonable detail.
2. **If any element is missing, serve a fresh, compliant Dispute Notice today.** Serve it by both email and DHL/FedEx/UPS with confirmed receipt, to Dr. Vogel at the § 15.2 address, expressly labelled "Dispute Notice pursuant to Section 14.3(a) of the Master Services Agreement," describing the dispute and stating the relief sought (the full $11.9 million with a reservation as to characterisation — see Issue 2 — plus interim relief and interest). Serving a belt-and-braces notice costs nothing and starts a clean, provable 60-day clock. Do this even if the September 12 notice is thought to be compliant, expressly and without prejudice to Greenleaf's position that the clock started on September 12.
3. **Offer and document the § 14.3(b) senior-representative meeting.** Write to Kuiper proposing a videoconference between Greenleaf and Kuiper representatives at VP level or above, on stated dates. If Kuiper declines or fails to respond, the correspondence establishes Greenleaf's good-faith compliance and neutralises the objection. If Kuiper accepts, the meeting is held under Rule 408 / without-prejudice protection and Greenleaf loses nothing.
4. **Do not file the Request before the 60-day period has demonstrably expired**, unless the emergency situation compels it — and if it does, file the emergency arbitrator application under § 14.3(d) rather than the merits Request.
5. **Plead compliance affirmatively in the Request.** Rules art. 4.2(c) requires "a description of the nature and circumstances of the dispute." Include a short section recording the Dispute Notice, its date and mode of service, the offer of the senior-representative meeting, and the expiry of the 60-day period. This forecloses the objection at the pleading stage.

### ISSUE 4 — Dual English/German Language Designation With No Primary Language and No Award Language

**Severity: HIGH**

#### Clause wording

> "The language of the arbitration shall be English and German, with all submissions accepted in either language."

[MSA § 14.2, sentence 4.]

#### Institutional rule from which it deviates

**Rules art. 12.1:** "The language or languages of the arbitration shall be as agreed by the parties."

**Rules art. 12.2:** "**Where the parties have designated more than one language, the Tribunal shall determine procedures for the use of such languages**, including: (a) which language or languages shall be used for written submissions; (b) which language or languages shall be used for hearings; (c) **which language shall be used for the award**; and (d) whether translation of documents or interpretation at hearings shall be required, and if so, at whose cost."

**Rules art. 12.4:** "The Tribunal may order that any documents submitted in a language other than the language(s) of the arbitration be accompanied by a translation into the language(s) of the arbitration."

#### Nature of the deviation

There is **no conflict** with the Rules: art. 12.1 gives effect to whatever the parties agree, and art. 12.2 contemplates exactly this situation. The deviation is one of *omission*. Having exercised the party autonomy that art. 12.1 confers, the parties then failed to make the three decisions that art. 12.2 identifies as consequential: which language governs written submissions, which governs hearings, and which governs the award. Article 12.2 therefore transfers all three decisions to the Tribunal.

The clause also creates a **latent internal inconsistency with MSA § 15.4**, which provides that "All Notices shall be in the English language," and requires an English translation of any non-English supporting documentation. So the Dispute Notice initiating the whole process must be in English, while the arbitration it initiates may be conducted in German. That inconsistency is minor in itself but is useful evidence that the parties' baseline contractual language of dealing was English.

#### Legal consequence

**(a) Kuiper has already moved to exploit the omission, and its position is colourable.** The October 28 email states that Kuiper "interprets this provision as establishing German as a co-equal language of the proceedings," that it "expects all substantive submissions, witness statements, and expert reports to be filed in German," that it "will exercise our right under the clause to submit all of our materials in German," and — going further — that "the award should be rendered in German, and that the tribunal will need to determine this question pursuant to Article 12 of the . . . SAI Rules" [Vogel Email, Language of the Arbitration].

On the text, Kuiper is right about the mechanism: art. 12.2(c) does hand the award-language question to the Tribunal. Kuiper is also right that the clause designates German as a co-equal language and permits it to file in German. What Kuiper is *not* entitled to is a presumption. Article 12.2 requires the Tribunal to *determine* procedures having regard to the circumstances; art. 12.3 (which applies where there is no agreement) identifies "the language of the contract" as a relevant circumstance, and that reasoning informs the art. 12.2 exercise too. Here the MSA is written in English, § 15.4 requires all Notices in English, Kuiper's own Head of Legal corresponds with Greenleaf in English [Vogel Email], and Greenleaf is a U.S. corporation whose personnel and forensic expert (Palladian, a U.S. firm) work in English [Memo §§ 2.1, 2.3]. **The circumstances favour English as the primary procedural language and as the language of the award, notwithstanding that German remains a permitted filing language.**

**(b) The burden is structurally asymmetric and falls on Greenleaf.** Kuiper is a German company with German-speaking witnesses, German documents and German counsel; it can file in German at no incremental cost and impose translation costs on Greenleaf. Greenleaf cannot reciprocate. Under art. 12.2(d) the Tribunal decides "at whose cost" translation and interpretation are borne — but the clause's each-party-bears-own-costs provision (Issue 10) means Greenleaf will likely absorb its own translation and interpretation costs irrespective of outcome. In a document-heavy forensic dispute, translation of Kuiper's server logs, incident reports, and internal communications, plus interpretation at hearings, is a material and irrecoverable cost.

**(c) The award language has direct enforcement consequences.** Greenleaf is a Delaware corporation likely to enforce against a German entity, potentially in the United States or Germany. A German-language award would require certified translation for enforcement in a U.S. court under 9 U.S.C. §§ 201–208, and the New York Convention (art. IV(2)) requires a translation certified by an official or sworn translator or a diplomatic or consular agent where the award is not in the official language of the enforcing country. That is cost, delay, and one more thing for an award debtor to argue about. **Securing an English award is worth real money.**

**(d) It shrinks the arbitrator pool, compounding Issues 8 and 14.** A tribunal that must read submissions in both English and German, hear evidence in both, and write an award in one requires genuine bilingual competence on top of subject-matter expertise. Kuiper's own email concedes the difficulty of finding someone with "appropriate expertise in both cloud computing and data management, on the one hand, and life sciences regulation, on the other" [Vogel Email]; add bilingualism, independence, availability, and willingness to accept a 90-day award mandate (Issue 1), and the candidate set becomes very thin.

**(e) It aggravates the 90-day deadline.** Bilingual proceedings are slower: translation turnaround, interpreted hearings, and a bilingual record all add weeks. Issues 1 and 4 compound each other.

#### Severity rationale

**High.** No enforceability threat, but a live, already-asserted dispute that will impose material irrecoverable cost on Greenleaf, complicate enforcement if the award is in German, and shrink the arbitrator pool at a moment when speed matters.

#### Recommended fix

1. **Attempt a written language protocol with Kuiper before filing.** Propose: English as the primary procedural language and the language of the award; German-language documents admissible in original with English translations of the parts relied upon; witnesses free to testify in German with interpretation; each party bearing the cost of translating its own materials into English. This is a reasonable package that respects Kuiper's right to work in German while protecting the English record. Kuiper's stated wish for an "orderly and efficient" process [Vogel Email] gives an opening.
2. **If no agreement, make art. 12.2 observations part of the Request.** Rules art. 4.2(e) expressly permits "any observations regarding the number of arbitrators, the seat of the arbitration, and the language of the arbitration." Use it. Set out the art. 12.2 factors and the case for English, supported by: the MSA is in English; § 15.4 requires Notices in English; the parties' pre-arbitration correspondence including Kuiper's own October 28 email is in English; Greenleaf's witnesses and forensic expert work in English; and enforcement considerations under art. 33.1 (the SAI Court and Tribunal must "make every effort to ensure that the award is enforceable at law").
3. **Put the award-language question on the agenda of the first case management conference.** Rules art. 13.2 requires a CMC as soon as practicable after constitution, and art. 13.3(e) sweeps in "any other matters relevant to the efficient conduct of the proceedings." Obtain a determination under art. 12.2(c) early, not at the end.
4. **Push for a costs allocation for translation under art. 12.2(d).** Article 12.2(d) is a distinct power to allocate translation and interpretation costs, and it is arguable that it operates independently of the general costs provision the clause displaces (Issue 10). Preserve the argument that "each party shall bear its own costs" does not reach art. 12.2(d) allocations.
5. **Screen arbitrator candidates for genuine bilingual drafting capability, not just comprehension.** Reading German and writing a reasoned award in English at speed are different skills.
6. **Budget for translation.** Include a specific translation and interpretation line in the case budget, on the assumption it is irrecoverable.

MSA §§ 14.2 (sentence 4), 15.4; Rules arts. 4.2(e), 12.1, 12.2(a)–(d), 12.3, 12.4, 13.2, 13.3(e), 33.1; Memo §§ 2.1, 2.3, 4(c), 6(a); Vogel Email (Language of the Arbitration).

### ISSUE 5 — Perpetual Confidentiality With None of the Rules' Carve-Outs

**Severity: HIGH**

#### Clause wording

> "All arbitration proceedings, including the existence of the arbitration and any awards, shall remain strictly confidential in perpetuity."

[MSA § 14.2, sentence 7.]

#### Institutional rule from which it deviates

**Rules art. 20.1** imposes a general confidentiality obligation over the proceedings, submissions, evidence, awards and orders.

**Rules art. 20.2** then carves out five categories in which disclosure is permitted:

> "(a) to the extent required by applicable law, regulation, or legal process, including securities regulations, tax reporting obligations, and regulatory filings;
> (b) to the extent necessary to protect or pursue a legal right in bona fide legal proceedings before a court or other judicial or regulatory authority;
> (c) to the extent necessary for the enforcement or challenge of any award rendered in the arbitration;
> (d) with the written consent of all parties to the arbitration; and
> (e) to professional advisors of a party who are bound by professional duties of confidentiality."

**Rules art. 20.3:** confidentiality survives termination "for a period of five (5) years from the date of the final award," after which the obligations under the Rules cease, "**without prejudice to any confidentiality obligations arising under the parties' underlying agreement or applicable law**."

**Rules art. 20.4:** the Tribunal may issue protective orders, "including orders restricting access to certain confidential information to named individuals."

#### Nature of the deviation

Two distinct divergences, with very different consequences.

**(a) Duration: perpetual v. five years — a permitted enhancement, not a conflict.** Article 20.3 expressly preserves confidentiality obligations "arising under the parties' underlying agreement," so a perpetual contractual obligation coexists with the Rules without conflict. In principle this is neutral-to-favourable for Greenleaf, whose clinical trial data and platform vulnerabilities are as sensitive as Kuiper's.

**(b) Absence of carve-outs — the real problem.** The clause contains **none** of the five art. 20.2 exceptions. Read in isolation and literally, it would prohibit Greenleaf from: disclosing the arbitration in a regulatory filing; disclosing the underlying data-integrity events to the FDA; instructing a forensic expert; enforcing or resisting enforcement of the award in any court; or telling its own auditors. That construction is untenable, but Kuiper is advancing something very close to it.

#### Legal consequence

**(a) Kuiper's construction is expansive, strategically motivated, and largely wrong.** The October 28 email asserts that the perpetual obligation "extends to all information relating to the dispute, including the fact that a dispute exists, all correspondence between the parties concerning the dispute, the content of Kuiper's October 3, 2024 response, and any future arbitration filings, submissions, hearings, and awards," and that Greenleaf must ensure that neither it "nor any of its advisors, consultants, or third-party service providers — **including any forensic experts** Greenleaf may have retained — disclose any information regarding the dispute" [Vogel Email, Confidentiality]. The assignment memorandum records Kuiper's interpretation as "restricting Greenleaf's ability to disclose information to third parties, **including regulatory authorities**" [Memo § 6(c)].

That position fails on at least four grounds:

1. **It cannot override mandatory regulatory disclosure.** Greenleaf is a biotechnology company whose clinical trial databases were corrupted and which is undertaking "regulatory re-submissions to the U.S. Food and Drug Administration" [Memo § 3]. Data-integrity obligations to the FDA are statutory and non-waivable. Rules art. 20.2(a) expressly permits disclosure "required by applicable law, regulation, or legal process, including securities regulations, tax reporting obligations, and regulatory filings." A private contract cannot lawfully be construed to require a party to withhold from a regulator what the law requires it to disclose, and a New York court would not enforce it to that effect on public-policy grounds. **Greenleaf should not curtail any regulatory disclosure on the strength of Kuiper's letter.**
2. **It would prevent Greenleaf from enforcing its own award.** Rules art. 20.2(c) permits disclosure necessary "for the enforcement or challenge of any award." A confidentiality clause construed to bar the award creditor from producing the award to an enforcing court would defeat the object of the arbitration agreement itself — an absurd result no tribunal or court would adopt.
3. **It cannot bar disclosure to professional advisors and experts.** Rules art. 20.2(e) permits disclosure "to professional advisors of a party who are bound by professional duties of confidentiality," and art. 20.2(b) permits disclosure necessary "to protect or pursue a legal right in bona fide legal proceedings." Palladian Forensic Technologies LLC was retained precisely to analyse the events underlying the claim [Memo § 2.3]. Instructing a forensic expert is a paradigm case of pursuing a legal right; the suggestion that Greenleaf may not brief its own expert is untenable. The precaution Greenleaf must take is to ensure Palladian is under a written confidentiality undertaking — which brings Palladian within art. 20.2(e) beyond argument.
4. **It reaches back beyond the arbitration.** The clause is confined to "arbitration proceedings, including the existence of the arbitration and any awards." Kuiper seeks to extend it to pre-arbitration correspondence, the September 12 notice of breach, and its own October 3 response — none of which are "arbitration proceedings." There may be a separate confidentiality obligation elsewhere in the MSA, but Sections 1–11 are not in the record and Kuiper does not cite one; on § 14.2 alone the assertion overreaches.

**(b) Nonetheless, the risk is not zero and must be managed.** A confidentiality breach would give Kuiper a collateral claim and a distraction it would happily prosecute; and the absence of express carve-outs means every disclosure requires a reasoned justification rather than pointing at a contractual permission. Kuiper's letter is best read as an attempt to chill Greenleaf's communications with the FDA, its investors and its expert, and to create a record for a later breach allegation. It should be answered, not ignored — silence risks being characterised as acquiescence in Kuiper's construction.

**(c) The "in perpetuity" term is unusual and its outer limits are untested.** Perpetual confidentiality obligations of unlimited duration are enforceable in principle between commercial parties under New York law where the subject matter is genuinely confidential, but a perpetual obligation with no exceptions at all is the kind of term a court may read down. Greenleaf need not attack it — it benefits from confidentiality too — but should not assume it means what Kuiper says it means.

#### Severity rationale

**High.** The clause/rule divergence alone would be Moderate. It is High because Kuiper has affirmatively asserted a construction that, if accepted or acquiesced in, would interfere with Greenleaf's regulatory obligations to the FDA and its instruction of the expert on whom its causation case depends.

#### Recommended fix

1. **Send a reasoned reservation-of-rights letter to Kuiper promptly.** Reject the expansive construction; state that Greenleaf will comply with the confidentiality obligation as properly construed, and that it construes § 14.2 as subject to disclosures (i) required by law, regulation or legal process; (ii) necessary to pursue or protect legal rights; (iii) necessary to enforce or challenge an award; (iv) to professional advisors and experts bound by confidentiality; and (v) with written consent — i.e., the Rules art. 20.2 categories the parties incorporated by agreeing to arbitrate under the Rules. Do not invite a negotiation; state a position. Send it by both email and courier per § 15.3.
2. **Paper Palladian's engagement.** Confirm in writing that Palladian and its personnel are bound by confidentiality obligations coextensive with the MSA and the Rules. This converts an argument into a fact under art. 20.2(e). Do this before the next substantive exchange with Palladian.
3. **Continue all mandated regulatory disclosures without modification**, and keep a contemporaneous file recording, for each disclosure, the legal obligation compelling it. This is the evidentiary answer to any later breach allegation.
4. **Propose a stipulated confidentiality protocol, and if refused seek an art. 20.4 order.** Rules art. 20.4 empowers the Tribunal to make protective orders including "orders restricting access to certain confidential information to named individuals." A protocol expressly recording the permitted-disclosure categories converts a contested construction into a tribunal-sanctioned framework and removes the issue from the case.
5. **Assess whether the arbitration is disclosable by Greenleaf independently of the clause** — auditor enquiries, contingency reporting, and any investor or lender covenants. Where disclosure is required, art. 20.2(a) covers it; where it is discretionary, weigh it against the perpetual obligation.
6. **Do not seek to invalidate the perpetual term.** Greenleaf's clinical trial data and the fact of a platform failure affecting FDA submissions are at least as sensitive to Greenleaf as to Kuiper. The objective is to establish the carve-outs, not to destroy the obligation.

### ISSUE 6 — "Discovery Shall Be Limited to Document Production Only": Scope Ambiguity Threatening Witness and Expert Evidence

**Severity: HIGH**

#### Clause wording

> "Discovery shall be limited to document production only, and each party shall bear its own costs regardless of the outcome."

[MSA § 14.2, sentence 8. The costs limb is addressed separately at Issue 10.]

#### Institutional rule from which it deviates

**Rules art. 14.3:** "The Tribunal may order any party to produce documents or other evidence that it considers relevant to the case, subject to any applicable legal privilege."

**Rules art. 14.5:** "Any party may submit witness statements and expert reports with its written submissions."

**Rules art. 16.1:** "The Tribunal may decide the dispute on the basis of documents alone or may hold oral hearings . . . . **The Tribunal shall hold a hearing if requested by any party, unless the parties have agreed to a documents-only arbitration.**"

**Rules art. 17.1:** "Any party may present the testimony of witnesses, including expert witnesses."

**Rules art. 17.4:** "Each party may submit written witness statements and expert reports in accordance with the procedural timetable established by the Tribunal."

**Rules art. 18.1–18.2:** the Tribunal has broad discretion over procedure "provided that the parties are treated with equality and that each party is given a reasonable opportunity to present its case," and may "take evidence in such manner as it sees fit, including through witness testimony, expert evidence, site inspections, and any other means."

#### Nature of the deviation

**On the narrow reading, there is barely a deviation at all.** International arbitration under institutional rules does not provide for U.S.-style discovery in the first place. The SAI Rules contain no depositions, no interrogatories, and no requests for admission; document production under arts. 14.3 and 18.2 is discretionary and tribunal-controlled. On that reading, "discovery shall be limited to document production only" merely restates SAI practice and forecloses an argument Greenleaf was never going to win — and, incidentally, preserves the *one* disclosure tool Greenleaf actually needs, since its case depends on obtaining Kuiper's internal server logs and incident records [Memo §§ 2.3, 5].

**The risk lies in the broad reading.** "Discovery" is a term of art in U.S. litigation practice that is not confined to documents; in common U.S. usage it encompasses depositions, interrogatories, requests for admission, and also expert disclosures. A respondent motivated to narrow the evidentiary record could argue that the parties, by limiting "discovery" to "document production only," agreed that:

- no witness statements or expert reports may be submitted (contra arts. 14.5, 17.4);
- no witness or expert may be examined orally (contra arts. 17.1, 17.2, 17.3); and — most aggressively —
- the parties have "agreed to a documents-only arbitration" within the meaning of the exception in art. 16.1, so that **no oral hearing need be held even if Greenleaf requests one**.

That last construction is a stretch: "discovery" and "hearing" are different things, and the clause elsewhere plainly contemplates a contested proceeding. But the argument is available, and its consequences would be severe.

#### Legal consequence

**(a) Greenleaf's case is the one that depends on expert evidence.** Greenleaf bears the burden of proving that the data corruption originated on Kuiper's servers rather than in Greenleaf's API configurations — the precise issue joined by Kuiper's October 3 response [Memo § 2.3]. Its proof is Palladian's forensic analysis. Rules art. 14.2 places the burden on Greenleaf ("Each party shall have the burden of proving the facts relied on to support its claim or defence"). **A construction that excluded expert reports, or excluded the ability to put Palladian's expert forward for examination and to cross-examine Kuiper's answering expert, would be catastrophic to the claim and would fall almost entirely on Greenleaf.** Kuiper's defence, by contrast, is largely a documentary one about API configuration specifications.

**(b) The broad reading would itself be vulnerable, which is Greenleaf's protection.** A tribunal that refused to admit expert evidence or to hold a requested hearing in a $13.3 million forensic causation dispute would be exposed on Rules art. 18.1 ("reasonable opportunity to present its case") and, at the Singapore seat, on Model Law art. 18 and the natural-justice ground for setting aside (*Soh Beng Tee* [2007] SGCA 28). Rules art. 33.1 also directs the Tribunal to act so as to ensure the award is enforceable. A tribunal is therefore unlikely to adopt the broad reading. But "unlikely" is not a basis on which to build a case strategy, and the argument's mere availability gives Kuiper leverage over the procedural timetable.

**(c) The loss of depositions is real but modest.** Greenleaf loses the ability to depose Kuiper's technical personnel. Those individuals are in Munich and outside U.S. subpoena power in any event, and international arbitration would not ordinarily have permitted their deposition. The practical loss is limited to pre-hearing testing of the witnesses, which is partly recovered through witness statements under art. 17.4 and cross-examination at the hearing under art. 17.2.

**(d) The § 1782 route is very likely closed — correcting an assumption in the assignment memorandum.** The memorandum contemplates court-ordered evidence-gathering "potentially, [from] a U.S. federal court under 28 U.S.C. § 1782" [Memo § 5]. In *ZF Automotive US, Inc. v. Luxshare, Ltd.*, 596 U.S. 619 (2022), the Supreme Court held that § 1782's phrase "foreign or international tribunal" reaches only governmental or intergovernmental adjudicative bodies, and does not extend to private commercial arbitral panels. An SAI tribunal constituted under a private contract is a private commercial panel. **Greenleaf should not plan on § 1782.** A separate point: even if § 1782 were available, Kuiper would argue that resort to it circumvents the parties' agreed limitation on discovery — an argument that has traction in the § 1782 discretionary analysis.

**(e) The clause supplies no production standard.** It says document production is permitted but says nothing about how requests are made or what relevance/materiality threshold applies. Rules arts. 14.3 and 18.2 leave it to the Tribunal's discretion. This is a **gap Greenleaf should fill deliberately**, because Greenleaf is the party that needs production.

#### Severity rationale

**High.** The narrow construction is far more likely and would leave Greenleaf substantially unharmed. The rating reflects the magnitude of the downside if the broad construction gains any traction: it would remove the evidence on which Greenleaf's entire causation case rests, in a dispute where the burden is Greenleaf's.

#### Recommended fix

1. **Foreclose the broad reading on the record at the first opportunity.** In the Request (Rules art. 4.2(e) observations) and again at the case management conference (art. 13.3(c), which expressly contemplates discussion of "the anticipated need for document production, witness testimony, and expert evidence"), state Greenleaf's construction: that "discovery" in § 14.2 refers to pre-hearing disclosure inter partes and limits it to documents, and does not affect the parties' rights under arts. 14.5, 16.1, 17.1 and 17.4 to adduce witness and expert evidence and to be heard orally.
2. **Request an oral hearing expressly and in writing at the earliest stage.** Rules art. 16.1 makes a hearing mandatory on request unless the parties have agreed to a documents-only arbitration. Make the request before the point can be characterised as agreed away, and repeat it in the first procedural exchange.
3. **Seek a procedural order recording the evidentiary framework.** Ask the Tribunal, in Procedural Order No. 1 or the Terms of Reference (art. 13.1), to record that the parties may submit witness statements and expert reports and that witnesses and experts may be examined at a hearing. If Kuiper agrees, the issue is closed; if it objects, the objection is surfaced early rather than at the hearing.
4. **Propose the IBA Rules on the Taking of Evidence in International Arbitration as guidance, together with a Redfern Schedule.** This fills the production-standard gap on terms that are familiar, neutral, and favourable to a claimant seeking targeted categories of an opponent's internal records. Rules art. 33.2 permits procedural questions not addressed by the Rules to be resolved by the Tribunal, subject to the parties' agreement.
5. **Frame document requests early, tightly, and by category.** Identify now the categories Greenleaf needs — server logs for the June–August 2024 period, incident and outage reports, internal engineering communications, change-control and deployment records, monitoring alerts, retention-policy documentation, and any litigation-hold instructions. Tight, well-founded requests survive relevance objections; broad ones invite the response that Greenleaf is seeking the U.S.-style discovery the clause forecloses.
6. **Treat preservation as the priority, not production.** Production is worthless if the logs are deleted first. See Issue 15 and Part IX on the emergency arbitrator application.
7. **Remove § 1782 from the strategy.** If a U.S. evidentiary route is still wanted, it must rest on some basis other than § 1782, and counsel should assume none is available.

#### Record citations

MSA § 14.2 (sentence 8); Rules arts. 4.2(e), 13.1, 13.3(c), 14.2, 14.3, 14.5, 16.1, 17.1–17.4, 18.1, 18.2, 33.1, 33.2; Memo §§ 2.3, 4(i), 5. Authorities: *ZF Automotive US, Inc. v. Luxshare, Ltd.*, 596 U.S. 619 (2022); *Soh Beng Tee & Co Pte Ltd v Fairmount Development Pte Ltd* [2007] SGCA 28.

### ISSUE 7 — Sole Arbitrator Notwithstanding an Amount in Controversy of Approximately CHF 11.9 Million

**Severity: MODERATE**

#### Clause wording

> "The arbitration shall be conducted by a sole arbitrator mutually agreed upon by the parties . . . ."

[MSA § 14.2, sentence 2.]

#### Institutional rule from which it deviates

**Rules art. 6.2:** "Where the parties have agreed on the number of arbitrators, such agreement shall be given effect, **subject to Article 6.5**."

**Rules art. 6.3(a):** where the parties have *not* agreed, "For disputes where the amount in controversy exceeds CHF 5,000,000 . . . the default shall be a tribunal of three arbitrators, unless the SAI Court determines . . . that a sole arbitrator is appropriate."

**Rules art. 6.5:** "**Notwithstanding any agreement of the parties** regarding the number of arbitrators, the SAI Court may, **in exceptional circumstances and upon the reasoned request of a party**, determine that a different number of arbitrators is appropriate, having regard to the complexity of the dispute, the amount in controversy, and the interests of the parties."

**Rules art. 5.3:** "Any counterclaim shall be treated as if it were a separate claim for purposes of determining the amount in controversy under these Rules, **including for purposes of Article 6** (number of arbitrators) and Appendix I (Schedule of Fees)."

#### Nature of the deviation

Strictly, there is **no deviation**: art. 6.3 supplies a default that applies only "[w]here the parties have not agreed," and here they have. Article 6.2 requires the agreement to be given effect. The issue is the **residual override in art. 6.5**, and the fact that the quantum places this case well inside the band where the Rules' own default would be a three-member tribunal.

The amount in controversy is $13.3 million: Greenleaf's $11.9 million claim plus Kuiper's $1.4 million counterclaim, which art. 5.3 requires to be counted separately and expressly "for purposes of Article 6" [Memo § 3]. Using the conversion implied by the assignment memorandum (CHF 5 million ≈ $5.6 million), $13.3 million is approximately **CHF 11.9 million** — roughly 2.4 times the art. 6.3(a) threshold, and squarely within the CHF 5,000,001–20,000,000 band of Appendix I.

*A drafting observation:* the Rules express their threshold in Swiss francs while the claims are denominated in U.S. dollars, and neither the Rules nor the clause specifies a conversion rate or date. The point is unlikely to be outcome-determinative here — the amount exceeds the threshold on any plausible rate — but it should be addressed in the Request to avoid an argument later.

#### Legal consequence

**(a) Kuiper can make an art. 6.5 application, but its prospects are modest.** Article 6.5 requires *both* "exceptional circumstances" *and* a "reasoned request of a party." Amount in controversy is one of the three listed factors, so Kuiper has something to work with, particularly if it emphasises the technical complexity of the forensic issues and the bilingual record. But art. 6.2 makes party agreement the starting point, and the SAI Court will be slow to override an express bargain on a factor (quantum) the parties can be taken to have had in view when they contracted for a $14.5 million engagement [MSA, Recitals]. An SAI Court that overrode clear party agreement whenever the quantum exceeded the default threshold would drain art. 6.2 of content.

**(b) The exposure is delay and cost, not invalidity.** If Kuiper applies, resolution will take weeks; if it succeeds, the three-nomination process under art. 6.6 (15 days each for party nominations, then 15 days for the presiding arbitrator) adds a further 45+ days.

**(c) Greenleaf should want the sole arbitrator.** This is the key strategic point and it inverts the framing in the assignment memorandum. Under Appendix I:

| | Sole arbitrator | Three-member tribunal |
|---|---|---|
| Indicative arbitrator fees (CHF 5,000,001–20,000,000) | **CHF 50,000 – 200,000** | **CHF 120,000 – 500,000** |

The delta is up to **CHF 300,000**. Because the clause makes costs irrecoverable regardless of outcome (Issue 10) and requires advances in equal shares (art. 26.4), that delta translates into up to roughly CHF 150,000 of additional irrecoverable cost to Greenleaf. A sole arbitrator is also materially faster, which matters given Issue 1 and the evidence-preservation timeline. **Greenleaf's interest is in defending the sole-arbitrator designation, not curing it.**

**(d) A three-member tribunal would make the 90-day deadline still less achievable.** Three arbitrators must coordinate diaries for hearings and deliberations and produce a majority-signed reasoned award (arts. 25.1, 25.2). If Kuiper applies under art. 6.5, Greenleaf should point out that granting the application would compound the art. 1.4(c) impracticability problem created by the 90-day term.

#### Severity rationale

**Moderate.** No enforceability risk and no conflict with the Rules. It is a contingent risk of delay and materially increased irrecoverable cost, triggered only if Kuiper applies.

#### Recommended fix

1. **Affirm the sole-arbitrator designation in the Request.** Rules art. 4.2(e) invites observations on the number of arbitrators; art. 4.2(f) requires, where the agreement provides for a sole arbitrator, "the name and contact details of any proposed arbitrator or a proposal for the method of appointment." Use both to lock in the position at the outset.
2. **Prepare the art. 6.5 opposition now.** The answer is: the parties expressly agreed; art. 6.2 requires that agreement to be given effect; art. 6.5 requires exceptional circumstances which quantum alone does not supply; a sole arbitrator is consistent with the parties' evident intent to have a fast, economical procedure (as the 90-day term and the costs provision both show); and a three-member tribunal would make the parties' own 90-day deadline unachievable.
3. **Do not raise art. 6.5 on Greenleaf's own behalf.** It cuts against Greenleaf on cost, speed, and consistency of position.
4. **Address the CHF/USD conversion in the Request.** State the amount in controversy in USD and CHF with the conversion rate and date used, so the fee band and any art. 6 analysis proceed on Greenleaf's stated basis.
5. **Note the interaction with arbitrator selection.** A sole arbitrator means one appointment decision carries the entire case. Invest in the candidate diligence accordingly (Issues 8 and 14).

#### Record citations

MSA § 14.2 (sentence 2), Recitals (Contract Value); Rules arts. 4.2(e)–(f), 5.3, 6.2, 6.3(a), 6.5, 6.6, 25.1, 25.2, 26.4, App. I (Administrative Charges; Arbitrator Fees); Memo §§ 3, 4(a).

### ISSUE 8 — The 15-Calendar-Day Window to Agree the Sole Arbitrator

**Severity: MODERATE**

#### Clause wording

> "The arbitration shall be conducted by a sole arbitrator mutually agreed upon by the parties **within 15 calendar days of the filing of the Request for Arbitration**."

[MSA § 14.2, sentence 2.]

#### Institutional rule from which it deviates

**Rules art. 6.4:** "Where the parties have agreed on a sole arbitrator, the parties shall endeavour to agree on the identity of the sole arbitrator **within 30 calendar days of the filing of the Request for Arbitration**. If the parties fail to agree within that period, the SAI Court shall appoint the sole arbitrator."

Related: art. 4.1 (commencement on receipt of the Request by the Secretariat); art. 4.3 (the Secretariat sends the Request to the Respondent "upon receipt of the filing fee"); art. 3.3–3.4 (communications deemed received on delivery; time periods calculated from the day following receipt).

#### Nature of the deviation

A direct numerical conflict: 15 days against 30. The clause halves the period.

Three structural defects compound it.

**(a) The clause is silent on the consequence of failure.** It states a deadline but supplies no fallback. Rules art. 6.4 fills the gap — but art. 6.4's fallback is expressly triggered by failure to agree "**within that period**," meaning the *30-day* period. On the Rules' own terms, the SAI Court's default appointment power does not arise until day 30. **The clause therefore creates a 15-day deadline that no institutional mechanism enforces.**

**(b) The trigger is misaligned with the Rules' service mechanics.** The 15 days run from "the filing of the Request." Under art. 4.3, the Secretariat transmits the Request to the Respondent only "upon receipt of the filing fee." Even where the fee accompanies the Request under art. 4.2(g), transmission and receipt consume days, and art. 3.4 calculates time periods "from the day following the date of receipt." A meaningful fraction of a 15-day window can therefore elapse before the Respondent has the Request in hand. A 30-day window absorbs that friction; a 15-day window does not.

**(c) It collides with the arbitrator profile the case requires.** As discussed at Issues 4 and 14, the candidate must be bilingual in English and German, competent in cloud infrastructure and life-sciences regulation, independent, available, and willing to accept a contractual 90-day award obligation. Fifteen days is not enough for conflict checks, availability confirmation, and party diligence on such a candidate.

#### Legal consequence

**(a) The 15-day term is practically inert as against the institution.** The only body that can appoint on default is the SAI Court, and its power under art. 6.4 is keyed to the 30-day period. If Greenleaf applies to the SAI Court on day 16, the likely outcome is that the Court declines as premature. The clause's contractual force is therefore reduced to a promise between the parties, breach of which sounds in nothing useful.

**(b) Kuiper has pre-announced its objection.** The October 28 email states that Kuiper "will not consider itself bound by the 15-day contractual deadline and will instead rely on the full 30-day period provided under Article 6 of the SAI Rules," and that "[s]hould Greenleaf attempt to invoke the SAI Court's appointment authority before the expiry of that 30-day period, Kuiper will object" [Vogel Email, Arbitrator Appointment Timeline]. Kuiper's reasoning — that identifying a suitably qualified, available, conflict-free arbitrator "cannot be compressed into 15 days" — is, on the merits, correct.

**(c) There is a theoretical composition-irregularity argument, but it is not one Greenleaf will run.** An appointment made at day 30 under the Rules rather than day 15 under the clause could in principle be said not to accord with "the agreement of the parties" for Model Law art. 34(2)(a)(iv) purposes. Only Greenleaf could complain, and Greenleaf will not. The risk is negligible. The converse — Greenleaf forcing a day-16 appointment over Kuiper's objection — is the scenario to avoid, because it would hand Kuiper a genuine composition complaint.

**(d) Appointment delay does not itself worsen Issue 1.** The 90-day award clock runs from *constitution* (§ 14.2, sentence 5; Rules art. 8.1), so a later appointment postpones the start rather than eating the window. Delay does, however, cost time against the log-deletion timeline — which is why the emergency arbitrator application must not wait on appointment of the merits tribunal.

#### Severity rationale

**Moderate.** It creates friction, an announced dispute, and a false expectation about the timetable, but the Rules supply a clean fallback at day 30 and the practical consequence is a delay of about two weeks. It is rated Moderate rather than High because nothing turns on it that cannot be managed by planning around the 30-day period.

#### Recommended fix

1. **Plan to the 30-day period, not the 15-day period.** Build the case timetable on the assumption that the arbitrator will be agreed or appointed at or shortly after day 30. Do not represent to the client or the SAI that a day-15 appointment is achievable.
2. **Start the arbitrator search before filing.** Nothing requires Greenleaf to wait for the Request to begin diligence. Identify three to five candidates now, run conflict pre-checks, and confirm availability and willingness to accept the 90-day mandate. Rules art. 4.2(f) then permits Greenleaf to name a proposed arbitrator *in the Request itself*, which effectively starts the negotiation on day 0 and makes a 15-day agreement at least conceivable.
3. **Write to Kuiper before filing proposing a joint selection process.** Exchange candidate lists with CVs and disclosures on a defined timetable. Kuiper's own email complains that the 15-day window leaves no room for "reasonable due diligence" [Vogel Email]; a pre-filing process removes that complaint and may produce agreement quickly.
4. **Do not apply to the SAI Court before day 30 unless Kuiper has plainly refused to engage.** A premature application will likely fail, will consume goodwill with the institution at the outset, and risks giving Kuiper a composition objection. If Kuiper stonewalls, document the stonewalling and apply on day 31.
5. **Reserve rights on the 15-day term without insisting on it.** Record in correspondence that Greenleaf does not waive § 14.2's timetable, while proceeding pragmatically to the 30-day period.

#### Record citations

MSA § 14.2 (sentence 2); Rules arts. 3.3, 3.4, 4.1, 4.2(f)–(g), 4.3, 6.4, 8.1; Memo §§ 4(b), 6(b); Vogel Email (Arbitrator Appointment Timeline).

### ISSUE 9 — Temporal Lock to "the SAI Arbitration Rules in Effect at the Time of the Signing of This Agreement"

**Severity: MODERATE**

#### Clause wording

> "[S]hall be finally resolved by arbitration administered by the Stonebridge Arbitral Institute in accordance with the **SAI Arbitration Rules in effect at the time of the signing of this Agreement**."

[MSA § 14.2, sentence 1. The Agreement was signed March 15, 2023 — MSA, Recitals and signature block.]

#### Institutional rule from which it deviates

**Rules, Preamble ¶ 3:** "Where the parties' arbitration agreement references the SAI Arbitration Rules **without specifying an edition**, the Rules in effect at the time the Request for Arbitration is filed shall apply. Where the parties' arbitration agreement **references a specific edition** of the Rules, the SAI shall administer the arbitration under that edition, **subject to any modifications the SAI Court deems necessary for proper administration**."

**Rules art. 35.1:** "These Rules shall apply to all arbitrations in which the Request for Arbitration is filed on or after 1 January 2022."

**Rules art. 35.3:** "Where a party's arbitration agreement references a specific prior edition of the SAI Rules, the SAI shall administer the arbitration under the referenced edition **to the extent practicable**. The SAI Court may, **in its discretion, apply provisions of the current edition that are procedural or administrative in nature**, provided that such application does not prejudice the rights of either party."

#### Nature of the deviation

The intended effect is to freeze the applicable rules at the 2022 Edition (effective January 1, 2022), which were the rules in force on March 15, 2023. The assignment memorandum records that the SAI published a **2024 Edition effective July 1, 2024** [Memo § 4]. The memorandum is right that the temporal lock is atypical: most clauses refer to the rules in force at the time of arbitration, or omit a temporal anchor.

The deviation is that the Rules' own default runs the other way. Under Preamble ¶ 3 the *baseline* is the edition in force when the Request is filed; a frozen edition applies only where the agreement "references a specific edition."

**And that is the difficulty: the clause arguably does not "specify an edition" at all.** It identifies the applicable rules by a *date referent* — "in effect at the time of the signing of this Agreement" — rather than by naming the 2022 Edition. A literal reading of Preamble ¶ 3 admits the argument that, absent an edition being specified, the fallback applies and the 2024 Edition governs. The better view is that a date referent that unambiguously identifies one edition *is* a reference to a specific edition, and that is how the assignment memorandum reads it [Memo § 4]. But the point is genuinely arguable, and it is the sort of threshold argument a respondent raises to create friction.

#### Legal consequence

**(a) Whichever edition applies, the SAI Court retains a discretion to import current-edition provisions.** Preamble ¶ 3 subjects administration under an earlier edition to "any modifications the SAI Court deems necessary for proper administration," and art. 35.3 permits the SAI Court to "apply provisions of the current edition that are **procedural or administrative in nature**," subject to no prejudice. **The temporal lock is therefore porous by design.** In particular, Appendix I (Schedule of Fees) is quintessentially administrative; Greenleaf should expect current fee tables to be applied regardless of which edition governs the procedure.

**(b) The content of the 2024 Edition cannot be assessed on this record.** The 2024 Edition is not among the documents provided. It is not possible to state whether it materially changed the provisions that matter here — the art. 6.3 quantum threshold, the art. 24 award timetable, the art. 20 confidentiality carve-outs, the art. 15bis emergency arbitrator procedure, or the Appendix I fees. **This is a live evidentiary gap and it should be closed before filing** (see Part X). It is not appropriate to speculate about the direction of any change.

**(c) The emergency arbitrator procedure is unaffected.** Article 15bis and Appendix II are present in the 2022 Edition. Whichever edition applies, the emergency arbitrator mechanism is available. See Issue 15.

**(d) Practical administration risk is low but non-zero.** Administering under a superseded edition is routine for institutions and art. 35.3 expressly contemplates it. The realistic exposure is a short administrative exchange at filing, plus the possibility of the SAI Court applying current-edition procedural provisions under art. 35.3.

#### Severity rationale

**Moderate.** It creates an arguable threshold question and a discretionary institutional overlay that neither party can fully control, and it cannot be finally assessed without the 2024 Edition. It does not affect the validity of the agreement to arbitrate.

#### Recommended fix

1. **Obtain the SAI Arbitration Rules, 2024 Edition, and its transitional provisions immediately**, and re-run the comparison in this report against it. Until that is done, the assessment of Issue 9 is provisional.
2. **State Greenleaf's position expressly in the Request.** Plead that § 14.2 references a specific edition — the 2022 Edition — by unambiguous date referent, and that the arbitration falls to be administered under it per Preamble ¶ 3 and art. 35.3. Attach the 2022 Edition. Taking the position first is worth more than the argument's abstract merits.
3. **Confirm the applicable edition with the SAI Secretariat before filing.** A short pre-filing enquiry avoids a post-filing dispute and, if the Secretariat's view differs, allows Greenleaf to re-plan rather than react.
4. **Assume current-edition fees apply.** Budget on the current Appendix I equivalents rather than the 2022 tables.
5. **If the 2024 Edition is materially more favourable to Greenleaf on any point, consider proposing a bilateral agreement to adopt it.** Rules art. 1.2 permits party modification; art. 35.2 contemplates party agreement on the applicable edition. This would be a package to negotiate alongside the Issue 1 variation.

#### Record citations

### ISSUE 10 — "Each Party Shall Bear Its Own Costs Regardless of the Outcome"

**Severity: MODERATE**

#### Clause wording

> "Discovery shall be limited to document production only, and **each party shall bear its own costs regardless of the outcome**."

[MSA § 14.2, sentence 8.]

#### Institutional rule from which it deviates

**Rules art. 26.1:** the costs of the arbitration comprise "(a) the fees and expenses of the arbitrator(s); (b) the administrative charges of the SAI; (c) the fees and expenses of any expert appointed by the Tribunal; and **(d) reasonable legal and other costs incurred by the parties**."

**Rules art. 26.3:** "The Tribunal shall have the authority to allocate the costs of the arbitration between the parties, taking into account . . . the outcome of the arbitration, the conduct of the parties, the complexity of the issues, and any other relevant factor. **Agreements between the parties regarding allocation of costs shall be given effect unless the Tribunal determines that such allocation would be manifestly unreasonable in the circumstances of the case.**"

**Rules art. 25.4:** "The award **shall** fix the costs of the arbitration and decide which of the parties shall bear them or in what proportion . . . in accordance with Article 26."

**Rules arts. 26.4, 27.1:** advance payments in equal shares by Claimant and Respondent unless the SAI Court directs otherwise.

**Appendix II, EA-6:** "The emergency arbitrator **may allocate the costs of the emergency arbitrator procedure**, including the emergency arbitrator fee and reasonable legal costs, between the parties."

#### Nature of the deviation

The clause displaces the ordinary costs-follow-the-event discretion in art. 26.3. Rules art. 26.3 expressly accommodates it: party agreements on allocation "shall be given effect." So this is a permitted modification, subject to one qualification — **the "manifestly unreasonable" override**, which the parties cannot contract out of because it is embedded in the very provision that gives their agreement effect.

Two ambiguities are worth preserving:

**(a) What does "its own costs" cover?** Read narrowly, it means each party's own legal and expert costs — art. 26.1(d) — leaving the *costs of the arbitration* proper (arbitrator fees, SAI administrative charges, tribunal-appointed expert fees, art. 26.1(a)–(c)) to be allocated by the Tribunal under art. 25.4. Read broadly, it fixes everything at a 50/50 split, since arts. 26.4 and 27.1 require equal advances. The narrow reading is textually preferable: arts. 26.1(a)–(c) are institutional costs, not a party's "own" costs. It also sits better with art. 25.4's mandatory language that the award "shall fix the costs of the arbitration and decide which of the parties shall bear them."

**(b) Does it reach the emergency arbitrator procedure?** Appendix II, EA-6 confers a discrete allocation power on the emergency arbitrator over "the costs of the emergency arbitrator procedure, including the emergency arbitrator fee." Since the clause pre-dates and does not mention the EA procedure, there is an argument that EA-6 is unaffected. Greenleaf should preserve it, given that it may pay a **non-refundable CHF 20,000** emergency arbitrator fee (Appendix II, EA-2) [and see Memo § 5].

#### Legal consequence

**(a) Greenleaf forfeits cost recovery on a claim it expects to win.** Greenleaf is the claimant, holds supportive preliminary forensic findings [Memo § 2.3], and faces a counterclaim it regards as answerable. In a costs-follow-the-event regime it would expect substantial recovery. Under the clause it recovers nothing.

**(b) The irrecoverable exposure is material.** On the record and Appendix I:

| Item | Source | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Filing fee (amount in controversy exceeding CHF 5,000,000) | App. I, § I | CHF 5,000 |
| SAI administrative charges (CHF 5,000,001–20,000,000 band) | App. I, § II | CHF 25,000 – 60,000 |
| Sole arbitrator fees (same band) | App. I, § III | CHF 50,000 – 200,000 |
| Emergency arbitrator fee (non-refundable) | App. II, EA-2 | CHF 20,000 |
| **Institutional subtotal** | | **CHF 100,000 – 285,000** |
| Greenleaf's legal fees, Palladian's forensic fees, translation and interpretation (Issue 4) | not in record | substantial, all irrecoverable |

If Kuiper succeeds on an art. 6.5 application for a three-member tribunal (Issue 7), the arbitrator-fee line rises to CHF 120,000–500,000.

**(c) It removes a deterrent and an important lever.** A party facing no costs risk has less reason to be proportionate. It also weakens Greenleaf's settlement position, because the costs Greenleaf sinks are permanently sunk. And it means the *net* recovery figure — not the claim figure — must anchor settlement authority: on the downside case in Issue 2 (consequential damages wholly excluded, counterclaim succeeding in full), Greenleaf's net position before its own irrecoverable costs is roughly $7.3 million, and materially less after them.

**(d) Two arguments remain available.** First, art. 26.3's "manifestly unreasonable" override survives the parties' agreement by its own terms; if Kuiper conducts the arbitration abusively — serial unmeritorious applications, obstruction of document production, spoliation of the server logs — Greenleaf can invite the Tribunal to disapply the agreed allocation for that conduct. Second, security for costs remains available: art. 15.1(d) permits orders for security for costs and art. 15.2 permits security for the costs of interim measures. Neither is displaced by the clause.

**(e) Note the interaction with MSA § 14.4 and the withheld invoices.** Section 14.4 requires the parties to "continue to perform their respective obligations under this Agreement to the extent not affected by the dispute." Greenleaf has withheld $1.4 million in invoices "pending resolution of the service failures" [Memo § 2.3]. Kuiper may argue that the withholding itself breaches § 14.4, strengthening its counterclaim and possibly supporting an interest claim. Since costs are irrecoverable either way, the counterclaim's success falls straight to the bottom line. Greenleaf should form a considered view on the withholding before filing.

#### Severity rationale

**Moderate.** A valid, bargained allocation the Rules expressly honour. It is a significant and quantifiable financial detriment rather than a legal defect, and it is largely unfixable without Kuiper's agreement.

#### Recommended fix

1. **Plead the narrow construction from the outset.** State in the Request that "each party shall bear its own costs" addresses each party's own legal and expert costs under art. 26.1(d), and does not displace the Tribunal's mandatory obligation under art. 25.4 to fix and allocate the costs of the arbitration under art. 26.1(a)–(c). Even a partial recovery of institutional costs is worth having.
2. **Expressly reserve the art. 26.3 "manifestly unreasonable" argument** in the Request and in the first procedural exchange, and log every instance of obstructive conduct by Kuiper contemporaneously for later use.
3. **Preserve the EA-6 allocation.** In any emergency arbitrator application, expressly request allocation of the CHF 20,000 fee and associated legal costs under EA-6, and reserve the argument that the clause does not reach the EA procedure.
4. **Preserve the art. 12.2(d) translation-cost allocation** as a distinct power (Issue 4).
5. **Consider proposing a reciprocal costs variation as part of the Issue 1 package.** Kuiper may value certainty on the award timetable enough to trade a costs-follow-the-event regime. Assess first whether Greenleaf actually wants that: costs-shifting cuts both ways, and Kuiper's $1.4 million counterclaim is a strong one.
6. **Build a full irrecoverable-cost budget and reset settlement authority against net recovery**, not gross claim value.
7. **Consider security for costs under art. 15.1(d)** if Kuiper's financial position warrants, though on the record (annual revenue approximately €480 million [Memo § 2.1]) it likely does not.

### ISSUE 11 — Blanket Waiver of "Any Right to Appeal the Award on Any Grounds"

**Severity: MODERATE**

#### Clause wording

> "The parties waive any right to appeal the award on any grounds."

[MSA § 14.2, sentence 9.]

#### Institutional rule from which it deviates

**Rules art. 29.1:** "Every award shall be binding on the parties. By agreeing to arbitration under these Rules, the parties undertake to carry out any award without delay."

**Rules art. 29.2:** "The parties waive their right to any form of recourse **insofar as such waiver can validly be made under the applicable law**."

**Rules art. 29.3:** "For the avoidance of doubt, Article 29.2 **does not affect any right of a party to seek the setting aside of an award that is available under the law of the seat of the arbitration and that cannot be waived under that law**."

#### Nature of the deviation

The clause purports an unqualified waiver "on any grounds." The Rules' equivalent is deliberately qualified twice over — art. 29.2 by "insofar as such waiver can validly be made," and art. 29.3 by an express carve-out for non-waivable setting-aside rights at the seat. The clause omits both qualifications.

The deviation is therefore one of **overbreadth**, and the overbroad portion is simply ineffective.

#### Legal consequence

**(a) At the Singapore seat, the supervisory jurisdiction cannot be contracted away.** The seat is Singapore [MSA § 14.2], so the supervisory regime is the International Arbitration Act, which gives the UNCITRAL Model Law force of law and supplements it with additional statutory setting-aside grounds. The court's power to set aside under Model Law art. 34 and the IAA's additional grounds is a matter of the supervisory jurisdiction of the courts of the seat; it is not a private right that parties may extinguish by agreement in the arbitration clause. Rules art. 29.3 acknowledges precisely this. Accordingly:

- the waiver is **ineffective** insofar as it purports to exclude setting-aside applications at the seat;
- it is largely **surplusage** insofar as it purports to exclude an appeal on a point of law, because the Singapore international arbitration regime provides no such appeal in the first place (unlike the domestic Arbitration Act regime); and
- it is **ineffective** insofar as it purports to bar the enforcement defences in Article V of the New York Convention, which are available to the award debtor in the enforcing forum as a matter of that forum's law. A U.S. court asked to confirm under 9 U.S.C. §§ 201–208 would apply Article V regardless of what the parties agreed.

**(b) The practical consequence is that Greenleaf gains no protection from the waiver — which matters most for Issue 1.** The most likely challenge to any award in this matter is a Kuiper application under Model Law art. 34(2)(a)(iv) arising from the tribunal's failure to meet the contractual 90-day deadline, or under art. 34(2)(a)(iii) arising from an award of consequential damages notwithstanding § 14.2's authority limitation (Issue 2). **The blanket waiver will not stop either.** This is the single most important practical conclusion of Issue 11: it removes the temptation to treat the waiver as a safety net and reinforces the recommendation at Issue 1 to fix the 90-day term by agreement.

**(c) A modest severability point.** If the waiver is invalid in part, the ordinary approach is to sever the invalid portion and give effect to the remainder — the arbitration agreement itself is unaffected. There is no realistic argument that the overbroad waiver taints the agreement to arbitrate.

**(d) On balance the clause is mildly favourable to Greenleaf.** As anticipated award creditor, Greenleaf wants finality and Kuiper is the party more likely to challenge. The waiver, though largely ineffective, gives Greenleaf a contractual argument — and potentially an estoppel-flavoured one — to deploy against a Kuiper challenge, and supports an application for security or expedited enforcement.

#### Severity rationale

**Moderate.** It is a genuine enforceability defect (a term partly void for attempting to oust a non-waivable supervisory jurisdiction), but its practical bite is limited and it is directionally favourable to Greenleaf. It is rated Moderate rather than Low because of the risk of *reliance*: treating it as effective protection against the Issue 1 annulment risk would be a serious strategic error.

#### Recommended fix

1. **Do not rely on the waiver as protection against annulment.** Fix Issue 1 substantively instead.
2. **Do not seek to amend or clarify the waiver.** Any attempt to renegotiate it invites Kuiper to seek a corresponding narrowing, and the waiver currently favours Greenleaf. Leave it alone.
3. **Deploy it defensively if Kuiper challenges.** In any Kuiper setting-aside application, cite § 14.2 alongside Rules arts. 29.1 and 29.2 and Kuiper's undertaking to carry out any award without delay, as context bearing on the merits and on any application for security pending challenge.
4. **Advise Greenleaf accurately.** Marcus Yuen should understand that the award will be susceptible to a Model Law art. 34 challenge at the seat notwithstanding the waiver, and that the practical protection against challenge is a procedurally impeccable arbitration — not the clause.
5. **Build the record to defeat a future challenge.** Ensure the tribunal's procedural orders record that each party had a reasonable opportunity to present its case, that hearings were held or waived on the record, and that any departure from the parties' agreed procedure was consented to in writing.

#### Record citations

MSA § 14.2 (sentence 9); Rules arts. 29.1, 29.2, 29.3; Memo §§ 2.2, 4(h), 7(3). Authorities: UNCITRAL Model Law arts. 34(2)(a)(iii)–(iv); Singapore International Arbitration Act; New York Convention art. V; 9 U.S.C. §§ 201–208.

### ISSUE 12 — Silence on the Law Governing the Arbitration Agreement Itself

**Severity: MODERATE**

#### Clause wording

The clause is silent. Section 14.1 designates New York law for "[t]his Agreement, and all claims or causes of action . . . that may be based upon, arise out of, or relate to this Agreement," and § 14.1(b) adds:

> "The governing law designated in this Section 14.1 governs the substantive rights and obligations of the Parties under this Agreement. For the avoidance of doubt, **the procedural law governing any arbitration commenced pursuant to Section 14.2 shall be determined by the seat of arbitration designated therein**."

[MSA § 14.1, 14.1(b).] Section 14.2 designates Singapore as the seat.

#### Institutional rule engaged (and the gap)

**Rules art. 11.1** and **art. 23.1** address only the law applicable to the *substance* of the dispute: "The Tribunal shall apply the rules of law designated by the parties as applicable to the substance of the dispute," and any designation of the law of a State refers to its substantive law and not its conflict-of-laws rules. **Neither provision addresses the law governing the arbitration agreement.** The Rules leave the point entirely to the parties, and the parties did not address it.

#### Nature of the deviation

This is a gap, not a conflict — but a consequential one, because the MSA creates a three-way split:

| Question | Governing law | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Substantive rights and obligations under the MSA | New York | MSA § 14.1 |
| Procedural law of the arbitration (*lex arbitri*) and supervisory jurisdiction | Singapore | MSA §§ 14.1(b), 14.2 |
| **Validity, scope, and interpretation of the arbitration agreement itself** | **unspecified** | — |

The third question is not academic. It determines the law by which one assesses: whether the clause's remedial exclusion (Issue 2) validly limits the tribunal's mandate; whether the 90-day deadline (Issue 1) is a mandatory or directory term; whether the appeal waiver (Issue 11) is effective; and the scope of "any dispute, controversy, or claim arising out of or relating to this Agreement."

#### Legal consequence

**(a) The likely answer is New York law, but the analysis is contested and forum-dependent.** Singapore's courts approach the question in three stages — express choice, implied choice, closest and most real connection — and hold that, absent an express choice for the arbitration agreement, the law governing the main contract is a strong indicator of an implied choice for the arbitration agreement, subject to displacement where that would negate the agreement: see *BCY v BCZ* [2016] SGHC 249 and *BNA v BNB* [2019] SGCA 84. The English approach in *Enka Insaat ve Sanayi AS v OOO Insurance Company Chubb* [2020] UKSC 38 is to similar effect. Applying that framework, § 14.1's choice of New York law for the MSA and for "all claims or causes of action . . . that . . . relate to this Agreement" points to **New York law governing the arbitration agreement**, with Singapore supplying the *lex arbitri*.

**(b) But the outcome is not certain, and a differently constituted forum may reach a different result.** Section 14.1(b) was evidently drafted to separate substance from procedure, but it does not name the arbitration agreement, and an argument that the seat's law governs the arbitration agreement (on a validation or closest-connection rationale) is available. That uncertainty is itself the harm: it means the enforceability of several key clause provisions cannot be stated with confidence in advance.

**(c) Practical impact is greatest on Issues 1, 2, and 11.** For each, the analysis turns partly on whether one is construing a New York-law contract term or a Singapore-law arbitration agreement. The reason all three are analysed in this report under both regimes is precisely this gap.

**(d) It also affects separability and any challenge to the clause's validity.** If Kuiper ever attacked the arbitration agreement itself, the applicable law would need to be settled first. Nothing on the record suggests it will — Kuiper's October 28 email proceeds on the footing that the clause is binding and invokes it [Vogel Email] — so this is a contingency rather than a live risk.

#### Severity rationale

**Moderate.** It does not threaten the arbitration and the probable answer (New York law) is favourable and predictable. It is rated Moderate because it introduces analytical uncertainty into the enforceability assessment of the two Critical issues, and because it is the kind of point best settled early and cheaply rather than litigated late.

#### Recommended fix

1. **Take a clear position in the Request.** Plead that, per § 14.1 and consistently with *BCY* and *BNA*, New York law governs the arbitration agreement, while Singapore law is the *lex arbitri* and supplies the supervisory jurisdiction. Establishing the position first shapes the framework.
2. **Seek to record the point in the Terms of Reference.** Rules art. 13.1 permits the Terms of Reference to define "the procedural rules to be followed, and any other matters the Tribunal considers necessary." A recorded common position removes the issue.
3. **Propose an express agreement with Kuiper.** A single sentence in the same side letter that addresses Issue 1 — "the law governing the arbitration agreement in § 14.2 is the law of the State of New York" — costs nothing and is unlikely to be controversial, since Kuiper's positions to date do not depend on the contrary.
4. **Analyse the two Critical issues under both regimes until the point is settled** — as this report does — and do not let the recovery strategy depend on which regime applies.
5. **Instruct Singapore counsel.** Greenleaf will need Singapore counsel in any event for court-ordered interim measures in aid of the arbitration and for any setting-aside proceedings. Add this question to the instruction.

#### Record citations

MSA §§ 14.1, 14.1(a)–(b), 14.2; Rules arts. 11.1, 11.2, 23.1, 23.2; Memo §§ 2.2, 7. Authorities: *BCY v BCZ* [2016] SGHC 249; *BNA v BNB* [2019] SGCA 84; *Enka Insaat ve Sanayi AS v OOO Insurance Company Chubb* [2020] UKSC 38.

### ISSUE 15 — Silence on the Emergency Arbitrator Procedure: No Opt-Out, Mechanism Preserved

**Severity: LOW (FAVOURABLE TO GREENLEAF)**

#### Clause wording

Section 14.2 is silent on emergency and interim relief. Section 14.3(d) addresses it expressly:

> "Notwithstanding the foregoing, nothing in this Section 14.3 shall prevent a Party from seeking emergency or interim relief **pursuant to the arbitration rules referenced in Section 14.2 or from a court of competent jurisdiction** where such relief is necessary to prevent irreparable harm."

[MSA § 14.3(d).]

#### Institutional rule engaged

**Rules art. 15bis.4:** "The emergency arbitrator procedure shall apply to all arbitrations commenced under these Rules **unless**: (a) the parties have agreed to opt out of the emergency arbitrator procedure; or (b) the arbitration agreement was concluded **before the effective date of the edition of the Rules first introducing the emergency arbitrator procedure**."

**Appendix II, EA-7:** "These Emergency Arbitrator Rules apply to all arbitrations under the SAI Rules (2022 Edition) unless the parties have **expressly** opted out in their arbitration agreement."

**Rules art. 15.4:** "A party's right to seek interim measures from a competent judicial authority is not waived by agreeing to arbitration under these Rules. A request to a judicial authority for interim measures . . . shall not be deemed incompatible with the agreement to arbitrate or constitute a waiver of that agreement."

**Rules art. 15.5, 15bis.1–15bis.6, 15bis.2** (appointment within 2 business days); **App. II, EA-2** (fee CHF 20,000, non-refundable), **EA-4** (schedule within 2 business days of appointment), **EA-5** (decision within 15 calendar days of appointment), **EA-6** (costs allocation).

#### Analysis

**The assignment memorandum's provisional conclusion is confirmed: the emergency arbitrator procedure is available to Greenleaf.** [Memo § 5.] The analysis is as follows.

**(a) There is no opt-out.** Appendix II, EA-7 requires an **express** opt-out "in their arbitration agreement." Section 14.2 contains none. Silence is not an opt-out. On the contrary, MSA § 14.3(d) affirmatively preserves the right to seek emergency relief "pursuant to the arbitration rules referenced in Section 14.2" — which is an express *incorporation* of the art. 15bis mechanism, not a waiver of it.

**(b) The art. 15bis.4(b) temporal carve-out cannot apply.** That carve-out disapplies the procedure where "the arbitration agreement was concluded before the effective date of the edition of the Rules first introducing the emergency arbitrator procedure." Article 15bis and Appendix II are contained in the 2022 Edition, which took effect **January 1, 2022**. The MSA was concluded **March 15, 2023** — after that date. The edition that first introduced the procedure therefore cannot have an effective date later than January 1, 2022, and the MSA post-dates it. The carve-out is inapplicable.

**(c) The temporal lock in Issue 9 does not disturb this.** Whether the 2022 or the 2024 Edition governs, the emergency arbitrator procedure exists in the 2022 Edition that the clause selects, and there is no basis on this record to think it was removed thereafter.

**(d) Court-ordered interim relief is preserved in parallel.** Rules art. 15.4 preserves the right to apply to a competent judicial authority and provides that doing so is not incompatible with the arbitration agreement or a waiver of it. MSA § 14.3(d) says the same. The clause contains no prohibition on court-ordered interim relief. **Greenleaf has both routes**, and can pursue them in parallel or in sequence. The natural judicial forum is the Singapore courts as courts of the seat, exercising their statutory power to grant interim measures in aid of arbitration under the International Arbitration Act. Singapore counsel should be instructed.

**(e) But § 1782 is not a third route.** For the reasons at Issue 6(d), *ZF Automotive US, Inc. v. Luxshare, Ltd.*, 596 U.S. 619 (2022) very likely forecloses 28 U.S.C. § 1782 assistance for a private commercial arbitration. This corrects the assumption at Memo § 5.

**(f) The timeline is tight and the deletion window is more advanced than the assignment memorandum suggests.** Palladian estimates automated deletion within 90 to 120 days of the events, and the earliest events occurred in **June 2024** [Memo § 5]:

| Event window | Deletion window (event + 90–120 days) | Status as at Nov. 4, 2024 |
|---|---|---|
| June 2024 | ~ Sept. 8 – Oct. 8, 2024 | **Likely already elapsed** |
| July 2024 | ~ Oct. 8 – Nov. 7, 2024 | **Closing now** |
| August 2024 | ~ Nov. 8 – Dec. 8, 2024 | **Imminent** |

Against that, the emergency arbitrator route takes approximately 17–19 days end to end: appointment within 2 business days of the application and fee (art. 15bis.2; EA-3), procedural schedule within 2 business days of appointment (EA-4), and decision within 15 calendar days of appointment (EA-5).

**(g) The relief sought is squarely within the emergency arbitrator's powers.** Rules art. 15.1(a) expressly contemplates "orders for the preservation of evidence." An emergency arbitrator may not decide the merits (art. 15bis.5), but a preservation order is not a merits decision. The decision binds on issuance and remains in effect until modified by the Tribunal once constituted (art. 15bis.3).

**(h) A practical point often overlooked: the application itself creates the litigation-hold obligation.** Palladian's assessment is that the logs will be deleted "unless a litigation hold is implemented by Kuiper" [Memo § 5]. Serving a preservation demand — and then an emergency application — puts Kuiper on unambiguous notice, so that subsequent automated deletion becomes at least arguable spoliation, supporting an adverse inference before the tribunal and a "conduct of the parties" argument under Rules art. 26.3 (see Issue 10).

#### Severity rationale

**Low, and favourable.** The gap operates entirely in Greenleaf's favour. It is included in the register because the assignment memorandum asked for the conclusion to be confirmed, and because the *timing* consequence — however favourable the legal position — is the most urgent item in this report.

2. **Prepare and file the Appendix II emergency arbitrator application immediately**, and do not sequence it behind the merits Request, the escalation period, or any of the pre-filing negotiations recommended elsewhere in this report. MSA § 14.3(d) expressly permits it during the escalation period.

## VI. CROSS-CUTTING ANALYSIS: THE NEW YORK / SINGAPORE BIFURCATION

The assignment memorandum directs that the bifurcation be considered throughout [Memo §§ 2.2, 7]. The structure is as follows.

### VI.1 The allocation

| Function | Governing regime | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Substantive rights and obligations under the MSA; construction of §§ 12.1, 12.2, 14.2's remedial exclusion; damages characterisation | **New York law** | MSA § 14.1; Rules arts. 11.1, 23.1 |
| *Lex arbitri*; conduct of the arbitration; supervisory jurisdiction; setting aside | **Singapore** (International Arbitration Act; UNCITRAL Model Law) | MSA §§ 14.1(b), 14.2 (seat) |
| Validity/scope/interpretation of the arbitration agreement itself | **Unspecified — probably New York** | Issue 12 |
| Recognition and enforcement of the award | Law of each enforcing forum + New York Convention | New York Convention art. V; 9 U.S.C. §§ 201–208 |
| Institutional administration | SAI Rules, 2022 Edition (subject to Issue 9) | MSA § 14.2 |

Section 14.1 excludes renvoi ("without giving effect to any choice-of-law or conflict-of-law rules"), which aligns with Rules art. 23.1, and excludes the CISG under § 14.1(a) — appropriately, since the MSA is a services agreement.

### VI.2 Where the bifurcation actually bites

**(a) The remedial exclusion (Issue 2) is a New York question at the merits and a Singapore question at the annulment stage.** Whether the exclusion is valid, and whether the $3.2 million is properly characterised as consequential, are New York substantive questions. But because § 14.2 frames the exclusion as a limit on the arbitrator's *authority*, an award that crosses it becomes a Singapore procedural question under Model Law art. 34(2)(a)(iii) — excess of mandate. **The same clause is therefore assessed twice, under two systems, at two stages, with two different tests.** That is the practical content of the bifurcation and it is why Issue 2 is rated Critical.

**(b) The 90-day deadline (Issue 1) is a New York question of construction and a Singapore question of consequence.** Whether the term is mandatory or directory, and whether it can be extended, are questions of contract construction under New York law (subject to Issue 12). Whether breach of it invalidates the award is a Singapore question under Model Law art. 34(2)(a)(iv) and the IAA. New York law is comparatively tolerant of arbitral timetable overruns and disfavours vacatur; Singapore's Model Law regime supplies a textual ground keyed precisely to departure from the parties' agreed procedure. **The seat is the more dangerous jurisdiction for this issue** — a point that would be missed by analysing New York law alone.

**(c) The appeal waiver (Issue 11) is governed by the law of the seat, and Singapore does not permit ouster of its supervisory jurisdiction.** Rules art. 29.3 concedes the point. A New-York-law analysis of the waiver would be beside the mark.

**(d) Confidentiality (Issue 5) is a New York question of construction constrained by U.S. regulatory law.** The FDA disclosure obligations that Kuiper's position collides with are U.S. federal obligations, not Singaporean ones. Singapore has no direct role, save that a tribunal seated there will be applying New York law to construe the clause.

**(e) Interim relief (Issue 15) is where the seat is an asset rather than a risk.** Singapore's courts have well-developed powers to grant interim measures in aid of arbitration and a strongly pro-arbitration posture. Combined with Rules art. 15.4 and MSA § 14.3(d), the Singapore seat gives Greenleaf a credible court-based preservation route alongside the emergency arbitrator.

### VI.3 The net assessment

The bifurcation is not, in itself, defective. New York substantive law with a Singapore seat is a coherent and common structure for a U.S.–EU technology services agreement, and Singapore is a first-rank arbitral seat whose courts support rather than obstruct arbitration. The three genuine costs are: (i) the gap over the law of the arbitration agreement (Issue 12); (ii) the need to run enforceability analysis twice for the two Critical issues; and (iii) the requirement for Singapore counsel in addition to New York counsel, for interim measures and for any post-award proceedings. **None of these is a reason to seek to change the seat, and Greenleaf should not attempt to.** The seat also determines the enforcement architecture favourably: both Singapore and Germany are New York Convention states, and the award will be enforceable against Kuiper in Germany subject to Article V.

- *Kenford Co. v. County of Erie*, 73 N.Y.2d 312 (1989) — consequential damages must be within the parties' contemplation.
- *Rocanova v. Equitable Life Assurance Society*, 83 N.Y.2d 603 (1994); *New York University v. Continental Insurance Co.*, 87 N.Y.2d 308 (1995) — punitive damages unavailable for ordinary breach of contract.

**Singapore**
- *BNA v BNB* [2019] SGCA 84; *BCY v BCZ* [2016] SGHC 249 — proper law of the arbitration agreement.
- *BBA v BAZ* [2020] SGCA 53; *BTN v BTP* [2020] SGCA 105 — admissibility v. jurisdiction; pre-arbitration conditions.
- *Soh Beng Tee & Co Pte Ltd v Fairmount Development Pte Ltd* [2007] SGCA 28 — natural justice and setting aside.
- International Arbitration Act (Singapore), giving force to the UNCITRAL Model Law, including art. 34 setting-aside grounds and the court's power to grant interim measures in aid of arbitration.

**United States (federal) and international**
- *ZF Automotive US, Inc. v. Luxshare, Ltd.*, 596 U.S. 619 (2022) — 28 U.S.C. § 1782 unavailable for private commercial arbitration.
- Convention on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards (New York, 1958), arts. IV(2), V(1)(c), V(1)(d).
- Federal Arbitration Act, 9 U.S.C. §§ 201–208.

**Comparative**
- *Enka Insaat ve Sanayi AS v OOO Insurance Company Chubb* [2020] UKSC 38 — proper law of the arbitration agreement.
