## I. QUESTION PRESENTED AND EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

### A. Question Presented

Whether the Arbitrator Disclosure Statement submitted by Hon. Margaret R. Fairhaven (Ret.) on June 17, 2024 satisfies the disclosure obligations imposed by AAA Commercial Arbitration Rules R-16 and R-17; if not, whether the omissions and inaccuracies identified would support a challenge under Rule R-18 by either party; and what course of action best protects Whitmore Capital Partners LLC's interests, including the enforceability of any resulting award.

### B. Summary of Conclusions

The disclosure statement is materially incomplete. Comparison against the record identifies **ten discrete deficiencies**, comprising both omissions and affirmative inaccuracies. Four of them are, in our assessment, independently sufficient to support a Rule R-18 challenge, and their cumulative effect is substantially greater than the sum of the parts.

The central strategic fact is this: **nine of the ten deficiencies, if disclosed, would have counselled Cascade against accepting this arbitrator, not Whitmore.** Judge Fairhaven was Whitmore's first-ranked candidate and Cascade's third. The undisclosed facts include an indirect financial interest in the very Whitmore fund that owns the beneficiary of the disputed license; shared service at a small nonprofit with Whitmore's own founder; a CLE co-panel appearance with this firm's lead partner on the precise subject matter of this dispute; and seven undisclosed prior arbitrations involving the parties' counsel, six of them ours. Barrow & Thistlewood accounts for approximately 48% of the arbitrator's estimated arbitration income over the past 36 months and 67% of her pending fee inventory.

This creates a serious asymmetry of risk. Whitmore has little practical incentive to unseat this arbitrator. But that is precisely the problem. If Whitmore prevails on a $46.9 million claim and Cascade thereafter discovers what our June 19, 2024 research memorandum has already documented, the award faces substantial vacatur exposure under Section 10(a)(2) of the Federal Arbitration Act, and Rule R-18(f) expressly preserves Cascade's right to raise grounds it could not reasonably have known. Worse, this firm now has documented actual knowledge of every one of these facts as of June 19, 2024 — five days before the stated objection deadline. Silence would convert a defensible arbitrator-selection outcome into an argument that Whitmore and its counsel knowingly sat on a defective disclosure.

**We therefore recommend that Whitmore not file a challenge, but that it promptly serve a written request for supplemental disclosure under Rule R-17(b) and (c), identify each deficiency on the record before June 24, 2024, and expressly reserve all rights under Rule R-18.** This forces the arbitrator to cure a defect she has an independent and continuing duty to cure, builds the record that defeats a later sandbagging argument, and — if both parties then elect to proceed on a complete record — converts an unwaived defect into an informed waiver that insulates the award. The full recommendation, including the contingency in which we do challenge, appears in Part VIII.

## II. THE APPLICABLE DISCLOSURE STANDARD AND ITS SOURCES

Four bodies of authority govern. They are cumulative, not alternative, and the disclosure statement itself invokes the first two.

### A. AAA Commercial Arbitration Rules R-16 and R-17 (Primary Standard)

The governing instrument is the AAA Commercial Arbitration Rules and Mediation Procedures (effective October 1, 2013; amended and effective September 1, 2022), incorporated by Section 12.3 of the Amended and Restated Operating Agreement of Cascade Thermal Systems Inc. dated January 8, 2019, and confirmed in the Arbitration Demand filed March 15, 2024.

**Rule R-16** states the substantive standard: an arbitrator "shall disclose to the AAA any circumstance likely to give rise to justifiable doubt as to the arbitrator's impartiality or independence, including any bias or any financial or personal interest in the result of the arbitration, or any past or present relationship with the parties, their representatives, or their counsel." Two features of R-16 are decisive here. First, the rule directs that "[t]he obligation to disclose under this Rule and Rule R-17 **shall be construed broadly**." Second, it defines the operative test objectively: the "justifiable doubt" standard "is intended to encompass **all circumstances that a reasonable person would consider relevant** in evaluating an arbitrator's ability to render a fair and unbiased determination." The arbitrator's own belief that a circumstance is innocuous is therefore not the measure; the reasonable party's view is.

**Rule R-17(a)** enumerates four non-exhaustive categories, each of which is directly engaged by this record:

- **R-17(a)(i)** — "Any known **direct or indirect** financial or personal interest in the outcome of the arbitration, including any interest, whether legal or beneficial, in the subject matter of the dispute, in any party, **or in any entity that may be affected by the result of the proceeding**." The rule's express reach to *indirect* and *beneficial* interests, and to non-party entities affected by the result, forecloses the narrower "direct equity positions" formulation the arbitrator adopted.

- **R-17(a)(ii)** — "Any known existing or past financial, business, professional, family, or social relationships with any party to the arbitration, its counsel, representatives, **or witnesses** that are likely to affect impartiality **or that might reasonably create an appearance of partiality or bias**. This includes, without limitation, relationships arising from employment, professional association, joint ventures, **board memberships, academic collaboration**, or regular social contact." The rule names, in terms, both of the categories the arbitrator failed to address: shared board service and academic collaboration. It also extends expressly to *witnesses*, not merely parties and counsel.

- **R-17(a)(iii)** — "Any prior or pending cases in which the arbitrator has been or is involved as arbitrator, counsel, expert, or in any other capacity, with any party to the arbitration, **its counsel**, or representatives. This obligation extends to **all matters**, whether administered by the AAA or by any other arbitral institution." The obligation is to disclose *all* such matters. It is not qualified by recollection.

- **R-17(a)(iv)** — A catch-all for "[a]ny other circumstances that might cause a reasonable party to question the arbitrator's ability to render a fair and impartial determination," to be "interpreted broadly."

**Rule R-17(b)** makes the duty continuing and self-executing: the arbitrator "must disclose any new information or any change in circumstances that arises after the initial disclosure as soon as practicable" and "**shall not await inquiry by a party or by the AAA** before making any required supplemental disclosure."

**Rule R-17(d)** supplies the consequence: failure "to make complete and timely disclosures as required by this Rule **may result in the arbitrator's removal** in accordance with Rule R-18, and **may constitute grounds for vacatur** of any resulting award under applicable law, including but not limited to Section 10 of the Federal Arbitration Act."

### B. Rule R-18 — Challenge Procedure and Waiver

**R-18(a)** permits any party to challenge on grounds of justifiable doubt, by written notice to the AAA and the other party "stating with specificity the grounds," within seven calendar days after the later of (i) communication of the appointment or (ii) the date "the circumstances giving rise to the challenge became known **or should reasonably have become known** to the challenging party."

**R-18(d)** identifies what the AAA weighs in deciding a challenge, and one item is squarely on point: the AAA "shall consider the circumstances disclosed by the arbitrator, **the disclosures required but not made**, the grounds stated in the challenge," and other relevant information. Non-disclosure is itself an express input into the challenge decision.

**R-18(f)** governs waiver: a party failing to make a timely challenge waives grounds "then known to the party," but "shall **not** be deemed to have waived its right to challenge based on grounds **that were not and could not reasonably have been known** at the time of the deadline," and nothing in the rule "shall preclude a party from raising issues of evident partiality in a proceeding to confirm, modify, or vacate an award."

**R-18(e)** confirms that the AAA's administrative decision does not affect a party's right to seek vacatur under FAA Section 10 or applicable state law.

### C. AAA/ABA Code of Ethics for Arbitrators in Commercial Disputes (2004)

The disclosure statement expressly invokes this Code in its Introduction. Canon II requires disclosure of any known direct or indirect financial or personal interest in the outcome, and of existing or past financial, business, professional or personal relationships that "might reasonably affect impartiality or lack of independence in the eyes of any of the parties." Two provisions bear directly on the deficiencies below:

- **Canon II(B)** imposes an affirmative duty of inquiry: a person requested to serve "should make a reasonable effort to inform themselves" of the interests and relationships subject to disclosure. Recollection is not the standard; reasonable investigation is.
- **Canon II(D)** resolves close calls: "Any doubt as to whether or not disclosure is to be made **should be resolved in favor of disclosure**."

Canon I (integrity and fairness of the process) and Canon IV (fair and diligent conduct of the proceedings) are also cited in the rules excerpt compiled for this matter.

### D. IBA Guidelines on Conflicts of Interest in International Arbitration (2014)

The AAA rules excerpt compiled for this case expressly commends the IBA Guidelines as a supplementary reference. Although not binding in a domestic AAA case, they are routinely used as persuasive taxonomy. General Standard 3 requires disclosure of facts that "may, in the eyes of the parties, give rise to doubts as to the arbitrator's impartiality or independence," judged from the parties' subjective perspective, and General Standard 7 imposes reciprocal duties of investigation on both arbitrator and parties. Applying the Guidelines' categorical framework, the repeat-appointment pattern, the co-service with counsel at a professional organization, and the co-panel appearance with lead counsel fall within the Orange List (circumstances requiring disclosure); an established, non-trivial financial interest in an entity whose value turns on the outcome would move toward the Red List. The point for present purposes is narrow but useful: not one of these categories is Green-List conduct that an arbitrator may properly leave unmentioned.

### E. FAA Section 10(a)(2) — The Vacatur Backstop

The seat is Minneapolis, Minnesota, and the Operating Agreement specifies that the Federal Arbitration Act governs the arbitration agreement's enforceability and procedure. A vacatur petition would therefore be heard in the District of Minnesota and reviewed under Eighth Circuit law. Section 10(a)(2) authorizes vacatur "where there was evident partiality or corruption in the arbitrators, or either of them."

The controlling framework begins with *Commonwealth Coatings Corp. v. Continental Casualty Co.*, 393 U.S. 145 (1968), in which the Supreme Court vacated an award because a neutral arbitrator failed to disclose a repeat commercial relationship with a party — a relationship generating fees the Court described as sporadic and modest. The plurality held that arbitrators "must disclose to the parties any dealings that might create an impression of possible bias." Later decisions have refined the test. The Eighth Circuit requires that the undisclosed facts be such that "a reasonable person would have to conclude that an arbitrator was partial," *Dow Corning Corp. v. Safety National Casualty Corp.*, 335 F.3d 742 (8th Cir. 2003); see also *Ploetz v. Morgan Stanley Smith Barney LLC*, 894 F.3d 894 (8th Cir. 2018). The Fifth Circuit, en banc, has held that nondisclosure of a trivial former relationship does not alone warrant vacatur absent a "significant compromising connection." *Positive Software Solutions, Inc. v. New Century Mortgage Corp.*, 476 F.3d 278 (5th Cir. 2007).

Three further authorities are important to how this record will be characterized:

- *Monster Energy Co. v. City Beverages, LLC*, 940 F.3d 1130 (9th Cir. 2019), vacated an award where the arbitrator failed to disclose his ownership interest in the administering ADR provider combined with that provider's substantial repeat business from one party. The combination of an *institutional* affiliation and a *repeat-player revenue stream* is the analytic template for Issues 3 and 8 below.
- *Applied Industrial Materials Corp. v. Ovalar Makine Ticaret Ve Sanayi, A.S.*, 492 F.3d 132 (2d Cir. 2007), holds that an arbitrator who knows of facts suggesting a non-trivial conflict must either investigate or disclose the possibility and his decision not to investigate; willful ignorance will not do.
- *Schmitz v. Zilveti*, 20 F.3d 1043 (9th Cir. 1994), and *New Regency Productions, Inc. v. Nippon Herald Films, Inc.*, 501 F.3d 1101 (9th Cir. 2007), establish that a failure to conduct a reasonable conflicts investigation can itself constitute evident partiality where the conflict was reasonably discoverable.

*Verification note:* the pin cites above are drawn from memory of the standard authorities in this area and should be confirmed and updated in Westlaw or Lexis before any of them is quoted in a filing to the AAA or a court.

### F. Synthesis — The Standard Applied Here

Distilled, the arbitrator was required to disclose, broadly construed and with doubts resolved in favor of disclosure, every known or reasonably discoverable circumstance — financial (direct or indirect), relational (with parties, counsel, representatives, or witnesses), and case-historical (all prior and pending matters with the parties or their counsel, at any institution) — that a reasonable party would consider relevant in evaluating her ability to decide this dispute fairly. Measured against that standard, the June 17, 2024 statement falls short in the respects set out below.

## IV. ISSUE-BY-ISSUE ANALYSIS

### ISSUE 1 — Seven Undisclosed Prior Arbitrations Involving the Parties' Counsel, Contradicting an Express Representation of Completeness

**What the disclosure says.** Section III lists seven matters and concludes: "To the best of my recollection, **the foregoing represents a complete list** of arbitrations in which I have served alongside counsel from either Barrow & Thistlewood LLP or Fielding Marsh & Collier LLP."

**Record evidence.** The Arbitrator Appointment Data compilation, "Case List" sheet, records twenty completed arbitrations between 2021 and 2024. Cross-checking each row against Section III of the disclosure identifies **seven completed matters involving one or both firms that do not appear in the disclosure at all**:

| AAA Case No. | Year | Caption | Firm's Role | Result | Est. Fees |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 01-22-0002015 | 2022 | *Crestfield Energy v. Loran Industrial* | B&T — claimant's counsel | Award for claimant; **B&T client prevailed** | $110,000 |
| 01-22-0002089 | 2022 | *Ashford Medical Devices v. Greenway Therapeutics* | B&T — respondent's counsel | Award for respondent; **B&T client prevailed** | $92,000 |
| 01-22-0002134 | 2022 | *Westbrook Partners v. Arcadian Ventures* | **FM — claimant; B&T — respondent** | Award for respondent; **B&T prevailed over FM** | $130,000 |
| 01-23-0002445 | 2023 | *Trident Coatings v. Bayshore Chemical* | B&T — claimant's counsel | Award for respondent; B&T client did not prevail | $105,000 |
| 01-23-0002578 | 2023 | *Redstone Logistics v. Fairfield Transport* | B&T — respondent's counsel | Award for respondent; **B&T client prevailed** | $118,000 |
| 01-23-0002643 | 2023 | *Thornwall Systems v. Kessler Automation* | FM — respondent's counsel | Award for claimant; FM client did not prevail | $82,000 |
| 01-23-0002789 | 2023 | *Warwick Technologies v. Cypress Digital* | B&T — claimant's counsel | Award for claimant; **B&T client prevailed** | $97,000 |

She served as sole arbitrator in every one of the seven. Six involve Barrow & Thistlewood; two involve Fielding Marsh; *Westbrook Partners* involves both, with our firm on the winning side against Claimant's counsel. Aggregate estimated fees from the seven undisclosed matters total **$734,000 — approximately 34.5% of her entire estimated arbitration income over the past 36 months** ($2,125,000 per the compilation's "Fee Summary" sheet).

Put another way: of nine completed matters in which Barrow & Thistlewood appeared before her, she disclosed **three**. Of five involving Fielding Marsh, she disclosed **three**. Together, 13 matters, 6 disclosed, 7 omitted.

**Analysis.** This is not an omission at the margins; it is a majority of the relevant universe. Rule R-17(a)(iii) requires disclosure of "any prior or pending cases" involving the parties' counsel, and states that the obligation "extends to all matters." Rule R-16 directs that the obligation "shall be construed broadly." The qualifier "[t]o the best of my recollection" does not save the representation: Canon II(B) requires a *reasonable effort to inform oneself*, and an arbitrator's own appointment history is uniquely within her control — retrievable from her billing records, her calendar, AAA case correspondence, and Lakeview's administrative files. A neutral who has conducted approximately 85 arbitrations in four years cannot discharge R-17(a)(iii) from memory, and the rules do not ask her to.

The affirmative representation of completeness compounds the omission. Had Section III simply listed seven matters without characterization, a party might reasonably read it as illustrative. The word "complete" invited both parties to stop looking. Cascade did stop looking. We did not.

One further point of magnitude. The 20-matter compilation is expressly a sample of her *most recent* completed arbitrations, not the full population of approximately 85 matters since 2020 (disclosure § II; Entity and Relationship Profiles § 3.1). Seven omissions in the most recent twenty implies that the true number of undisclosed matters across the full population may be materially larger. That inference should be put to the arbitrator directly.

**Challenge assessment: STRONG.** Standing alone, a pattern of repeat appointments involving counsel is not disqualifying; it is a familiar feature of a concentrated regional arbitration market, and *Positive Software* teaches that trivial undisclosed connections do not warrant vacatur. What elevates this issue is not the appointments but the **non-disclosure of a majority of them coupled with a written certification that the list was complete**. Rule R-18(d) instructs the AAA to weigh "the disclosures required but not made." That is exactly what this is. On this record the AAA would be well within its discretion to sustain a challenge, and a reviewing court applying *Commonwealth Coatings* would find the concealment more troubling than the underlying relationship.

**Data-integrity note.** The compilation's own summary rows state "Undisclosed matters involving B&T or FM: 5" and give Barrow & Thistlewood a client win rate of "7 of 9 (77.8%)." Neither figure reconciles to the row-level data, which shows **seven** undisclosed matters and a Barrow & Thistlewood client prevailing in **eight of nine** (88.9%). The row-level entries are the more reliable source: they tie exactly to the fee totals reported on the "Fee Summary" sheet ($1,020,000 for B&T; $410,000 for FM). The discrepancy should be resolved before any figure is asserted to the AAA, and we should be prepared for opposing counsel to attack whichever number we cite. Under either count the comparison with Fielding Marsh — whose clients prevailed in **one of four** adjudicated matters (25.0%) — is stark.

### ISSUE 2 — Undisclosed Pending Arbitration Involving Respondent's Counsel

**What the disclosure says.** Section III item 6 discloses *Orion Fabrication LLC v. Mountain West Supply Co.* as a currently pending matter in which Barrow & Thistlewood represents the respondent, noting that post-hearing briefs are submitted and an award is anticipated "within the next sixty days." No other pending matter involving either firm is identified.

**Record evidence.** The Arbitrator Appointment Data compilation, "Pending Matters" sheet, lists **AAA Case No. 01-24-0003105, *Halcyon Biotech v. Stonewall Partners***, in which Barrow & Thistlewood LLP serves as respondent's counsel, Judge Fairhaven serves as sole arbitrator, she was appointed **April 22, 2024**, the matter is in the pre-hearing phase, estimated remaining fees are **$175,000**, and the "Disclosed in Arbitrator Statement?" column reads "**No**." The Entity and Relationship Profiles memorandum (Barrow & Thistlewood, C. Aldridge to D. Hargrave, June 19, 2024) § 3.5 confirms the omission and observes that the disclosure therefore "understates the number of concurrent pending matters in which the arbitrator and Barrow & Thistlewood LLP are simultaneously engaged."

**Analysis.** This omission is qualitatively more serious than Issue 1, for three reasons.

First, it concerns a **concurrent, ongoing** engagement. A completed matter is a historical datum. A pending matter means that while this arbitrator sits in judgment on Whitmore's $46.9 million exposure, she is simultaneously presiding over another live proceeding in which the same law firm appears before her and from which she expects to earn $175,000. That is a present relationship, squarely within R-16's reference to "any past or **present** relationship with the parties, their representatives, or their counsel."

Second, **recollection cannot explain it.** She was appointed in *Halcyon* on April 22, 2024 — seven weeks before her selection here on June 3, 2024, and less than two months before she signed this disclosure. The matter is active on her docket. She disclosed the other pending Barrow & Thistlewood matter (*Orion*) in detail, including its procedural posture. The selective omission of the second pending matter is difficult to attribute to memory.

Third, it means the disclosure's picture of concurrency is wrong on its face. The true position is that **Judge Fairhaven has three simultaneous pending matters in which Barrow & Thistlewood appears** — this one, *Orion*, and *Halcyon* — representing $560,000 of her $835,000 pending fee inventory (**67.1%**). The disclosure discloses two of the three.

**Challenge assessment: STRONG.** Concurrent service is the paradigm case for mandatory disclosure under every authority surveyed in Part II, and it is precisely the kind of "significant compromising connection" that *Positive Software* distinguishes from a trivial historical contact. A challenge grounded on Issue 2 alone would present the AAA with a clean, documentary, non-speculative failure.

### ISSUE 3 — Undisclosed Magnitude of the Economic Relationship with Respondent's Counsel

**What the disclosure says.** Section IV addresses "Financial Interests and Investments" but confines itself to securities holdings; it says nothing about fee income from repeat appointments. Section IX discloses her $850 hourly rate and the approximately $340,000 estimated fee for *this* engagement. Nowhere does the statement quantify, or even gesture at, what fraction of her practice derives from matters in which the parties' counsel appear.

**Record evidence.** Arbitrator Appointment Data compilation, "Fee Summary" sheet:

- Barrow & Thistlewood LLP: **9 matters, $1,020,000, 48.0% of total arbitration income** over 36 months.
- Fielding Marsh & Collier LLP: **5 matters, $410,000, 19.3%.**
- Total across all 20 completed matters: **$2,125,000.**

Counting each matter once (the *Westbrook* matter involves both firms), the two firms in this arbitration together account for **13 of her 20 most recent completed matters and approximately $1,300,000 — 61.2% — of her estimated arbitration income.** On the "Pending Matters" sheet, Barrow & Thistlewood matters represent **$560,000 of $835,000 (67.1%)** of her pending fee inventory. The Entity and Relationship Profiles memorandum § 3.5 independently reports the 48.0% Barrow & Thistlewood figure. (That memorandum reports Fielding Marsh at $280,000 / 13.2% across four matters; the difference from the spreadsheet's $410,000 / 19.3% across five is entirely attributable to whether *Westbrook Partners* — where both firms appeared — is counted on the Fielding Marsh side. Both treatments are defensible; the underlying data are consistent.)

**Analysis.** *Commonwealth Coatings* vacated an award over an undisclosed repeat relationship generating roughly $12,000 in fees across several years. Here the undisclosed economic relationship with **Respondent's** counsel is approximately **$1,020,000**, or nearly half of the neutral's professional income, with a further $560,000 in the pipeline. *Monster Energy* is the modern analogue: the Ninth Circuit found evident partiality where an undisclosed institutional/financial interest coincided with a party's substantial repeat business with the ADR provider.

Two responses are available to the arbitrator and both are weak. She may say the aggregate figures are third-party estimates rather than her books. True, but she is uniquely able to produce the real figures, and R-17 obliges her to make the disclosure that puts the parties on notice of the *category* of interest even if the precise number requires her own records. She may say that repeat appointments by capable counsel are inevitable in the Minneapolis market. Also true, and it is a good answer to a *bias* allegation — but it is no answer at all to a *disclosure* allegation. Canon II(D) resolves the doubt in favor of disclosure.

The asymmetry between the firms is what makes this dangerous rather than merely notable. Whitmore's counsel supplies roughly two and a half times the income that Cascade's counsel supplies, and Whitmore's counsel wins before her at a rate of 88.9% (row-level data; 77.8% on the compilation's summary figure) against Fielding Marsh's 25.0%. We do not contend those outcome statistics show actual bias — with samples of nine and four they cannot, and the 25.0% figure includes *DataSync*, which Fielding Marsh won before her in 2024. But we must assume Cascade will deploy them, and in combination with a 48% income share that was never mentioned, they present an unattractive picture.

**Challenge assessment: STRONG**, and in our view the single most durable ground for a post-award vacatur petition, because it is quantitative, documentary, and does not depend on inferences about relationships.

### ISSUE 4 — Undisclosed Indirect Financial Interest in Whitmore Fund II LP; the "No Direct Equity Positions" Formulation

**What the disclosure says.** Section IV states: "**I hold no direct equity positions in any party to this arbitration.** I have not purchased, sold, or otherwise transacted in the stock, membership interests, partnership interests, or other equity securities of Cascade Thermal Systems Inc., Whitmore Capital Partners LLC, or any entity that I understand to be a direct subsidiary or affiliate of either party." It then concedes: "I have not undertaken a position-by-position review of each fund or sub-investment held within my managed accounts," and states that "[t]o the extent any pooled investment vehicle in which I hold an interest may have underlying investments in entities related to the parties, **I am not aware of any such investment** that would constitute a material financial interest."

**Record evidence.** Entity and Relationship Profiles § 3.4 and § 4.3 establish the following chain from public filings:

1. The arbitrator's personal portfolio is managed on a discretionary basis by **Northbridge Wealth Advisors** (arbitrator's own disclosure, § IV).
2. Northbridge Wealth Advisors also manages the **Northbridge Select Alternatives Fund**, a fund-of-funds allocating to private equity and alternative vehicles (SEC filings; Form ADV / Form D).
3. Per that fund's **most recent public filing dated March 2024**, it holds a **2.3% limited partner interest in Whitmore Fund II LP**.
4. **Whitmore Fund II LP** owns **Volaris Manufacturing Group LLC** (Arbitration Demand, March 15, 2024, § III.C; Entity Profiles § 1.3).
5. **Volaris is the direct beneficiary** of the disputed ThermalCore license — $2.8 million per year against an independently assessed fair market value of $9.5 million per year (Ridgeline Advisory Group valuation, November 15, 2023), the differential being the $46.9 million claimed.

The relief sought includes **rescission of the License Agreement** and **disgorgement of Volaris's profits** (Arbitration Demand, Prayer for Relief ¶¶ 2–3). An award granting either would directly reduce the value of Volaris and therefore of Whitmore Fund II LP's portfolio.

**Analysis.** Three points.

*First, the formulation is precisely engineered.* "No **direct** equity positions" is literally true even if she holds a substantial indirect beneficial interest. Rule R-17(a)(i), however, reaches "any known **direct or indirect** financial or personal interest," "whether legal **or beneficial**," "in any party, **or in any entity that may be affected by the result of the proceeding**." Volaris and Whitmore Fund II LP are textbook "entit[ies] that may be affected by the result." A disclosure drafted to be technically accurate while omitting the category the rule expressly names does not satisfy R-16's instruction that the obligation "shall be construed broadly."

*Second, the arbitrator disclosed her own failure to investigate.* Section IV concedes no position-by-position review, then Section X certifies "diligent inquiry." Those statements cannot both be right. *Applied Industrial* holds that an arbitrator on notice of a possible non-trivial conflict must investigate or disclose the possibility and the decision not to investigate; *Schmitz* and *New Regency* treat a failure to run a reasonably available conflicts check as itself supporting evident partiality. The inquiry available here was a single email to her portfolio manager at Northbridge asking whether her accounts hold the Select Alternatives Fund and whether any holding has exposure to Whitmore Capital Partners or its funds. She did not make it. Her "I am not aware" is, on her own account, the product of not having looked.

*Third, the fact is unproven but the disclosure failure is not.* We should be candid internally: the public record does **not** establish that her portfolio actually holds the Northbridge Select Alternatives Fund. Entity Profiles § 3.4 says so expressly. Cascade cannot presently prove an actual financial interest. But it does not need to in order to prevail on a disclosure theory. What R-17 required was disclosure of the *possibility* and of her decision not to run it down. The gap in the record is a gap the arbitrator alone can close — and if a challenge or a vacatur petition forces her to close it, the answer either eliminates the issue entirely or transforms it into the most serious ground in this memorandum.

**Challenge assessment: STRONG on the disclosure/failure-to-investigate theory; contingent on the underlying financial fact.** If the arbitrator holds the fund, this approaches a non-waivable interest in the outcome and we should expect the AAA to remove her. If she does not, the residual issue is the inadequate inquiry and the carefully limited "direct" phrasing. **This is the highest-value question to put to her, and the one most likely to be answered against her interest.**

### ISSUE 5 — Undisclosed Shared Organizational Service with Respondent's Founder and Managing Partner

**What the disclosure says.** Section VI discloses the arbitrator's advisory board service at the Minnesota ADR Ethics Institute since 2021 and expressly identifies **Ellen Wu** of Barrow & Thistlewood as a fellow advisory board member. It then states: "I am not aware of any other organizational affiliation that would give rise to justifiable doubt as to my impartiality." Section V separately represents: "I am not aware of any personal or family relationship with **any party**, counsel, or individual identified in the arbitration demand that would give rise to justifiable doubt regarding my impartiality."

**Record evidence.** Entity and Relationship Profiles §§ 1.1, 3.3, and 4.2, sourced to the **IRS Form 990 filings of the Minnesota ADR Ethics Institute for fiscal year 2023**, establish that **Garrett T. Whitmore — founder and Managing Partner of Respondent Whitmore Capital Partners LLC — serves on the Institute's Board of Trustees.** The Board of Trustees is the Institute's governing body, responsible for strategic direction, budgetary oversight, and **appointments to leadership positions, including appointments to the advisory board on which the arbitrator sits**. The Institute is described as a small nonprofit with limited staff; the advisory board comprises approximately twelve members (disclosure § VI); and the Institute's published bylaws and posted meeting minutes indicate that both bodies attend annual meetings and hold **periodic joint sessions** on programming and organizational strategy.

Mr. Whitmore is not a peripheral figure in this case. The Arbitration Demand names him throughout as the individual who "personally directed the terms of the license" and who "exercises authority over both fund vehicles" (§§ III.B, V.C), and identifies him as an **anticipated adverse witness** at the evidentiary hearing (§ VIII.A.2).

**Analysis.** This is, in our assessment, the most damaging single omission, and for a reason distinct from Issues 1–3: it is a relationship not with counsel but **with the Respondent itself, through its principal and a testifying witness.**

The structural relationship makes it worse. The arbitrator sits on a body whose members are appointed by, and serve at the pleasure of, a board on which the Respondent's founder sits. A reasonable party in Cascade's position would consider that relevant — which is the R-16 test, verbatim. Rule R-17(a)(ii) expressly enumerates "board memberships" as a covered relationship category, and expressly extends to relationships with "witnesses."

The selectivity of the diligence is the aggravating feature. Judge Fairhaven plainly *did* check the Institute's roster against the participants in this case: that is the only plausible explanation for her volunteering Ellen Wu's advisory board service, which no one had asked about. Having run that check against opposing counsel's firm, she either did not run it against the Respondent's principal or ran it and did not report the result. Either way, the disclosure she chose to make undercuts any claim that the omission reflects an oversight of the kind R-17 tolerates. Canon II(D) — resolve doubt in favor of disclosure — leaves no room here.

Whether she and Mr. Whitmore have actually interacted is a matter of inference (Entity Profiles § 3.3 says it is "reasonable to infer" they do, given the organization's size and joint sessions). But R-17 does not condition disclosure on demonstrated interaction. The affiliation itself is the disclosable fact.

**Challenge assessment: STRONG.** A relationship between the neutral and the Respondent's founder — who both controls the Respondent and will testify — that runs through a governance structure in which he helps appoint her, is not a Green List circumstance under any framework. Combined with the disclosure's affirmative statement that she is unaware of any relationship with "any party," this ground alone would support a Rule R-18 challenge by Cascade and would feature prominently in any Section 10(a)(2) petition.

### ISSUE 6 — Undisclosed Co-Authorship Between the Arbitrator's Spouse and Claimant's Key Expert Witness; Affirmative Misstatement

**What the disclosure says.** Section V identifies Dr. Alan Fairhaven as her husband, a retired University of Minnesota mechanical engineering professor, and states: "He is not currently engaged in any active research collaborations with industrial partners, and **to the best of my knowledge, he has no financial or professional relationship with Cascade Thermal Systems Inc., Whitmore Capital Partners LLC, or any of their officers, directors, or employees.**"

**Record evidence.** Entity and Relationship Profiles § 3.2, sourced to academic publication databases:

> Title: "Optimization of Phase-Change Heat Exchangers in High-Load Industrial Applications"
> Journal: *Journal of Advanced Thermal Engineering*, Vol. 42, Issue 3, **September 2022**
> Authors: **Alan R. Fairhaven, Ph.D.; Rita S. Peshkov, Ph.D.**

**Dr. Rita Peshkov is Vice President of Research & Development at Cascade Thermal Systems Inc.** — that is, an officer and employee of the Claimant. She has held that role since before the 2019 Whitmore acquisition, is the named inventor or co-inventor on the majority of Cascade's fourteen ThermalCore patents, and is designated in the Arbitration Demand (March 15, 2024) at § VIII.B.2 as Claimants' **key expert witness on technical matters**, expected to testify on the technology's specifications and uniqueness, the absence of substitutes, and — critically — "the technical basis for the valuation methodology employed by Ridgeline Advisory Group." Her curriculum vitae, "including her educational background, professional experience, patent inventorship, **publication history**, and professional affiliations," is **Exhibit H** to the Demand.

The paper's subject matter — phase-change heat exchangers in high-load industrial applications — is not adjacent to this dispute; it *is* this dispute's technology. The Demand describes ThermalCore as encompassing "proprietary heat-exchange systems designed for high-load industrial applications, including phase-change heat exchangers" (§ V.B).

**Analysis.** Three observations.

*First, this is an affirmative inaccuracy, not merely an omission.* The statement that Dr. Fairhaven "has no financial or professional relationship with Cascade Thermal Systems Inc. ... or any of their officers, directors, or employees" is incorrect as written. Co-authorship of a peer-reviewed article with a company's VP of R&D is a professional relationship with an employee of that company. The "to the best of my knowledge" qualifier limits her culpability but not the inaccuracy of the representation, and it sits uneasily beside the sentence immediately preceding it, in which she affirmatively states that her husband is "not currently engaged in any active research collaborations."

*Second, the fact was reasonably discoverable — by her, from the case file.* The disclosure's own Introduction represents that she "reviewed the identities of the parties, their counsel, and the individuals identified in the arbitration demand and answering statement." Dr. Peshkov is identified in the Demand five separate times, by name and title, as Claimants' key expert. Her publication history was submitted as Exhibit H. Rule R-17(a)(ii) expressly names "academic collaboration" as a covered relationship category and expressly extends the duty to relationships with **witnesses**. An arbitrator who reads a witness list containing a specialist in her spouse's exact field, in her spouse's own department's discipline, in the same metropolitan area, is on inquiry notice. *Applied Industrial* and *Schmitz* address precisely this posture.

*Third — and this is what makes the issue strategically distinctive — Issue 6 is the one deficiency that runs in Whitmore's favor.* Every other ground identified in this memorandum would, if raised, be raised by Cascade. This one belongs to us. A neutral whose husband collaborated on published research with the opposing side's principal technical expert, on the technology at the heart of the case, has an undisclosed connection that a reasonable respondent would want to know about before the expert takes the stand and before her credibility is weighed.

We should be measured about its strength. A single co-authored paper published nearly two years ago, between the arbitrator's *spouse* and a witness — not between the arbitrator and a party — is a second-degree connection. *Positive Software* counsels that attenuated historical contacts do not compel vacatur. Cascade will characterize it as ordinary academic life in a small field. But Entity Profiles § 3.2 is right that co-authorship in a specialist journal ordinarily reflects "a sustained professional collaboration" involving joint research design, data analysis, drafting, and peer-review response over many months — and Rule R-17 does not exempt second-degree relationships. Canon II(A)(2) covers relationships that "might reasonably affect impartiality ... **in the eyes of any of the parties**."

**Challenge assessment: SUBSTANTIAL.** Sufficient to support a good-faith Rule R-18 challenge by Whitmore, and — importantly for the strategy in Part VIII — sufficient to give Whitmore a **legitimate, non-pretextual reason of its own** to put the adequacy of this disclosure before the AAA. Unlikely on its own to compel vacatur, but a meaningful contributor to a cumulative-effect argument, and the natural vehicle for raising the whole disclosure record.

### ISSUE 7 — Undisclosed CLE Co-Panel Appearance with Respondent's Lead Counsel on the Subject Matter of This Dispute

**What the disclosure says.** Section V represents: "I have **not socialized with, and do not maintain a personal friendship with, any counsel of record** or any individual identified in the pleadings as a party representative or anticipated witness." Section III discloses prior arbitrations involving the two firms. Nothing in the statement discloses any professional contact with Mr. Hargrave outside those arbitrations.

**Record evidence.** Entity and Relationship Profiles §§ 2.1, 3.6, and 4.1, sourced to Lakeview Dispute Resolution Center event listings and Minnesota State Bar Association CLE records:

- **Event:** "Arbitrating Complex IP Licensing Disputes," a CLE panel discussion
- **Date:** **October 14, 2023**
- **Venue:** Lakeview Dispute Resolution Center, 750 Nicollet Mall, Suite 3200, Minneapolis — **the center with which the arbitrator is affiliated as a neutral**
- **Panelists:** **Hon. Margaret R. Fairhaven (Ret.)**; **Dominic Hargrave**, lead partner, Barrow & Thistlewood LLP and lead counsel for Respondent here; Prof. Naomi J. Strickland, University of Minnesota Law School; Marcus DelVecchio, Henning & Burwell P.A.
- **Topics:** valuation methodologies, arbitrator selection in technology-intensive cases, and management of expert testimony in IP licensing arbitration

The event occurred approximately five months before the Demand was filed (March 15, 2024) and eight months before the appointment was confirmed (June 10, 2024).

**Analysis.** The Section V representation is not literally falsified by this record — co-panel service at a professional education event is not "socializing" and does not establish "personal friendship." But the representation is materially incomplete, and R-16 directs broad construction. Rule R-17(a)(ii) covers "professional ... relationships" including those "arising from ... professional association," and R-17(a)(iv) sweeps in "any other circumstances that might cause a reasonable party to question the arbitrator's ability to render a fair and impartial determination," expressly including "the arbitrator's prior publications, public statements, or **expressed views on legal or factual issues relevant to the dispute**."

That last clause is the sharper edge. This was not a panel on courtroom civility. It was a panel on **arbitrating complex IP licensing disputes**, covering **valuation methodology** and **management of expert testimony** — the two contested analytical questions in this case, where the entire claim turns on whether $2.8 million or $9.5 million per year is the right number and on the weight to be given competing expert valuation testimony. Judge Fairhaven expressed views on those topics, from a shared platform with Respondent's lead counsel, five months before this Demand was filed. R-17(a)(iv) required disclosure of that fact on its own terms, independent of the relationship it evidences.

Co-panel service also carries the ordinary incidents Entity Profiles § 3.6 describes: shared preparation, coordination of materials, and informal professional interaction before and after the event. That is collegial familiarity between the neutral and one side's lead advocate — the sort of thing Canon II(D) directs be disclosed rather than judged innocuous by the neutral herself.

**Challenge assessment: SUBSTANTIAL.** On its own, a single CLE panel would not ordinarily unseat an arbitrator; ADR neutrals and arbitration counsel appear together on such panels constantly, and an over-strict rule would be unworkable. But two features lift this above the routine: the panel's subject was the subject of this case, and it was hosted at the arbitrator's own affiliated ADR center, which ties Issue 7 to Issue 8. Its principal value to Cascade will be corroborative — one more undisclosed point of contact with the Respondent's side, in a disclosure that repeatedly failed to surface them.

### ISSUE 8 — Incomplete Disclosure of the Lakeview Dispute Resolution Center Affiliation

**What the disclosure says.** Sections II and VII disclose that the arbitrator is a neutral on Lakeview's panel, identify Lakeview's address and its Managing Director (Harold Lund), state that Lakeview maintains approximately thirty neutrals, and acknowledge that "[i]n the ordinary course of its operations, Lakeview receives case referrals from various law firms ... **including from both Barrow & Thistlewood LLP and Fielding Marsh & Collier LLP**." Section VII adds that "[t]his is not unusual," that this matter was administered by the AAA rather than referred through Lakeview, and that Lakeview's referral relationships do not influence her decision-making.

**Record evidence.** Entity and Relationship Profiles § 4.1 identifies what the disclosure omits: "The public record does not clearly indicate whether the arbitrator's affiliation with Lakeview is structured as an **employment relationship, an independent contractor arrangement, or an equity-holder or member-owner arrangement.** The financial terms of the affiliation — including whether Lakeview takes a percentage of the arbitrator's fees ..., whether Lakeview receives separate administrative fees for referred matters, or whether **Lakeview's revenue model depends on maintaining referral relationships with law firms that regularly retain its affiliated neutrals** — are not publicly available." Section 4.1 also notes that Lakeview hosted the October 14, 2023 CLE panel at which the arbitrator and Mr. Hargrave appeared together (Issue 7). The relationship matrix in Entity Profiles § V classifies this connection as disclosed only "**partial** — financial terms not disclosed."

**Analysis.** To the arbitrator's credit, Section VII is the most forthcoming part of the statement: she volunteered the referral relationship with both firms without being asked, and correctly noted that Lakeview has no administrative role here. The deficiency is one of depth, not candor.

What is missing is the information that would let a party assess whether the affiliation creates an economic interest. *Monster Energy* is directly instructive: the Ninth Circuit found evident partiality where the arbitrator held an undisclosed **ownership** interest in the ADR provider and that provider had substantial repeat business from one party. The disclosure here does not say whether Judge Fairhaven is a Lakeview owner, member, employee, or independent contractor. If she holds an ownership or profit-participation interest, and if Barrow & Thistlewood is a material source of Lakeview's referral volume, the *Monster Energy* structure is reproduced almost exactly — and Barrow & Thistlewood's 48% share of her personal arbitration income (Issue 3) makes it a plausible hypothesis that the firm is also a significant Lakeview referral source.

Two further points connect this issue to others. First, Lakeview hosted the CLE panel that brought her together with Respondent's lead counsel (Issue 7), which suggests Lakeview functions as a venue where the neutral and the referring firms cultivate relationships. Second, Section VII's assurance that "the referral of cases to Lakeview does not influence my decision-making" is a statement about her subjective state of mind. Rule R-16 sets an objective test — what a reasonable person would consider relevant — and an arbitrator's self-assessment of her own impartiality is not the disclosure the rule requires.

**Challenge assessment: SUBSTANTIAL, and contingent.** As disclosed, this is not a challenge ground; the arbitrator addressed the topic in good faith. But the omitted variable — the financial structure of the affiliation — is dispositive of whether *Monster Energy* applies. **This should be the second question we put to the arbitrator**, immediately after the Northbridge question. Depending on the answer, this issue either resolves benignly or becomes the most serious ground on this record.

### ISSUE 9 — Defective Standard of Inquiry and an Unsupportable Certification

**What the disclosure says.** The statement contains, in Section X, an unqualified certification: "I hereby certify that **based on a diligent inquiry**, the foregoing represents all circumstances known to me that might give rise to justifiable doubt as to my impartiality or independence in this matter. I have endeavored to provide a thorough and complete disclosure, and I have not knowingly withheld any information that I believe would be material." The Introduction likewise states: "**I have made a diligent effort to identify all matters warranting disclosure.**"

Set against those certifications are three qualifiers embedded in the body of the statement:

1. § III: "**To the best of my recollection**, the foregoing represents a complete list of arbitrations ...";
2. § IV: "**I have not undertaken a position-by-position review** of each fund or sub-investment held within my managed accounts";
3. § V: "**to the best of my knowledge**, he has no financial or professional relationship with Cascade ... or any of their officers, directors, or employees."

**Record evidence.** The seven undisclosed completed matters and one undisclosed pending matter (Issues 1–2); the absence of any inquiry to Northbridge Wealth Advisors regarding fund-of-funds exposure (Issue 4, and the arbitrator's own concession in § IV); the Form 990 identification of Garrett T. Whitmore as an Institute Trustee, retrievable from a public nonprofit database (Issue 5); and the publicly indexed co-authored paper listed in Exhibit H to the Demand (Issue 6).

**Analysis.** Issue 9 is not a freestanding conflict; it is the finding that explains all the others and elevates them from a series of oversights into a pattern.

Canon II(B) of the AAA/ABA Code requires an arbitrator to "make a **reasonable effort to inform themselves**" of disclosable interests and relationships. Rule R-16 requires the disclosure obligation to be "construed broadly." Neither authority permits a neutral to substitute recollection for records, particularly as to her own case history, or to decline a one-step inquiry into her own investment accounts and then certify diligence.

The certification therefore fails on its own terms. A "diligent inquiry" that (i) did not consult her appointment records, (ii) did not consult her portfolio manager, (iii) did not check the Institute's Form 990 or membership roster against the Respondent's principal despite checking it against opposing counsel's firm, and (iv) did not cross-reference an exhibit to the Demand containing a witness's publication list, is not diligent. This matters at two levels: it strengthens each individual ground, and it is independently significant under *Applied Industrial*, *Schmitz*, and *New Regency*, which treat a failure to conduct a reasonably available conflicts investigation as itself capable of supporting evident partiality.

**Challenge assessment: SUPPORTING.** Not a standalone ground, but the connective tissue of any challenge or vacatur petition. It converts eight discrete gaps into one systemic failure of the disclosure process and rebuts the innocent-oversight defense the arbitrator will otherwise advance.

### ISSUE 10 — Misstatement of the Rule R-18 Challenge Procedure; Disclosure Served After Selection and Confirmation

**What the disclosure says.** Section X states: "Pursuant to AAA Commercial Arbitration Rule R-18, the parties have until **June 24, 2024** — seven (7) calendar days from the date of this disclosure — to raise any objection to my service as arbitrator. ... **If no objection is received within the prescribed period, I will proceed with my duties as Sole Arbitrator.**"

The cover page records: Date of Party Selection **June 3, 2024**; Date of AAA Appointment Confirmation **June 10, 2024**; Date of This Disclosure **June 17, 2024**.

**Record evidence.** Rule R-18(a) (challenge due within seven days after the later of communication of the appointment **or** the date the circumstances "became known or should reasonably have become known"); Rule R-18(f) (no waiver of grounds "that were not and could not reasonably have been known"; no preclusion of evident-partiality issues in a confirmation, modification, or vacatur proceeding); Rule R-16 ("Any person **appointed or to be appointed** as an arbitrator shall disclose ..."); Rule R-12(a) (list of candidates to be accompanied by "a brief biographical summary and **any disclosures then available**"). Timeline corroborated by Entity Profiles § 3.1 and § VI.

**Analysis.** Two defects.

*The statement of the rule is incomplete in a way that favors the arbitrator.* Section X presents June 24 as an absolute cutoff after which she simply proceeds. Rule R-18(a) actually keys the seven days to the **later** of appointment or discovery of the circumstances, and R-18(f) expressly preserves grounds that could not reasonably have been known by that date. That distinction is decisive here: because the arbitrator's own disclosure omitted Issues 1 through 8, Cascade could not reasonably have known them by June 24, and its right to challenge them is preserved by R-18(f) whenever it learns of them — including after an award, in a Section 10(a)(2) proceeding. The parties' actual position is materially better than Section X describes. We should not rely on the June 24 date as a repose.

*The disclosure came too late to inform the selection it was supposed to inform.* Rule R-16 obliges disclosure by any person "appointed **or to be appointed**," and Rule R-12(a) contemplates that disclosures accompany the candidate list. Here the parties ranked and selected Judge Fairhaven on **June 3**, the AAA confirmed her on **June 10**, and the disclosure issued on **June 17** — fourteen days after the parties made the very decision the disclosure exists to inform. Cascade ranked her third with no disclosure before it. Whitmore ranked her first.

That last sentence is why Issue 10 requires care. **On this record it is Whitmore, not Cascade, whose selection decision is exposed.** Cascade selected in the dark. Whitmore selected knowing — through Ms. Wu, who sits on the same Institute advisory board and who Entity Profiles § 2.1 states "is involved in arbitrator selection strategy for matters handled by the practice group, **including this arbitration**" — at least that Ms. Wu served with the arbitrator, and knowing (through Mr. Hargrave) of the October 2023 CLE panel; and Mr. Whitmore knew of his own trusteeship. A late disclosure that left one side blind while the other side had independent knowledge is not a good fact for us, and Cascade will use it.

**Challenge assessment: SUPPORTING, with a caution.** Not a basis for a Whitmore challenge. Significant because it (i) refutes any assumption that June 24 extinguishes Cascade's rights, and (ii) frames the waiver asymmetry analyzed in Part VI.

## V. CUMULATIVE ASSESSMENT

The individual issues should not be assessed only individually. Rule R-18(d) directs the AAA to weigh "the circumstances disclosed by the arbitrator, **the disclosures required but not made**, [and] the grounds stated in the challenge" together, and courts applying Section 10(a)(2) assess the total picture a reasonable person would form.

That total picture, on this record, is:

- A neutral who derives approximately **48% of her arbitration income** from Respondent's counsel, with **67% of her pending fee inventory** attributable to that firm;
- who disclosed **three of nine** completed matters with that firm and **two of three** concurrent pending matters with it;
- who **certified that her list was complete** when **seven completed matters representing $734,000 — over a third of her 36-month income — were missing**;
- who sits on the advisory board of a small nonprofit governed by a board of trustees that includes **Respondent's founder, controlling principal, and an anticipated adverse witness**, and who disclosed a fellow advisory-board member from Respondent's law firm while omitting Respondent's own principal;
- who appeared on a CLE panel with **Respondent's lead counsel**, at **her own affiliated ADR center**, on **the exact subject matter of this dispute**, five months before the Demand;
- whose portfolio is managed by a firm whose fund-of-funds product holds a limited partner interest in **the very Whitmore fund that owns the beneficiary of the disputed license**, and who declined to check whether she is invested in it;
- whose spouse **co-authored a paper on the disputed technology with Claimant's key expert witness**, contrary to an affirmative representation that he has no professional relationship with Cascade's employees;
- and who certified all of the above as the product of "diligent inquiry."

No single item in that list would necessarily unseat an experienced neutral. Taken together, they describe a disclosure that did not do the work Rule R-17 assigns it. We assess that a Rule R-18 challenge by Cascade, properly documented, would have a **realistic prospect of success before the AAA**, and that a post-award Section 10(a)(2) petition premised on Issues 1–5 would present a **serious, non-frivolous risk to any award in Whitmore's favor** — the more so in the Eighth Circuit if the Northbridge or Lakeview financial questions resolve unfavorably.

## VI. WAIVER, TIMING, AND THE ASYMMETRY BETWEEN THE PARTIES

Rule R-18(f) is the pivot of the strategic analysis. It provides that a party failing to challenge within the R-18(a) window waives grounds **"then known to the party,"** but preserves grounds **"that were not and could not reasonably have been known"** at the deadline, and expressly does not preclude raising evident partiality in a later confirmation or vacatur proceeding. Rule R-18(e) confirms that the AAA's administrative ruling does not affect FAA Section 10 rights.

Applied to these facts, the two parties stand in fundamentally different positions.

**Cascade's position.** Cascade's knowledge as of June 24, 2024 is limited to what the disclosure statement told it — Sections I through X as summarized in Part III. Issues 1 through 8 are, by definition, matters the arbitrator did not disclose. Nothing in the record suggests Cascade has independently developed them; the research that produced them is our own privileged work product. Cascade therefore **waives essentially nothing** by letting June 24 pass. Under R-18(f) it may raise every one of Issues 1–8 whenever it learns of them, including in a petition to vacate an adverse award. The nondisclosure that presently benefits us is, in the medium term, the mechanism that keeps Cascade's rights alive.

**Whitmore's position.** Whitmore's knowledge is materially greater, and it is documented. Ellen Wu, who Entity Profiles § 2.1 states is "involved in arbitrator selection strategy ... including this arbitration," has served with the arbitrator on the Institute advisory board since approximately 2021. Mr. Hargrave appeared on the October 14, 2023 panel with her. Garrett T. Whitmore knows of his own trusteeship. And as of **June 19, 2024**, this firm's own research memorandum reduced the entire picture — all ten issues — to writing, addressed to the lead partner, **five days before the stated deadline**.

That documented knowledge cuts two ways, and both need to be understood.

*First, as to Whitmore's own challenge rights:* Issue 6 (the Peshkov co-authorship) is the ground that favors us, and we learned of it on June 19. If we do not raise it by June 24, R-18(f) will be read to waive it, because it is a ground "then known to the party." Our right to raise Issue 6 expires this week.

*Second, and far more consequentially, as to the durability of any award:* Whitmore cannot use the arbitrator's nondisclosure as a shield and its own silence as a sword. Suppose the tribunal rejects the $46.9 million claim in January–February 2025 and issues an award for Whitmore. Cascade thereafter learns — through post-award discovery, a public filing, or simple diligence — of the Northbridge chain, the Whitmore trusteeship, the CLE panel, and seven undisclosed matters. It petitions to vacate under Section 10(a)(2) in the District of Minnesota. Whitmore's defenses will be waiver and triviality. Waiver fails under R-18(f) because Cascade could not reasonably have known. Triviality is a hard sell against a 48% income share, a party principal on the appointing board, and an indirect interest in the fund that owns the license beneficiary. And Cascade will then produce the fact that Whitmore's counsel had catalogued every one of these connections in a June 19, 2024 memorandum and said nothing — which converts a nondisclosure case against the arbitrator into a bad-faith case against the party, and is the kind of fact that moves a court applying the demanding *Dow Corning* standard.

The exposure is not symmetrical, and it is not small. A vacated award after a fifteen-day hearing means the parties return to Rule R-19 replacement and re-arbitrate, with Whitmore having spent its fees, disclosed its case, and lost the arbitrator it preferred — while Cascade will have obtained a free preview of the defense.

## VII. STRATEGIC ASSESSMENT

Three courses are available.

**Option A — Say nothing.** Attractive on its face: the arbitrator is Whitmore's first-ranked candidate; nine of ten deficiencies would trouble Cascade rather than us; the hearing dates of January 13 – February 7, 2025 are secured; and a challenge would forfeit all of it. But Option A buys a favorable tribunal at the price of an unenforceable award, and it does so with this firm's documented knowledge on the file. It also leaves Issue 6 — our own legitimate ground — waived. We do not recommend it.

**Option B — File a Rule R-18 challenge.** Whitmore has a good-faith basis to challenge on Issue 6, and a challenge would be the most complete protection against later vacatur. But the cost is high and the benefit uncertain. Under R-18(c)–(d), if Cascade agrees to the challenge or the arbitrator withdraws, we lose our preferred neutral and go back to Rule R-19 / R-12 selection, almost certainly losing the January–February 2025 hearing window. And a challenge grounded on Issue 6 alone would look opportunistic: we would be objecting to the one connection that favors Cascade while remaining silent about eight that favor us — which, if the full record later emerges, is worse for our credibility than having said nothing.

**Option C — Request supplemental disclosure under Rule R-17(b)–(c), on the record, before June 24, reserving all rights.** This is the course we recommend, and it is available precisely because R-17(b) makes the arbitrator's duty continuing and self-executing: she "shall not await inquiry by a party or by the AAA before making any required supplemental disclosure." Asking her to discharge a duty she already owes is not a challenge, is not an accusation, and does not put her office at issue. It:

- **preserves the arbitrator** unless her answers make her untenable, in which case we would not have wanted her anyway;
- **cures the R-18(f) problem prospectively**, because once the omitted facts are disclosed to both parties, the seven-day clock under R-18(a) begins to run against Cascade on those grounds, and a Cascade decision to proceed becomes an informed election rather than a preserved objection;
- **defeats the sandbagging narrative**, replacing "Whitmore knew and stayed silent" with "Whitmore identified the gaps and put them on the record within two days of discovering them";
- **preserves Issue 6** by raising it within the R-18(a) window without expending a challenge;
- **resolves the two open financial questions** (Northbridge and Lakeview) that presently determine whether this record is a manageable disclosure problem or a disqualifying interest; and
- **costs almost nothing** if the answers are benign.

The principal risk of Option C is that it hands Cascade the research. That is real, and it is the reason Option A is tempting. But the information is not durably ours: it was assembled entirely from public sources — SEC filings, IRS Form 990s, academic databases, CLE archives, and public award records (Entity Profiles § VII) — and any competent conflicts check by Fielding Marsh at any point over the next seven months would find the same material. We are choosing between disclosing it now, on our terms, before a hearing, and having it discovered later, on Cascade's terms, after an award. The second is much worse.

A second risk is that the arbitrator takes offence. In practice, an experienced neutral presented with a courteous request framed under her own continuing R-17(b) duty will supplement, and will be grateful to have the gaps closed before an award rather than after. If she reacts badly, that reaction is itself information about her suitability.

## VIII. RECOMMENDATION

**We recommend Option C, implemented as follows.**

**1. Do not file a Rule R-18 challenge at this time.** The arbitrator's experience, subject-matter background, and availability serve Whitmore's interests, and no ground presently known compels her removal. Preserve the challenge as a contingency.

**2. Serve a written request for supplemental disclosure on or before June 21, 2024** — not June 24, to leave room to act on the response — addressed to the AAA with simultaneous copy to Fielding Marsh & Collier LLP as Rule R-20(b) requires. Frame it expressly as an invitation to the arbitrator to discharge her continuing obligation under Rule R-17(b) and (c), not as a challenge. The request should ask her to confirm or supplement as to:

   a. **All** prior and pending arbitrations, at any institution, in which either Barrow & Thistlewood LLP or Fielding Marsh & Collier LLP has appeared before her — specifically referencing Rule R-17(a)(iii)'s "all matters" language and asking that the response be drawn from her records rather than recollection;
   b. All **currently pending** matters in which either firm appears, including counsel, appointment date, and posture;
   c. Whether her managed accounts at Northbridge Wealth Advisors hold any interest, direct or indirect, in the **Northbridge Select Alternatives Fund** or any other pooled vehicle with exposure to Whitmore Capital Partners LLC, Whitmore Fund II LP, Whitmore Fund III LP, or Volaris Manufacturing Group LLC — and asking that she make inquiry of her portfolio manager rather than rely on present awareness;
   d. The **structure and financial terms of her Lakeview affiliation** — employee, independent contractor, member, or equity holder; whether Lakeview participates in her fee revenue; and the approximate share of Lakeview's referral volume attributable to either firm;

## X. CONCLUSION

The June 17, 2024 disclosure statement does not satisfy AAA Commercial Arbitration Rules R-16 and R-17. It omits seven completed and one pending arbitration involving the parties' counsel while certifying that its list was complete; it says nothing about an economic relationship with Respondent's counsel accounting for roughly half of the arbitrator's professional income; it addresses financial interests in terms ("no direct equity positions") that exclude precisely the indirect and beneficial interests the rule reaches, on the arbitrator's own admission that she did not look; it omits the Respondent principal's trusteeship at an organization whose governing board appoints the advisory board on which she sits; it omits a CLE panel shared with Respondent's lead counsel on the subject matter of this dispute; it affirmatively misstates her spouse's professional relationships with Claimant's personnel; and it certifies all of this as the product of diligent inquiry.

Eight of these deficiencies would support a Rule R-18 challenge by Cascade, and four of them — the undisclosed case history, the undisclosed concurrent matter, the undisclosed 48% income concentration, and the Institute trusteeship — would be substantial contributors to a Section 10(a)(2) petition. Only one deficiency belongs to Whitmore, and it expires on June 24, 2024 if unraised.

Whitmore's interest is not in unseating this arbitrator. It is in obtaining an award that survives. Those objectives point to the same action: a prompt, courteous, comprehensive request for supplemental disclosure under Rule R-17(b), served on the AAA with copy to opposing counsel before the deadline, with all rights reserved. We are available to circulate a draft letter for your review today.
