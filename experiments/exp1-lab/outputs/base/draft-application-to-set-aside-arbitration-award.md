## I. PRELIMINARY STATEMENT

1. Greystone does not come to this Court to relitigate an arbitration it lost. It comes because the arbitration it lost was not the arbitration the parties bargained for.

2. Three defects require vacatur, each independently sufficient and together overwhelming.

3. **First**, the sole arbitrator's son has been, since September 2022, a senior associate at Ridgeline Chambers LLP — the law firm that represented the prevailing party, Veridian Aerospace Systems, LLC ("Veridian"), from the filing of the Request for Arbitration through the issuance of the Final Award. He practices in the same office and in the same Aerospace & Defense industry group as Veridian's lead counsel, Marcus D. Wheaton, who co-chairs that group. The arbitrator never disclosed this relationship. Worse, she affirmatively represented the opposite: her August 22, 2023 PAS Disclosure Statement states that she has "no prior personal, professional, or financial relationship with Marcus D. Wheaton or Ridgeline Chambers LLP," that she is "not affiliated with any law firm or other organization that could give rise to conflicts," and — under a formal certification — that she had "made a diligent and thorough inquiry into all facts and circumstances that might give rise to justifiable doubts as to [her] impartiality or independence" and was "not aware of any other facts or circumstances, beyond those set forth herein." Greystone learned the truth only after the Award issued, through public-records research conducted by undersigned counsel. That is evident partiality under 9 U.S.C. § 10(a)(2).

4. **Second**, the arbitrator excluded the single most probative piece of evidence in the case — independent laboratory re-testing of the actual disputed goods, which found a 2.1% defect rate against a contractual threshold of 3% — on the ground that it was "untimely," when the untimeliness was caused entirely and exclusively by Veridian's own 45-day violation of the arbitrator's own discovery order. The arbitrator declined to sanction Veridian for that violation, then imposed on Greystone the full evidentiary consequence of it. Having excluded the independent evidence, the arbitrator then discredited Greystone's remaining quality evidence precisely because it was *not* independent — it was "generated by Greystone's own personnel" and drawn from a "smaller sample size." That is misconduct in refusing to hear evidence pertinent and material to the controversy, and "other misbehavior by which the rights of [a] party have been prejudiced," under 9 U.S.C. § 10(a)(3).

5. **Third**, the arbitrator twice exceeded the powers the parties conferred on her, in violation of 9 U.S.C. § 10(a)(4). She awarded $2,700,000 in penalties that a defense prime contractor imposed on Veridian under a separate contract to which Greystone was a stranger — the paradigm of consequential damages — after expressly acknowledging "the tension between this characterization and the express language of Section 14.1," the provision by which the parties agreed that "the arbitrator **shall not** award consequential, punitive, or speculative damages." And she awarded $1,900,000 on a price-adjustment claim only by writing into MSA Section 7.4 a "sustained for at least two consecutive quarters" prerequisite that she twice acknowledged the contract does not contain. An arbitrator's authority is the parties' agreement. When she awards a remedy the agreement forbids, and enforces a condition the agreement omits, she is no longer interpreting the contract; she is dispensing her own brand of industrial justice.

6. These are not complaints about the arbitrator's assessment of the evidence or her reading of Illinois law. Greystone accepts, as it must, that "factual or legal error, no matter how gross," is not a ground for vacatur. *Affymax, Inc. v. Ortho-McNeil-Janssen Pharms., Inc.*, 660 F.3d 281, 285 (7th Cir. 2011). Each ground pleaded here rests on the text of § 10 and on facts drawn from the arbitrator's own Disclosure Statement, her own Procedural Order No. 3, her own April 2, 2024 evidentiary ruling, and her own Final Award.

7. The Award directs Greystone — a company with approximately $285 million in annual revenue — to pay $18,700,000, to absorb $412,500 in arbitration costs, and to forgo $21,100,000 in counterclaims, with post-award interest running at 5% per annum from March 14, 2025. Greystone respectfully asks the Court to vacate the Award in its entirety and to direct a rehearing before a new, properly disclosed, and genuinely neutral arbitrator.

## III. STANDING

11. Section 10(a) of the FAA authorizes vacatur "upon the application of **any party to the arbitration**." 9 U.S.C. § 10(a). Greystone is a signatory to the arbitration agreement contained in Section 14.1 of the parties' Master Supply Agreement dated January 15, 2020 (the "MSA"); it was named as Respondent in PAS Case No. 2023-ARB-04871; it appeared, participated fully through counsel, presented witnesses and evidence at an eight-day evidentiary hearing, and submitted post-hearing and reply briefing; and it is the party ordered by the Final Award to pay $18,700,000 in damages, to bear $412,500 in arbitration costs, and to reimburse Veridian $137,500 in deposit equalization. Greystone is therefore a "party to the arbitration" within the plain meaning of § 10(a) and possesses statutory standing.

12. Greystone likewise satisfies Article III standing. It has suffered a concrete, particularized, and actual injury — an $18.7 million monetary liability presently accruing post-award interest at 5% per annum, together with the extinguishment of $21.1 million in counterclaims. That injury is fairly traceable to the Final Award and would be redressed by the vacatur and rehearing sought here.

13. Greystone anticipates that Veridian may argue that Greystone forfeited its objection to the arbitrator's impartiality by not challenging her appointment during the arbitration, relying on the Final Award's observation at ¶ 32 that "neither party challenged the appointment of the arbitrator or raised any objection to the Disclosure Statement." That argument fails as a matter of law and fact. Waiver requires knowledge of the right waived. Greystone had none: the relationship was concealed, the Disclosure Statement affirmatively denied any relationship with Ridgeline Chambers LLP or Mr. Wheaton, and the arbitrator certified that she had conducted a "diligent and thorough inquiry" and knew of no other disclosable circumstance. A party cannot be charged with forfeiting an objection that the arbitrator's own certification told it did not exist. Nor was Greystone on constructive notice: the arbitrator's surname is shared by roughly one lawyer in a firm of Ridgeline Chambers' size, the son practices in a different city from the arbitrator's Chicago office, and nothing in the arbitral record identified him. Greystone's separate objection under § 10(a)(3) was expressly preserved on the record at the evidentiary hearing. *See* Final Award ¶ 44 ("Greystone formally objected to the Tribunal's ruling on the record and expressly preserved its challenge for any subsequent proceedings.").

## IV. JURISDICTION AND VENUE

### A. Subject-Matter Jurisdiction

14. The Federal Arbitration Act does not itself confer federal subject-matter jurisdiction; an independent jurisdictional basis must appear on the face of the application. *Badgerow v. Walters*, 596 U.S. 1, 8–9 (2022) (holding that a court may not "look through" a § 9, § 10, or § 11 application to the substance of the underlying dispute). Greystone therefore pleads diversity jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1332(a)(1) on the face of this Application.

15. **Complete diversity.** Greystone is a Delaware corporation with its principal place of business in Illinois and is therefore a citizen of Delaware and Illinois. 28 U.S.C. § 1332(c)(1). Veridian is a limited liability company; its citizenship is that of each of its members. *Thomas v. Guardsmark, LLC*, 487 F.3d 531, 534 (7th Cir. 2007); *Cosgrove v. Bartolotta*, 150 F.3d 729, 731 (7th Cir. 1998). On information and belief, based on Veridian's public filings, its Virginia organization, its Reston, Virginia headquarters, and the record of the arbitration, each member of Veridian is a citizen of the Commonwealth of Virginia, and no member of Veridian is a citizen of Delaware or Illinois. Complete diversity therefore exists. Greystone will supplement this allegation upon confirming the identity and citizenship of Veridian's members, and respectfully requests leave to take limited jurisdictional discovery should Veridian dispute this allegation.

16. **Amount in controversy.** The matter in controversy is the Final Award itself, which directs payment of $18,700,000 in damages, allocates $412,500 in arbitration costs to Greystone, directs a $137,500 deposit reimbursement, extinguishes $21,100,000 in counterclaims, and accrues post-award interest at 5% per annum. The amount in controversy therefore exceeds $75,000, exclusive of interest and costs, by more than two orders of magnitude.

### B. Venue

17. Venue is proper in this District under 9 U.S.C. § 10(a), which authorizes vacatur applications in "the United States court in and for the district wherein the award was made." The seat of arbitration was Chicago, Illinois, MSA § 14.1; the Final Award recites that it "has been rendered at the seat of arbitration in Chicago, Illinois, United States of America," Final Award ¶ 167; and Chicago lies within Cook County, in the Eastern Division of the Northern District of Illinois. *See* N.D. Ill. LR 5.1(a).

18. Venue is independently proper under 28 U.S.C. § 1391(b)(2), because a substantial part of the events giving rise to this Application — the arbitrator's appointment and non-disclosure, all eight days of the evidentiary hearing at the PAS offices in Chicago, the issuance of the challenged evidentiary rulings, and the rendering of the Award — occurred in this District. *See Cortez Byrd Chips, Inc. v. Bill Harbert Constr. Co.*, 529 U.S. 193, 195, 204 (2000) (FAA venue provisions are permissive and supplement the general venue statute).

19. Venue and personal jurisdiction are further established by the parties' own bargain. MSA Section 14.3 provides that "[f]or any matter that is not subject to arbitration under Section 14.1 of this Agreement, including but not limited to ... **proceedings to confirm, vacate, or modify an arbitration award**, the Parties irrevocably consent to the exclusive jurisdiction of the state and federal courts located in Cook County, Illinois, and each Party irrevocably waives any objection to venue in such courts." Veridian has thus consented in advance to this forum for precisely this proceeding and has waived any objection to it, including any objection based on *forum non conveniens*.

### C. Procedural Vehicle

Under 9 U.S.C. § 6, "[a]ny application to the court hereunder shall be made and heard in the manner provided by law for the making and hearing of motions, except as otherwise herein expressly provided." This Application is accordingly presented as a motion supported by the arbitral record and by declarations, and is not subject to the pleading-and-answer sequence applicable to ordinary civil complaints. ## V. TIMELINESS UNDER 9 U.S.C. § 12

21. Section 12 of the FAA provides that "[n]otice of a motion to vacate, modify, or correct an award must be served upon the adverse party or his attorney within three months after the award is filed or delivered." 9 U.S.C. § 12. The three-month period is strict and is not extended by the movant's later discovery of grounds for vacatur. *See, e.g., Florasynth, Inc. v. Pickholz*, 750 F.2d 171, 175 (2d Cir. 1984); *Cullen v. Paine Webber Grp., Inc.*, 863 F.2d 851, 854 (11th Cir. 1988); *Move, Inc. v. Citigroup Global Mkts., Inc.*, 840 F.3d 1152, 1157 (9th Cir. 2016) (declining to apply equitable tolling to § 12).

22. This Application is timely, and Greystone does not seek and does not require tolling of any kind.

23. **The triggering date.** The Final Award is dated March 14, 2025, and recites that it "was transmitted to the Parties and their counsel by Pendleton Arbitration Services on March 14, 2025." Final Award, § IX. March 14, 2025 is therefore both the date the Award was filed with PAS and the date it was delivered to the parties. The three-month period runs from that date.

24. **The outer limit.** Three months after March 14, 2025 is Saturday, June 14, 2025. Because § 12 does not specify its own method of computing time, the computation rules of Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 6(a) apply. Under Rule 6(a)(1)(C), when the last day of a period falls on a Saturday, Sunday, or legal holiday, "the period continues to run until the end of the next day that is not a Saturday, Sunday, or legal holiday." June 15, 2025 is a Sunday. The last day for service is therefore **Monday, June 16, 2025**.

25. **Actual service.** Greystone has served notice of this Application, together with the Application itself, upon Veridian and upon its counsel of record on **June 12, 2025** — two days before the three-month anniversary and four days before the Rule 6(a)-extended deadline. Service is effected in the manner set forth in the accompanying Certificate of Service and in accordance with Federal Rule of Civil Procedure 5 and MSA Section 15.1. Greystone does not rely on the Rule 6(a) extension; service was accomplished within three calendar months of the Award on any method of computation.

26. **No intervening event alters the calculation.** No party sought correction of the Award under Rule 33 of the PAS Commercial Arbitration Rules (2022 Edition) within the thirty-day window the arbitrator reserved, *see* Final Award ¶ 168, and no such request tolled or restarted the § 12 period. As of the filing of this Application, Veridian has not filed an application to confirm the Award under 9 U.S.C. § 9. Should Veridian do so, Greystone requests that any such application be consolidated with this proceeding and held in abeyance pending resolution of this Application.

27. Greystone acted with diligence throughout. It retained undersigned counsel on April 15, 2025 — approximately one month after the Award. Counsel's investigation discovered the undisclosed relationship on April 17, 2025, and completed and documented the investigation on April 28, 2025. This Application follows within the statutory period.

## VI. FACTUAL AND PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

### A. The Master Supply Agreement

28. On January 15, 2020, Greystone (as "Supplier") and Veridian (as "Buyer") executed the MSA, Agreement No. MSA-2020-0115, under which Greystone agreed to manufacture and supply precision-machined titanium turbine blade blanks and aluminum structural connectors for Veridian's V-900 advanced turbofan engine program, produced for a major defense prime contractor. The MSA had a five-year initial term running from January 15, 2020 through January 14, 2025, with two optional one-year renewals exercisable by Veridian. Veridian committed to minimum annual purchase volumes aggregating $164,000,000 over the initial term.

29. Four MSA provisions are directly implicated by this Application.

30. **Section 9.2 (Acceptable Defect Threshold).** The parties agreed that "a shipment shall not be deemed Non-Conforming solely on the basis of individual unit defects, provided that the aggregate defect rate for such shipment does not exceed three percent (3%) of the total units delivered in such shipment (the 'Acceptable Defect Threshold')." Where the rate is at or below 3%, "the shipment as a whole shall be deemed conforming for all purposes under this Agreement," and Veridian's "sole remedy" is replacement of the specific non-conforming units at Greystone's cost.

31. **Section 7.4 (Price Adjustment for Raw Material Cost Increases).** The parties agreed that "[i]f the cost of raw materials used in the manufacture of the Products, specifically titanium sponge and/or aerospace-grade aluminum billets, increases by more than eight percent (8%) year-over-year as measured by the Metal Market Index ('MMI') published by Ferrous Analytics Group, Supplier may request a price adjustment of up to five percent (5%) of the then-current price for the affected Products, effective as of the first day of the next calendar quarter following Supplier's written notice." Section 7.4 then specifies, in detail, the *only* conditions on the exercise of that right: written notice with supporting MMI data identifying the affected Products and the requested percentage; a fifteen-business-day window in which Buyer may request a meeting; a thirty-day negotiation period following any such request; automatic effectiveness on the first day of the next calendar quarter if no meeting is requested or if negotiations do not produce agreement; a 5% cap; and a limit of one adjustment per contract year per Product category. Section 7.4 contains no durational or "sustained" requirement of any kind.

32. **Section 14.1 (Arbitration; Limitation on Remedial Authority).** The parties agreed to binding arbitration before a sole arbitrator administered by PAS under its Commercial Arbitration Rules (2022 Edition), seated in Chicago, Illinois, governed by Illinois substantive law. Critically, the parties expressly circumscribed the arbitrator's remedial authority: "The arbitrator shall have the authority to award any remedy or relief that a court of competent jurisdiction could order or grant, including specific performance of any obligation created under this Agreement; ***provided, however, that the arbitrator shall not award consequential, punitive, or speculative damages, it being the express agreement of the Parties that such damages are excluded from any award***." The MSA states the purpose of that limitation in the same sentence: it "reflects the Parties' mutual intent that neither Party shall be exposed to open-ended consequential or punitive liability," and "is intended to be read in conjunction with the limitation of liability provisions set forth in Section 11.2." Section 11.2, in turn, bars in capitalized text "ANY INDIRECT, INCIDENTAL, CONSEQUENTIAL, SPECIAL, OR PUNITIVE DAMAGES ... INCLUDING BUT NOT LIMITED TO LOSS OF PROFITS, LOSS OF REVENUE, LOSS OF USE, OR COST OF PROCUREMENT OF SUBSTITUTE GOODS."

33. **Section 14.3 (Forum Selection).** Quoted at ¶ 19 above.

### B. The Underlying Dispute

34. The parties performed without material incident through 2021. Beginning in mid-2022, global titanium sponge prices escalated sharply following international sanctions that disrupted major producers' supply chains. The Metal Market Index published by Ferrous Analytics Group recorded a **31% year-over-year increase** in aerospace-grade titanium sponge prices as of Q3 2022 — nearly four times the 8% trigger in Section 7.4. The Final Award records that this increase "was well-documented in the record and was not disputed by either party." Final Award ¶ 17.

35. On September 12, 2022, Greystone's CEO Harold K. Voss served written notice invoking Section 7.4, accompanied by the Q3 2022 MMI data, and requested the maximum permitted 5% adjustment on titanium-based products effective January 1, 2023. Final Award ¶ 18. Veridian objected by letter dated October 15, 2022, asserting for the first time that Section 7.4 "required the increase in raw material costs to be 'sustained for two consecutive quarters.'" *Id.* ¶ 19. Greystone implemented the 5% adjustment on January 1, 2023. *Id.* ¶ 20.

36. On February 28, 2023, Greystone delivered 4,200 titanium turbine blade blanks under Purchase Order VAS-2023-0217. In March 2023, Veridian rejected the shipment, asserting — based on testing performed in its own in-house laboratory in Reston, Virginia — that approximately 12% of the units (504 of 4,200) exhibited grain-structure inconsistencies exceeding ASTM B348 Grade 5 tolerances. Greystone's contemporaneous pre-shipment quality assurance records reflected a defect rate of **1.8%** (76 of 4,200), comfortably within the 3% Acceptable Defect Threshold. *Id.* ¶¶ 21–23.

37. Veridian withheld $6,300,000 in invoices beginning March 2023, and on April 18, 2023 served a notice of material breach and termination effective May 18, 2023, citing (i) delivery of non-conforming goods under Section 9.2 and (ii) the Section 7.4 price increase. *Id.* ¶¶ 24–25.

38. Veridian filed its Request for Arbitration with PAS on June 5, 2023, asserting four claims totaling $22,200,000. Greystone answered on July 21, 2023 and counterclaimed for $14,800,000 in lost profits from wrongful termination and $6,300,000 in unpaid invoices, totaling $21,100,000. *Id.* ¶¶ 26–28.

Disclosure Statement § IV.

Section V acknowledged a continuing duty: "I understand and acknowledge that my duty to disclose is a continuing obligation under Rule 12(d) ... from the date of my appointment through the issuance of any final award, and that **a failure to make such supplemental disclosures may constitute grounds for challenge to my service as arbitrator or to the validity of any award rendered**."

Section VII, captioned "Certification and Signature," states: "I certify that the foregoing disclosures are true, complete, and accurate to the best of my knowledge and belief as of the date of this statement. **I have made a diligent and thorough inquiry into all facts and circumstances that might give rise to justifiable doubts as to my impartiality or independence.** I am not aware of any other facts or circumstances, beyond those set forth herein, that might create any such doubts or that could reasonably be considered relevant to my ability to serve as an impartial and independent arbitrator in this matter."

46. Notably, the arbitrator demonstrated in the same document that she understood the breadth of her obligation. In Section IV.E she volunteered, "[i]n the interest of full disclosure," a 2019 PAS arbitration involving Crestfield Dynamics Corp., an unrelated Wichita, Kansas aerospace components manufacturer, represented by different counsel, in a matter she described as "factually and legally unrelated" to this dispute — disclosing it "solely to ensure transparency regarding my experience with disputes in the aerospace components industry." She disclosed a stranger's case from four years earlier. She did not disclose her own son's employment at opposing counsel's firm.

### D. Procedural Order No. 3 and Veridian's 45-Day Violation

47. On December 4, 2023, Greystone moved to compel production of retained sample units from the disputed shipment, which were in Veridian's sole physical possession at its Reston, Virginia facility. Greystone's expert, Dr. Nikolai Ferren, Ph.D., P.E., of Blackthorn Materials Consulting LLC, required physical access to conduct independent metallurgical re-testing at Lakeshore Testing Laboratories in Gary, Indiana.

48. On December 15, 2023, the arbitrator granted the motion in Procedural Order No. 3. Her findings there are directly at odds with her later evidentiary ruling and merit quotation:

> "The retained sample units are plainly relevant and material to the central factual dispute in this arbitration ... The parties' competing defect rate claims — 12% according to Claimant versus 1.8% according to Respondent — present a sharp factual dispute that goes to the very heart of the largest component of Claimant's damages claim, valued at $8.2 million. Independent metallurgical testing of the retained samples by Respondent's expert could provide critical objective evidence to assist the Arbitrator in resolving this dispute at the evidentiary hearing."

> "**Denying Respondent physical access to the retained samples, which are in Claimant's sole possession, would effectively deprive Respondent of any meaningful ability to rebut Claimant's metallurgical evidence through independent testing. Such a result would be inconsistent with the Arbitrator's obligation to ensure that each party is afforded a full and fair opportunity to present its case.**"

49. Procedural Order No. 3 ¶ 2 ordered production "no later than January 15, 2024," and stated: "**Time is of the essence with respect to this deadline.**" Paragraph 5 explained that the January 15 date "has been specifically calibrated to afford Respondent sufficient time to receive the samples, conduct the necessary metallurgical testing, and incorporate the findings into its expert report by the February 15, 2024 deadline." Paragraph 6 warned that noncompliance "may result in the imposition of sanctions, including but not limited to adverse inferences, preclusion of evidence, cost-shifting, or such other remedies as the Arbitrator deems just and appropriate."

50. **Veridian did not comply.** The arbitrator so found: "Veridian did not comply with the January 15, 2024 production deadline established by Procedural Order No. 3. The retained samples from the disputed February 2023 shipment were not produced to Greystone until March 1, 2024 — forty-five (45) days after the deadline." Final Award ¶ 38.

51. The Award further records that "Greystone raised Veridian's noncompliance with the Tribunal on multiple occasions during this period, requesting that sanctions be imposed and that Veridian be compelled to produce the samples immediately," and that the arbitrator convened a February 12, 2024 teleconference at which Veridian attributed the delay to "logistical difficulties in retrieving the retained samples from a secure storage facility." *Id.* The arbitrator "determined that no sanctions were warranted." *Id.*

52. The consequence was arithmetically inevitable. The expert-report deadline was February 15, 2024. The samples arrived March 1, 2024 — **fourteen days after the deadline had already passed**. As Dr. Ferren stated: "it was a physical impossibility for me to conduct independent re-testing and include the results in my initial expert report." Supplemental Ferren Report § I.

### E. The Excluded Evidence

53. Dr. Ferren moved with dispatch. He received the 150 units on March 1, 2024; they reached Lakeshore Testing Laboratories — an ISO/IEC 17025-accredited independent facility — on March 4; testing ran March 5 through March 18; and he finalized his report on **March 22, 2024, twenty-one days after receipt**.

54. The results are the reason this matters. Dr. Ferren subjected each of the 150 units to four categories of testing:

| Test Category | Units Tested | Non-Conforming | Conformance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Visual / Dimensional | 150 | 1 | 99.3% |
| Chemical Composition (OES, ASTM B348 Gr. 5) | 150 | 0 | 100.0% |
| Mechanical Properties (ASTM E8/E8M, E18) | 150 | 0 | 100.0% |
| Microstructure / Grain (ASTM E112) | 150 | 3 (incl. borderline) | 98.0% |
| **Overall (any defect)** | **150** | **3** | **97.9% (2.1% defect rate)** |

55. Dr. Ferren classified two borderline units as non-conforming expressly "to ensure that my defect rate assessment is not understated," yielding a conservative **2.1% defect rate** — below the MSA's 3% threshold, consistent with Greystone's 1.8% internal figure, and lower by a factor of nearly six than Veridian's claimed 12%. He calculated that "the probability of observing only 3 non-conforming units in a sample of 150 drawn from a population with a true 12% defect rate is vanishingly small (less than 0.001%)."

56. ## VII. THE STANDARD OF REVIEW

73. Greystone acknowledges at the outset that judicial review of an arbitral award is among the narrowest known to law, and it frames each ground accordingly.

74. **Section 10 supplies the exclusive grounds.** In *Hall Street Associates, L.L.C. v. Mattel, Inc.*, 552 U.S. 576, 584 (2008), the Supreme Court held that §§ 10 and 11 provide "the exclusive grounds for expedited vacatur and modification" of an award under the FAA. Greystone invokes only grounds enumerated in § 10.

75. **Ordinary error is not a ground.** "[T]he sole question for us is whether the arbitrator (even arguably) interpreted the parties' contract, not whether he got its meaning right or wrong." *Oxford Health Plans LLC v. Sutter*, 569 U.S. 564, 569 (2013). "[T]he question for decision by a federal court asked to set aside an arbitration award ... is not whether the arbitrator or arbitrators erred in interpreting the contract; it is not whether they clearly erred ...; it is whether they interpreted the contract at all." *Chicago Typographical Union No. 16 v. Chicago Sun-Times, Inc.*, 935 F.2d 1501, 1505 (7th Cir. 1991) (Posner, J.). "[F]actual or legal error, no matter how gross, is insufficient to support overturning an arbitration award." *Affymax*, 660 F.3d at 285. Greystone accepts each of these propositions and does not ask this Court to review the arbitrator's weighing of the metallurgical evidence, her apportionment of causation for the V-900 delays, or her application of Illinois damages law.

76. **Greystone does not rely on "manifest disregard."** In this Circuit, "manifest disregard of the law" survives, if at all, only as shorthand for the statutory ground in § 10(a)(4) — reserved for an award that directs a party to violate the law or that "orders a party to do something the law forbids." *George Watts & Son, Inc. v. Tiffany & Co.*, 248 F.3d 577, 580–81 (7th Cir. 2001) (Easterbrook, J.); *see also Wise v. Wachovia Sec., LLC*, 450 F.3d 265, 268–69 (7th Cir. 2006). Greystone pleads no freestanding manifest-disregard claim. Each ground below is anchored in the text of § 10.

77. **But deference is not abdication.** The narrowness of review makes the § 10 grounds indispensable, not ornamental. Congress enumerated four categories of defect that no amount of deference tolerates, and two of them — evident partiality and refusal to hear pertinent and material evidence — protect the integrity of the arbitral process itself rather than the correctness of its result. Deference to an award presupposes an arbitrator who disclosed what the parties were entitled to know, a hearing at which both sides could present their evidence, and a decision-maker who stayed within the authority the parties conferred. Where those premises fail, the justification for deference fails with them. As the Supreme Court put it in *Commonwealth Coatings Corp. v. Continental Casualty Co.*, 393 U.S. 145, 149 (1968), "we should, if anything, be even more scrupulous to safeguard the impartiality of arbitrators than judges, since the former have completely free rein to decide the law as well as the facts and are not subject to appellate review."

78. **Burden.** Greystone bears the burden of establishing a statutory ground for vacatur, and it accepts that burden as to each ground pleaded.

## VIII. GROUND ONE — EVIDENT PARTIALITY UNDER 9 U.S.C. § 10(a)(2)

### A. The Legal Standard

79. Section 10(a)(2) authorizes vacatur "where there was evident partiality or corruption in the arbitrators, or either of them."

80. In *Commonwealth Coatings*, the Supreme Court vacated an award where a neutral arbitrator failed to disclose a sporadic business relationship with a party. Justice Black, writing for the plurality, held that arbitrators "not only must be unbiased but also must avoid even the appearance of bias," and that they "should disclose to the parties any dealings that might create an impression of possible bias." 393 U.S. at 149–50. Justice White, concurring in the judgment and supplying the controlling narrower rationale, agreed that an arbitrator "cannot be expected to provide the parties with his complete and unexpurgated business biography," but insisted that "where the arbitrator has a substantial interest in a firm which has done more than trivial business with a party, that fact must be disclosed." *Id.* at 151–52 (White, J., concurring).

81. The Seventh Circuit applies the formulation that "evident partiality" is shown where "a reasonable person would have to conclude that an arbitrator was partial to one party to the arbitration." *Health Servs. Mgmt. Corp. v. Hughes*, 975 F.2d 1253, 1264 (7th Cir. 1992) (adopting *Morelite Constr. Corp. v. N.Y.C. Dist. Council Carpenters Benefit Funds*, 748 F.2d 79, 84 (2d Cir. 1984)). In *Merit Insurance Co. v. Leatherby Insurance Co.*, 714 F.2d 673 (7th Cir. 1983), Judge Posner declined to vacate where the undisclosed relationship — a supervisory employment relationship that had ended fourteen years before the arbitration — was remote in time and trivial in substance, while emphasizing that the court "d[id] not condone the arbitrator's failure to disclose." *Id.* at 680–83; *see also Sphere Drake Ins. Ltd. v. All Am. Life Ins. Co.*, 307 F.3d 617, 620–21 (7th Cir. 2002) (Easterbrook, J.).

82. Two features of the doctrine matter here.

83. **First, nondisclosure cases are analytically distinct from actual-bias cases.** Where a party proves actual bias, it must show the arbitrator's partiality directly. Where an arbitrator fails to disclose a material relationship, the inquiry is whether the undisclosed facts create a reasonable impression of partiality — because nondisclosure deprives the complaining party of the very information it needed to challenge the arbitrator in the first place. *See Schmitz v. Zilveti*, 20 F.3d 1043, 1046–47 (9th Cir. 1994); *Applied Indus. Materials Corp. v. Ovalar Makine Ticaret Ve Sanayi, A.S.*, 492 F.3d 132, 137 (2d Cir. 2007). Greystone therefore need not — and does not — allege that Arbitrator Rourke consciously favored Veridian.

84. **Second, the closest analogue in the case law is a father-son relationship.** *Morelite* itself involved an arbitrator whose father was the president of the parent body of the union that was a party. The Second Circuit vacated, reasoning that "[w]e are unwilling to hold that a father-son relationship is a mere triviality" and that "people are more likely to be affected by the interests of their parents and children than by the interests of others." 748 F.2d at 84. That reasoning applies with full force to an arbitrator's son employed by the prevailing party's counsel — a firm whose revenues, reputation, and internal standing his career depends upon.

### B. The Undisclosed Relationship Requires Vacatur

85. Applying that standard, a reasonable person apprised of the following facts would have to conclude that the arbitrator was partial:

86. **(a) The relationship is a first-degree familial one.** Garrett Rourke is the arbitrator's son. Okada Decl. Ex. C. This is not a distant acquaintance, a former colleague, or a fourteen-years-stale employment tie of the sort *Merit* found trivial. It is the closest relationship the law recognizes outside marriage.

87. **(b) The relationship is current, not historical.** Garrett Rourke joined Ridgeline Chambers in September 2022 and remained a senior associate there throughout the arbitration and as of April 2025. Okada Decl. Ex. A. Every distinguishing feature that saved the award in *Merit* — remoteness in time, termination of the relationship, triviality of the connection — is absent.

88. **(c) The relationship runs to the prevailing party's counsel, and to its lead counsel's own practice group.** Garrett Rourke and Marcus D. Wheaton work from the same office at 900 K Street NW, Suite 800, Washington, D.C., and belong to the same Aerospace & Defense industry group — of which Wheaton is co-chair, and thus in a position of seniority over the arbitrator's son. Okada Decl. Ex. B. That group's declared specialty is the precise subject matter of this arbitration.

89. **(d) The relationship carries an economic dimension.** A senior associate's compensation, advancement to partnership, and professional standing depend on the firm's success and on the assessment of its senior partners, including the co-chair of his industry group. An arbitrator resolving an $18.7 million dispute in which that co-chair is lead counsel is deciding a matter that bears, however indirectly, on her child's livelihood. Whether or not it in fact influenced her, a reasonable person cannot regard that as immaterial.

90. **(e) The nondisclosure was not silence but affirmative misstatement.** This is the feature that most sharply distinguishes this case from *Merit*, *Sphere Drake*, and the line of authority holding that mere nondisclosure of a remote connection is insufficient. Arbitrator Rourke did not simply omit the relationship. She affirmatively declared that she had "no prior personal, professional, or financial relationship with Marcus D. Wheaton **or Ridgeline Chambers LLP**"; that she had "not had any professional or social contact with Mr. Wheaton **or any other attorney at Ridgeline Chambers LLP**"; that she was "not affiliated with any law firm ... that could give rise to conflicts"; that she was "not aware of any other facts or circumstances that might give rise to justifiable doubts"; and — under certification — that she had "made a diligent and thorough inquiry into all facts and circumstances that might give rise to justifiable doubts as to [her] impartiality or independence." A neutral who certifies a diligent and thorough inquiry, and reports that it turned up nothing, cannot have overlooked her own son's employer.

91. **(f) Her own disclosures establish that she understood the standard.** In the same document, she volunteered a 2019 arbitration involving an unrelated Kansas company, represented by different counsel, in a matter she herself characterized as "factually and legally unrelated," disclosing it "solely to ensure transparency." A neutral sensitive enough to disclose that is a neutral who knew that a son at opposing counsel's firm was disclosable.

92. **(g) The applicable rules and standards mandated disclosure.** Rule 12(a) of the PAS Commercial Arbitration Rules (2022 Edition) required disclosure of "any circumstance likely to give rise to justifiable doubts as to the arbitrator's impartiality or independence," expressly including relationships with counsel for any party; Rule 12(d) made the duty continuing. The arbitrator expressly invoked the IBA Guidelines on Conflicts of Interest in International Arbitration as the governing standard for her disclosures. Disclosure Statement § IV. Under the IBA Guidelines, a close family relationship between an arbitrator and counsel representing a party falls within the Red List of relationships requiring mandatory disclosure — a category that, at minimum, is waivable only upon full disclosure and the informed consent of all parties. There was no disclosure here, and therefore no consent, informed or otherwise.

93. **(h) She acknowledged the consequence in advance.** Section V of her own Disclosure Statement states that "a failure to make such supplemental disclosures may constitute grounds for challenge to my service as arbitrator or **to the validity of any award rendered**." She was right.

### C. The Absence of a Formal Appearance Does Not Cure the Defect

94. Greystone anticipates that Veridian will emphasize that Garrett Rourke never appeared as counsel of record. That fact does not save the Award, for three reasons.

95. **First, the duty to disclose attaches to the relationship, not to the family member's participation.** The purpose of disclosure is to let the parties evaluate risk and decide whether to object, seek recusal, or proceed with informed consent. A rule limiting disclosure to family members formally staffed on the matter would let the neutral decide unilaterally which family ties are material — which is exactly the judgment the disclosure requirement takes away from the arbitrator and gives to the parties.

96. **Second, the argument proves too much.** Whether Garrett Rourke participated informally in the Veridian matter is a fact peculiarly within the knowledge of Ridgeline Chambers and unavailable from public sources. Veridian cannot both withhold that information and rely on its absence.

97. **Third, non-participation was never established, only unverified.** Greystone's review of the arbitral record shows no formal appearance. It does not and cannot show the absence of strategy discussions, research support, or informal consultation within a single-office practice group co-chaired by lead counsel.

98. Should the Court conclude that resolution of this ground requires further factual development, Greystone respectfully requests leave to take limited discovery directed to (i) the nature and extent of any involvement by Garrett Rourke in the Veridian matter; (ii) any communications between Arbitrator Rourke and Garrett Rourke or Ridgeline Chambers personnel concerning the arbitration; and (iii) the conflict-check materials Arbitrator Rourke reviewed in connection with the "diligent and thorough inquiry" she certified.

### D. Prejudice and Remedy

99. Evident partiality under § 10(a)(2) requires no separate showing that the outcome would have differed; the defect goes to the constitution of the tribunal, and it taints every determination the tribunal made. But if any confirmation of consequence were needed, the Award supplies it: the arbitrator awarded Veridian on all four claims and denied Greystone's counterclaims in their entirety, including by excluding Greystone's only independent evidence on the largest disputed issue and by allocating 75% of costs against it.

100. Because the defect is in the identity of the decision-maker, the Award must be vacated in full, and any rehearing must be before a differently constituted tribunal.

## IX. GROUND TWO — MISCONDUCT IN REFUSING TO HEAR PERTINENT AND MATERIAL EVIDENCE UNDER 9 U.S.C. § 10(a)(3)

### A. The Legal Standard

101. Section 10(a)(3) authorizes vacatur "where the arbitrators were guilty of misconduct in refusing to postpone the hearing, upon sufficient cause shown, or in refusing to hear evidence pertinent and material to the controversy; or of any other misbehavior by which the rights of any party have been prejudiced."

102. The Seventh Circuit construes § 10(a)(3) to guarantee a "fundamentally fair hearing." *Generica Ltd. v. Pharmaceutical Basics, Inc.*, 125 F.3d 1123, 1130 (7th Cir. 1997). Fundamental fairness requires that "the arbitrator must give each of the parties to the dispute an adequate opportunity to present its evidence and argument." *Id.* (internal quotation marks omitted); *accord Slaney v. Int'l Amateur Athletic Fed'n*, 244 F.3d 580, 592 (7th Cir. 2001).

103. Arbitrators possess broad latitude over evidentiary and scheduling matters, and Greystone does not suggest otherwise. But that latitude is not unlimited. Vacatur is warranted where an arbitrator excludes evidence that is pertinent and material to a dispositive issue without a reasonable basis for doing so, thereby depriving a party of a fair opportunity to be heard. *See Tempo Shain Corp. v. Bertek, Inc.*, 96 F.3d 564, 571 (2d Cir. 1996) (vacating where the panel excluded material testimony with "no reasonable basis"); *Hoteles Condado Beach v. Union de Tronquistas Local 901*, 763 F.2d 34, 39–40 (1st Cir. 1985).

104. The party seeking vacatur must show that the excluded evidence was pertinent and material and that its exclusion prejudiced its rights. Greystone establishes both.

### B. The Excluded Evidence Was Pertinent and Material — as the Arbitrator Herself Found

105. Materiality is not a matter of argument here; it is a matter of the arbitrator's own findings, made twice.

106. In Procedural Order No. 3, she held that the retained samples were "plainly relevant and material to the central factual dispute in this arbitration," that independent testing "could provide critical objective evidence," and that denying access "would effectively deprive Respondent of any meaningful ability to rebut Claimant's metallurgical evidence through independent testing" — a result "inconsistent with the Arbitrator's obligation to ensure that each party is afforded a full and fair opportunity to present its case."

107. In the Final Award, she reiterated that "[t]he central factual dispute in this claim is the defect rate," Final Award ¶ 69, and that the excluded report "may contain information relevant to the defect rate dispute," Ruling § V.F.

108. The report was dispositive in the most literal sense: at 2.1%, the shipment was conforming under MSA Section 9.2 "for all purposes under this Agreement," Veridian's $8.2 million replacement-cost claim would have failed at its root, and — because Claims 2 and 4 were derivative of the non-conformity finding and Counterclaim 1 turned on whether Greystone was in material breach — the outcome of the entire arbitration would have been placed in doubt.

### C. The Exclusion Had No Reasonable Basis Because the Untimeliness Was Veridian's Doing

109. The sole ground for exclusion was untimeliness relative to the February 15, 2024 expert-report deadline. But the arbitrator had already found, in the same proceeding, that the samples necessary to produce the report were in Veridian's sole possession, that she had ordered their production by January 15, 2024 with time "of the essence," and that **Veridian produced them on March 1, 2024 — 45 days late and 14 days after the expert-report deadline had already expired.** Final Award ¶ 38.

110. The chronology permits no other conclusion. Greystone could not have obtained independent testing before February 15, 2024 by any means available to it. That is not advocacy; it is arithmetic. As Dr. Ferren stated, "it was a physical impossibility."

111. The Ruling's actual rationale — "Respondent had ample opportunity throughout the discovery period to arrange for independent testing," Ruling § V.C — is contradicted by the arbitrator's own Procedural Order No. 3, which existed precisely because Greystone had *no* such opportunity. An evidentiary ruling premised on a factual predicate the arbitrator herself had already rejected is not an exercise of discretion; it is the absence of one.

112. Nor was Greystone dilatory once the impediment was removed. Samples received March 1; laboratory delivery March 4; testing March 5–18; report March 22; motion to admit March 28. Twenty-one days from receipt to finished report on a 150-unit, four-protocol metallurgical examination at an accredited laboratory.

### D. The Arbitrator Compounded the Error by Declining to Sanction the Violating Party

113. Procedural Order No. 3 ¶ 6 expressly reserved sanctions for noncompliance, "including but not limited to adverse inferences, **preclusion of evidence**, cost-shifting, or such other remedies as the Arbitrator deems just and appropriate."

114. Greystone "raised Veridian's noncompliance with the Tribunal on multiple occasions ..., requesting that sanctions be imposed." Final Award ¶ 38. The arbitrator convened a teleconference on February 12, 2024, accepted Veridian's explanation of "logistical difficulties in retrieving the retained samples from a secure storage facility," and "determined that no sanctions were warranted." *Id.*

115. The net effect of the two rulings, read together, is that the only preclusion sanction imposed in this arbitration was imposed on the party that complied with the discovery order, for a delay caused by the party that violated it. Veridian breached an order in which time was "of the essence," suffered nothing, and then obtained the exclusion of the evidence its own breach delayed. That is "misbehavior by which the rights of [a] party have been prejudiced" within the meaning of § 10(a)(3), independent of the refusal to hear the evidence itself.

### E. The Arbitrator Refused to Consider Any Remedy Short of Total Exclusion

116. Section 10(a)(3) also reaches misconduct "in refusing to postpone the hearing, upon sufficient cause shown." The Ruling declined a continuance, but on grounds untethered to the cause of the delay: that the proceeding had been "ongoing for approximately ten months" and that a continuance "would undermine the efficiency and finality that the arbitration process is intended to provide." Ruling § V.E. It did not weigh that 45 of those days were consumed by Veridian's noncompliance; it did not consider a short adjournment limited to Veridian's rebuttal; it did not consider admitting the report subject to reduced weight; it did not consider permitting Veridian its own re-testing of the very samples Procedural Order No. 3 ¶ 4 required to be preserved for exactly that purpose.

117. The Final Award's own procedural recitation states the point without qualification: "The ruling did not discuss or consider any alternatives to exclusion, such as a brief continuance of the hearing to permit Veridian to prepare a rebuttal to the Ferren report, or the admission of the report with a reduced evidentiary weight, or any other remedy that might have accommodated both parties' interests." Final Award ¶ 43.

118. The asserted prejudice to Veridian was also, on the record, curable and partly self-inflicted. Veridian possessed the samples; Procedural Order No. 3 ¶ 4 required their preservation for Veridian's re-examination; Veridian had a retained metallurgical expert already engaged; and Veridian had from March 28 (the motion) to April 25 (the last hearing day) — with a ten-day recess between April 11 and April 22 built into the schedule — to respond. Exclusion was the most drastic remedy available and the only one the arbitrator considered.

### F. Prejudice Is Established by the Award's Own Reasoning

119. The prejudice here is not speculative. It is demonstrated by the way the arbitrator resolved the defect-rate dispute *after* excluding the independent evidence.

120. She discounted Greystone's quality assurance records **because they were not independent**: they "were generated by Greystone's own personnel and are necessarily subject to the concern that they were produced by a party with an inherent interest in the outcome," and "the inherent limitations of a manufacturer's self-certification of its own product quality must be recognized when weighed against the findings of an independent expert engaged by the purchaser." Final Award ¶ 75.

121. She discounted Greystone's evidence **because of its sample size**: Greystone's pre-shipment inspection "tested approximately 2% of each production lot — approximately 84 units out of 4,200 — whereas Dr. Lichtman's analysis was based on a larger and more comprehensive sample." *Id.* ¶ 76.

122. She discounted Greystone's evidence **because of its methodology**: Greystone's grain-boundary analysis was "less rigorous," "relying primarily on visual inspection and hardness measurements rather than the more detailed metallographic examination and beta-transus analysis performed by Veridian's expert." *Id.*

123. Each of these three deficiencies was cured by the excluded report. Dr. Ferren's testing was performed at an ISO/IEC 17025-accredited **independent** laboratory, on **150 units** (nearly twice Greystone's pre-shipment sample and 3.6% of the batch), using **full metallographic cross-sectioning, etching, and optical microscopy at 50x–500x with ASTM E112 grain-size measurement and grain-boundary alpha morphology assessment** — the very methodology whose absence the arbitrator held against Greystone. The exclusion created the evidentiary vacuum, and the Award then filled that vacuum against the party the exclusion disadvantaged.

124. The arbitrator's consolation — that Greystone "may present its existing expert evidence ... and may cross-examine Claimant's metallurgical expert," Ruling § V.F — is no answer. Cross-examination cannot supply an independent 150-unit laboratory analysis, and the "existing expert evidence" was the evidence she then rejected as insufficiently independent, insufficiently sampled, and insufficiently rigorous.

125. Greystone was not afforded "an adequate opportunity to present its evidence." *Generica*, 125 F.3d at 1130. The hearing was not fundamentally fair. Vacatur is warranted under § 10(a)(3).

## X. GROUND THREE — THE ARBITRATOR EXCEEDED HER POWERS BY AWARDING CONSEQUENTIAL DAMAGES THE MSA FORBADE (9 U.S.C. § 10(a)(4))

### A. The Legal Standard

126. Section 10(a)(4) authorizes vacatur "where the arbitrators exceeded their powers, or so imperfectly executed them that a mutual, final, and definite award upon the subject matter submitted was not made."

127. An arbitrator's authority is wholly derivative of the parties' agreement. "[A]rbitration is a matter of contract," *First Options of Chicago, Inc. v. Kaplan*, 514 U.S. 938, 943 (1995), and "parties are generally free to structure their arbitration agreements as they see fit," *Volt Info. Scis., Inc. v. Bd. of Trustees*, 489 U.S. 468, 479 (1989). That freedom includes the freedom to limit the remedies an arbitrator may award.

128. An arbitrator therefore "exceed[s] [her] powers" when she "stray[s] from interpretation and application of the agreement and effectively dispense[s] [her] own brand of industrial justice." *Stolt-Nielsen S.A. v. AnimalFeeds Int'l Corp.*, 559 U.S. 662, 671 (2010) (quoting *Major League Baseball Players Ass'n v. Garvey*, 532 U.S. 504, 509 (2001)). The award must "draw its essence from the contract"; the arbitrator "does not sit to dispense his own brand of industrial justice." *United Steelworkers v. Enterprise Wheel & Car Corp.*, 363 U.S. 593, 597 (1960).

129. The dividing line is between construing a contractual term and disregarding one. An arbitrator who interprets an ambiguous limitation and reaches a debatable result is insulated by *Oxford Health*. An arbitrator who identifies an unambiguous prohibition, acknowledges that it applies, and grants the prohibited relief anyway has not interpreted the contract at all — she has overridden it. *See Chicago Typographical Union*, 935 F.2d at 1505 ("if the arbitrator must have based his award on some body of thought, or feeling, or policy, or law that is outside the contract ... then his award can be said not to draw its essence from the contract" (internal quotation marks omitted)); *Prostyakov v. Masco Corp.*, 513 F.3d 716, 723 (7th Cir. 2008).

### B. The Parties Expressly Withheld the Power the Arbitrator Exercised

130. MSA Section 14.1 is not a rule of decision the arbitrator was asked to apply to the merits; it is a limitation on her office, placed in the arbitration clause itself, immediately following the grant of remedial authority, and framed as an exception to it:

> "The arbitrator shall have the authority to award any remedy or relief that a court of competent jurisdiction could order or grant ...; ***provided, however, that the arbitrator shall not award consequential, punitive, or speculative damages, it being the express agreement of the Parties that such damages are excluded from any award***."

131. The provision is mandatory ("shall not"), categorical ("excluded from any award"), and self-explaining ("it being the express agreement of the Parties"). The MSA then states the risk allocation the clause effects: it "reflects the Parties' mutual intent that neither Party shall be exposed to open-ended consequential or punitive liability in connection with disputes arising under this Agreement," and is "to be read in conjunction with the limitation of liability provisions set forth in Section 11.2" — which bars, in capitalized text, "ANY INDIRECT, INCIDENTAL, CONSEQUENTIAL, SPECIAL, OR PUNITIVE DAMAGES."

132. These are sophisticated commercial parties, both represented by counsel in an arm's-length negotiation, Final Award ¶ 7, who allocated the risk of downstream supply-chain losses by contract. That allocation was part of the price of the bargain.

### C. The $2,700,000 Award Is Consequential Damages

133. Claim 4 sought "$4,600,000 in damages representing penalties imposed on Veridian by the defense prime contractor as a result of V-900 engine program delays." Final Award ¶ 112. The arbitrator awarded $2,700,000 of that sum, relabeled "direct flow-through costs." *Id.* ¶¶ 123–124, 160.

134. Under the Illinois law the parties chose, damages of this character are consequential, not direct. The arbitrator said so herself in stating the governing framework: "Direct damages are those that flow naturally and necessarily from the breach — i.e., the damages that every plaintiff in a similar situation would incur ..., regardless of the plaintiff's particular circumstances. Consequential damages, by contrast, are those that arise from the special circumstances of the non-breaching party and that would not necessarily be incurred by every plaintiff in a similar situation." *Id.* ¶ 118 (citing *Hadley v. Baxendale*, 9 Ex. 341 (1854)).

135. Measured against the arbitrator's own definition, the defense prime penalties are consequential:

- They arose exclusively from **Veridian's separate contract with a third party**, to which Greystone was not a party and had no access.
- Their existence, structure, trigger milestones, and dollar amounts were fixed by **that third-party contract's penalty provisions**, not by the MSA.
- Greystone had **no privity** with the defense prime, "no control over the terms of Veridian's contract with the defense prime (including the penalty provisions), and no knowledge of the specific dollar amounts of the penalties to which Veridian was exposed." *Id.* ¶ 116.
- They are precisely the damages that "would not necessarily be incurred by every plaintiff in a similar situation" — a buyer without a downstream penalty clause would incur none.

136. The label the arbitrator applied cannot change the substance. If it could, MSA Section 14.1 would be a nullity, defeasible by nomenclature.

### D. The Arbitrator Acknowledged the Prohibition and Overrode It

137. What removes this ground from the domain of arguable contract interpretation is that the arbitrator did not find Section 14.1 ambiguous, did not find it inapplicable, and did not find that the parties intended it to reach something narrower. She recognized that it applied and declined to give it effect on grounds external to the contract.

138. She opened the analysis by "acknowledg[ing] the language of MSA Section 14.1, which provides that 'the arbitrator shall not award consequential, punitive, or speculative damages.'" *Id.* ¶ 112. She had already flagged it earlier: "The Tribunal specifically notes the limitation language providing that 'the arbitrator shall not award consequential, punitive, or speculative damages,' which is discussed further in connection with Veridian's Claim 4 below." *Id.* ¶ 12.

139. Then, in the operative passage:

> "Second, the Tribunal **acknowledges the tension between this characterization and the express language of Section 14.1**. A rigid application of the consequential damages exclusion to bar all downstream costs, however, would effectively render a Tier 1 supplier remediless for the most predictable and direct impacts of a sub-supplier's breach. **The Tribunal does not interpret Section 14.1 to produce such an unreasonable result.**" *Id.* ¶ 121.

140. That is not construction of the parties' agreement. It is a policy judgment that the parties' agreement produces an outcome the arbitrator considers "unreasonable," and a decision to depart from the "express language" on that basis. But the parties were entitled to strike an allocation the arbitrator would not have chosen. A commercial party that accepts a consequential-damages bar is not left "remediless"; it is left with direct damages — of which the arbitrator awarded Veridian $8,200,000 in replacement costs and $5,900,000 in lost profits. What the bar denied Veridian was recovery of a third party's penalties. That is what the bar was for.

141. Nor did the arbitrator lack the tools to honor the clause. Had she concluded Section 14.1 was ambiguous, or that the penalties were direct under a defensible reading, *Oxford Health* would shield the result. She did neither. She identified an "express" prohibition and set it aside because enforcing it would be "unreasonable." The distinction between deciding what a contract means and deciding that it should mean something else is the distinction § 10(a)(4) polices.

142. The arbitrator exceeded her powers. The $2,700,000 award of consequential damages must be vacated.

## XI. GROUND FOUR — THE ARBITRATOR EXCEEDED HER POWERS BY ADDING A CONTRACTUAL PREREQUISITE SHE FOUND DID NOT EXIST (9 U.S.C. § 10(a)(4))

### A. Section 7.4 Contains No Durational Requirement — and the Arbitrator Said So Three Times

143. Claim 3 turned on a single question of contract text: whether Section 7.4 conditioned Greystone's price-adjustment right on the raw-material cost increase persisting for two consecutive quarters. The executed MSA states the trigger and its conditions with precision:

> "If the cost of raw materials used in the manufacture of the Products, specifically titanium sponge and/or aerospace-grade aluminum billets, **increases by more than eight percent (8%) year-over-year as measured by the Metal Market Index ('MMI') published by Ferrous Analytics Group**, Supplier may request a price adjustment of up to five percent (5%) of the then-current price for the affected Products, effective as of the first day of the next calendar quarter following Supplier's written notice."

144. Section 7.4 then specifies, in three further paragraphs, everything a valid invocation requires: written notice with supporting MMI data; identification of the affected Products, applicable MMI data points, and requested percentage; a fifteen-business-day window for Buyer to request a meeting; a thirty-day negotiation period; automatic effectiveness if no agreement is reached or no meeting is requested; a 5% cap; and one adjustment per contract year per Product category. The parties specified this mechanism in exhaustive detail. They did not specify a durational requirement.

145. The arbitrator agreed — repeatedly and in her own words.

146. In her recitation of the contract: "The Tribunal notes that the text of Section 7.4 ... establishes a trigger based on a year-over-year increase of more than 8% in the MMI as published by Ferrous Analytics Group. **The provision contains no express requirement that such increase be sustained for any specified duration prior to the supplier's exercise of the adjustment right.**" Final Award ¶ 10.

147. In her analysis: "The Tribunal **acknowledges that the text of Section 7.4 does not expressly include the phrase 'sustained for two consecutive quarters' or any other express durational requirement**. The text speaks in terms of an increase of 'more than eight percent (8%) ... on a year-over-year basis' without specifying that such increase must persist for any particular period before the adjustment right is triggered." *Id.* ¶ 105.

148. There was no dispute about the trigger's satisfaction. The Q3 2022 MMI data showed a **31% year-over-year increase** — nearly four times the 8% threshold — and the arbitrator found this "was well-documented in the record and was not disputed by either party." *Id.* ¶ 17. Greystone gave written notice on September 12, 2022 with the supporting MMI data. *Id.* ¶ 18. Every condition the contract imposed was met.

### B. Having Found the Contract Silent, the Arbitrator Supplied the Term

149. The arbitrator nonetheless held for Veridian, by creating the missing condition:

> "**The Tribunal finds an implied requirement that the cost increase be sustained for at least two consecutive quarters as reflected in the MMI data before Greystone is entitled to invoke the price adjustment mechanism under Section 7.4.**" *Id.* ¶ 107.

150. The stated basis was not the contract's language but the arbitrator's assessment of what sophisticated parties would want: "a reasonable interpretation ..., consistent with the commercial purpose of the MSA," "the reasonable expectations of sophisticated commercial parties," and "the MSA's overarching objective of pricing predictability over the contract term." *Id.* ¶¶ 106–107.

151. She then applied the invented condition to defeat the adjustment: as of September 12, 2022, "the titanium sponge price increase ... had been sustained for only one quarter," and so Greystone's invocation "was premature." *Id.* ¶ 108. That holding produced the $1,900,000 award on Claim 3. *Id.* ¶¶ 109–111, 159.

152. The two-quarter condition did not originate with the arbitrator. It originated with Veridian, in James R. Prescott's letter of October 15, 2022 — the position of an interested party in a live commercial dispute, adopted verbatim as the contract's meaning three years later. *Id.* ¶ 19.

### C. Supplying a Term the Contract Omits Is Not Interpretation

153. Under Illinois law — the law the parties chose, MSA §§ 14.1, 15.8 — "a court cannot alter, change or modify existing terms of a contract, or add new terms or conditions to which the parties do not appear to have assented." *Thompson v. Gordon*, 241 Ill. 2d 428, 449 (2011). Where the parties have specified the conditions on a contractual right in detail, the omission of a further condition is treated as deliberate, not as an oversight for the tribunal to correct.

154. Greystone does not ask this Court to hold that the arbitrator misapplied Illinois contract law. That would be an error of law, and error is not a ground for vacatur. *Affymax*, 660 F.3d at 285. The § 10(a)(4) question is narrower and different: did the arbitrator interpret the contract, or did she rewrite it? *Oxford Health*, 569 U.S. at 569; *Chicago Typographical Union*, 935 F.2d at 1505.

155. On the arbitrator's own account, she rewrote it. She did not find Section 7.4 ambiguous. She did not identify two competing readings of its language and choose one. She did not point to a course of dealing, a defined term, or another provision of the MSA supplying a durational element. She found that the text "contains no express requirement," Final Award ¶ 10, that it does not "specify[] that such increase must persist for any particular period," *id.* ¶ 105 — and then imported a specific, quantified condition ("at least two consecutive quarters as reflected in the MMI data") from her own view of what the commercial purpose of the agreement required. That is "some body of thought, or feeling, or policy ... outside the contract." *Chicago Typographical Union*, 935 F.2d at 1505. It is the arbitrator's own brand of industrial justice. *Stolt-Nielsen*, 559 U.S. at 671.

156. The imported condition was also decisive in a way that reached beyond Claim 3. Because the arbitrator held the price increase improper, she held it a **second material breach** by Greystone, Final Award ¶ 132, which she then used to (i) justify Veridian's termination and deny Counterclaim 1, *id.* ¶¶ 133–139; (ii) invoke the prior-material-breach doctrine to bar Greystone from enforcing Veridian's own unmet Year 3 minimum purchase commitment, *id.* ¶ 137; and (iii) support the denial of Counterclaim 2, on the reasoning that "a substantial portion of the $6,300,000 in outstanding invoices includes the 5% price increase that the Tribunal has found to be improperly imposed," *id.* ¶ 144. A single supplied term thus carried $1,900,000 in damages and $21,100,000 in denied counterclaims.

157. The arbitrator exceeded her powers. The Claim 3 award and every determination resting on it must be vacated.

## XII. THE AWARD IS NOT SEVERABLE AND MUST BE VACATED IN FULL

158. Greystone seeks vacatur of the entire Final Award, not merely of its component parts, for four reasons.

159. **First, Ground One is not severable in principle.** Evident partiality is a defect in the constitution of the tribunal. It does not attach to particular rulings; it attaches to the decision-maker who made all of them. There is no principled way to sever a tainted arbitrator's award into portions the taint reached and portions it did not.

160. **Second, the excluded evidence bore on the foundation of the whole Award.** The defect-rate finding drove Claim 1 ($8.2M), Claim 2 ($5.9M, derivative of the breach finding), Claim 4 ($2.7M, derivative of the same delays), and Counterclaim 1 (denied because Greystone was found in material breach for delivering non-conforming goods). Had the 2.1% independent finding been admitted and credited, the shipment would have been "deemed conforming for all purposes under this Agreement," MSA § 9.2, and the architecture of the Award would collapse.

161. **Third, the § 10(a)(4) defects cascade.** As set out at ¶ 156 above, the supplied Section 7.4 condition supported a second material-breach finding that in turn supported the denial of both counterclaims and the prior-material-breach ruling on Veridian's unmet minimum purchase commitment.

162. **Fourth, the cost allocation is expressly derivative.** The arbitrator allocated 75% of the $550,000 in arbitration costs to Greystone "[i]n light of th[e] outcome" — Veridian having "prevailed on all four of its affirmative claims" and Greystone's counterclaims "hav[ing] both been denied in their entirety." Final Award ¶ 151. If the outcome falls, so does the allocation that was justified by it.

163. In the alternative, and only if the Court declines full vacatur, Greystone requests partial vacatur of the $2,700,000 award of consequential damages under § 10(a)(4) (Ground Three), the $1,900,000 award on Claim 3 under § 10(a)(4) (Ground Four), and the cost allocation, together with remand of the balance.

Section 10(b) provides that "[i]f an award is vacated and the time within which the agreement required the award to be made has not expired, the court may, in its discretion, direct a rehearing by the arbitrators."
